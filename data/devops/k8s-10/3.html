Updates, scaling and deleting

Good to know (SDE-2)
  • RollingUpdate (default) - on a template change, pods are replaced one at a time from the highest ordinal down: 2, then 1, then 0, each waiting for the previous one to be Ready.
  • partition - updateStrategy.rollingUpdate.partition: 2 updates only pods with ordinal 2 or higher. That is a built-in canary: update one broker, watch it, then lower the partition to continue.
  • OnDelete - the controller updates nothing by itself; a pod takes the new template only when you (or an operator) delete it. Operators often use this style to control order themselves.
  • podManagementPolicy: Parallel - create and delete pods all at once (identity and storage stay stable). Faster, for systems that do not need ordered start.
  • Deleting the StatefulSet does not delete its PVCs. That is deliberate: your data is safe from a mistaken delete. The persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy field (whenDeleted / whenScaled: Retain or Delete) lets you choose automatic cleanup instead.

Careful: with OrderedReady, if kafka-0 is broken and never becomes Ready, the controller will not create or update kafka-1 and kafka-2. A rolling update can also get stuck on a bad template - the controller will not "fix" it by itself; you correct the template, then delete the stuck pod so it is recreated. Know this before you are paged at 3 a.m.

The node-failure rule: at most one

This is the most important - and most surprising - StatefulSet behaviour. When a node becomes unreachable, a Deployment's pods are replaced elsewhere after the eviction timeout (about 5 minutes by default). A StatefulSet pod is not. It stays in Terminating or Unknown, and kafka-1 is not recreated anywhere.

Why? Because the control plane cannot tell "the node is dead" from "the network to the node is cut". If the node is actually still alive and kafka-1 is still writing to its disk, starting a second kafka-1 elsewhere could mean two processes with the same identity - a split brain, possibly writing the same volume. StatefulSets promise at most one pod per identity, so they wait until someone confirms the old pod is really gone:

  • The node comes back, and the kubelet finishes deleting the pod; or
  • The Node object is deleted (for example by the cloud controller when the VM is gone); or
  • An admin marks the node with the node.kubernetes.io/out-of-service taint (non-graceful node shutdown), after making sure it is really powered off; or
  • An admin force-deletes the pod - only safe if you are sure the old one is not running.

From your world

This is fencing, the same problem as STONITH in an HA cluster or a VRRP pair where both routers think they are master. You never promote the standby until the old primary is fenced, because two actives on one shared resource is worse than a short outage. Kubernetes has no power switch, so it waits for proof. Where this breaks: for Kafka, the outage is small anyway - partitions led by the missing broker fail over to in-sync replicas on other brokers within seconds (that is Kafka's own replication, Replication & ISR), so only that broker's replicas are missing until it returns.

The story: a phone call vs a letterbox

Explain like I'm 5

You want to tell Grandma that you lost your first tooth. You can phone her. That is lovely, but it only works if Grandma is home, awake and not busy. If she is in the garden, you wait and wait, holding the phone, and you cannot do anything else.

Or you can write a letter and drop it in the letterbox. Then you go and play. The postman carries it. Grandma reads it when she gets back from the garden. Nobody had to wait for anybody. And if you want to tell Grandpa, Auntie and your best friend too, you just post more copies of the same news.

Computers have the same two choices. A phone call is called synchronous ("at the same time"): the caller waits for the answer. A letter is called asynchronous ("not at the same time"): the sender drops a message and moves on, and the reader picks it up later. Kafka is a giant, very fast, very safe letterbox for computers.

A second story: the restaurant order rail

Walk into a busy restaurant kitchen. The waiter does not stand next to the chef saying "one pasta... are you done yet?... are you done yet?". The waiter writes the order on a ticket and clips it to a rail above the kitchen. Then the waiter goes back to the tables. The chefs take tickets from the rail in order, at the speed they can cook. At 8 p.m., when twenty tables order at once, the rail just gets longer. Nobody shouts, nothing is lost, and the chefs keep cooking at full speed.

Three things make the rail useful, and they are the three reasons companies use messaging:

  1. The waiter does not wait for the chef. Taking orders and cooking happen at different times. (This is decoupling in time.)
  2. The rail soaks up the dinner rush. Orders arrive in bursts; cooking happens at a steady speed. The rail holds the difference. (This is buffering.)
  3. Many people can use the same ticket. The grill chef, the salad chef and the dessert chef all look at the same table's ticket. The manager can count tickets at the end of the night. (This is fan-out.)

Keep the order rail in your head. Every idea on this page is one of those three, told with computers.

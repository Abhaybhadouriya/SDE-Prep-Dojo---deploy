Interview questions

The 60-second answer: "Why would you use a message queue like Kafka instead of direct REST calls?"
"Direct calls couple services in time: the caller waits, latencies add up, and if any downstream service is down, the whole request fails. With a broker like Kafka, the producer writes one event and returns, and each downstream service consumes it at its own pace. That gives me three things: decoupling - services can be down, scaled or added without the producer changing; buffering - a traffic spike piles up as consumer lag instead of overloading the database; and fan-out - many teams read the same event, and with Kafka they can even replay history. The costs are eventual consistency, at-least-once delivery so consumers must be idempotent, ordering only per partition, and running the cluster. So I keep the steps the user waits on synchronous, like taking payment, and make everything after that asynchronous."

Why use a message queue at all instead of direct REST calls between services?

Because direct calls make services depend on each other being up, fast and known. A broker removes those dependencies: the producer only needs the broker to be up; consumers can be slow, down or added later. It absorbs spikes (the backlog becomes lag, not errors) and gives fan-out to many consumers. Kafka adds durable storage and replay.

How to answer

Name the three benefits (decouple, buffer, fan-out) with one concrete example each from a checkout. Then give the costs, then when you would not do it. Ending with "when not" shows judgement.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which steps of checkout would you keep synchronous? (Usually payment authorisation and stock reservation.)
  • How does the user find out if payment fails later? (A status page or push notification driven by a PaymentFailed event.)
  • What happens if Kafka itself is down? (Producers buffer briefly and then fail; you need a replicated cluster across availability zones.)
Is Kafka a queue? If a consumer reads a message, is it deleted?

No. Kafka is a distributed, append-only log. Reading does not delete anything; each consumer group just advances its own offset. Data is removed only by retention (time or size, 7 days by default) or by compaction (keep the latest value per key). That is what allows many independent consumer groups and replay. Classic queues like RabbitMQ or SQS delete a message once it is acknowledged.

How to answer

Say "log, not queue" in the first sentence, then the consequence (replay, many groups), then the nuance: inside one consumer group Kafka behaves like a work queue, and Kafka 4.2+ share groups add true queue-style per-message acknowledgement.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you replay from yesterday? (Reset the group's offsets by timestamp with kafka-consumer-groups.sh --reset-offsets --to-datetime.)
  • What happens if a consumer is slower than retention? (It loses data that is deleted before it reads it.)
During a flash sale, orders arrive 10x faster than the inventory service can process them. How does a message broker help, and what are the limits?

The broker absorbs the burst: orders are written to the orders topic at full speed, and the inventory consumers process them at their steady rate. The excess becomes consumer lag. As long as lag drains after the peak and stays well within retention, nothing is lost and the database is not overloaded. The limits: users wait longer for confirmation (lag in seconds), and if the incoming rate stays above capacity, lag grows without end. Then you must add consumers (up to the number of partitions), speed up processing (batch database writes), or shed or prioritise load.

How to answer

Draw the tank: fill rate, drain rate, level = lag. State the condition for safety (average drain rate above average fill rate, peak backlog smaller than retention). Then name two ways to add drain capacity and one way to protect the user experience.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you know you are in trouble? (Alert on lag in seconds and on lag growth rate.)
  • Can you just add 100 consumers? (Only up to the number of partitions in one group - page 04.)
  • How do you avoid overselling while orders queue up? (Reserve stock atomically at the database, see Flash Sale.)
What do you lose when you move from synchronous calls to asynchronous messaging?

Immediate consistency (other parts of the system see the change a bit later), simple error handling (the producer no longer learns that a consumer failed), exactly-once by default (Kafka is at-least-once, so duplicates are possible), simple ordering (only per partition, so you need the right key), and simple debugging (you need correlation IDs and tracing). You also add a dual-write risk between your database and the broker, and a cluster to operate.

How to answer

List four or five costs quickly, then pick the two most important for the given design and give the fix for each: idempotent consumers for duplicates, the outbox pattern for dual writes.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does idempotent mean, with an example?
  • Explain the dual-write problem.
When would you NOT use Kafka?

When the caller needs an immediate answer (a request/response API is simpler and faster), when the volume is small enough that a managed queue like SQS or even a database table is far cheaper to run, when you need per-message delays, priorities or complex routing (RabbitMQ or SQS fit better, or Kafka share groups if Kafka already exists), when you need one strict global order across all events, and when nobody can operate a cluster and there is no budget for a managed service.

How to answer

Give three crisp cases and name the alternative for each. This question is about judgement; a candidate who says "Kafka for everything" loses points.

Likely follow-ups
  • Kafka vs RabbitMQ in one sentence? (See page 12.)
  • How would you build a delayed message (send a reminder in 24 hours) if you already have Kafka?

Scenario: the email provider is down and checkout is failing

Your checkout service calls payment, then inventory, then the email provider, all synchronously. The email provider has an outage. Checkout success rate drops to near zero, and customers are being charged without getting an order confirmation on screen. The interviewer asks what is going on and how you would redesign it.

Weak answer: "Add retries to the email call and increase the timeout." This makes it worse: users wait even longer, threads pile up waiting on a dead service, and the checkout still fails.

Strong answer: "This is a cascading failure from a synchronous dependency that is not needed to complete checkout. Short term: put a circuit breaker on the email call so we fail fast and skip email, and return success once payment and inventory succeed. Long term: keep payment and stock reservation synchronous, then publish an OrderPlaced event (via an outbox so it cannot be lost) and let email, warehouse and analytics consume it. The email consumer de-duplicates by order ID, retries with back-off through a retry topic, and sends poison messages to a dead-letter topic. An email outage then shows up as growing lag on one consumer group, which we alert on, instead of as lost revenue."

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you make sure no confirmation email is sent twice?
  • How long can the email service be down before you lose events? (Until lag exceeds retention.)

Buffering spikes: the shock absorber

Traffic is never flat. A flash sale starts at 12:00 sharp. A cricket match ends and everyone orders food at once. A push notification makes a million phones open the app within a minute. The systems behind them - a database, a payment provider, an email gateway - have a fixed speed. They cannot become ten times faster for ten minutes.

A broker in the middle acts like a water tank with a tap on top and a pipe at the bottom. The tap (producers) can gush. The pipe (consumers) drains at its steady speed. The water level in the tank is the backlog. In Kafka, that backlog has a name you will hear constantly: consumer lag - how many messages are written but not yet processed by a consumer.

A real example: Slack's engineering team wrote about an outage where the Redis server holding their job queue ran out of memory during a surge. Their fix was to put Kafka in front of Redis as a durable buffer: web servers write jobs to Kafka, and a separate service moves them into Redis only as fast as Redis can safely take them, committing its Kafka position only after a job is safe. The burst now waits on disk in Kafka instead of overflowing memory (Scaling Slack's Job Queue).

From your world

This is an egress queue on a router port. A 10G link feeding a 1G link needs a buffer; the buffer occupancy is your queue depth. Consumer lag is queue depth. If depth keeps growing, the drain rate is below the fill rate, and no buffer is big enough - you must add drain capacity (more consumers) or slow the sender. When the sender is slowed, that is backpressure, just like a TCP receive window closing or PFC pause frames. Where this breaks: a router buffer is microseconds to milliseconds deep and tail-drops. A Kafka topic can hold hours or days of backlog on disk without dropping, so "absorb now, catch up later" is a normal design, not an emergency.

What lag number is "bad"? There is no universal number. A lag of 50,000 messages is fine if consumers process 100,000 per second (half a second behind). Watch two things instead: lag in time ("how many seconds behind is this consumer?") and whether lag keeps growing. Growing lag means you are under-provisioned.

The fix: drop a message and move on

Here are the real names for the pieces of the letterbox:

  • A message (Kafka calls it a record or an event) is a small piece of data that says something happened, like {"event":"OrderPlaced","orderId":"o-981","total":499}.
  • A producer is a program that sends messages. Here, the order service.
  • A consumer is a program that reads messages. Here, payment, inventory, email, warehouse and analytics.
  • A broker is the server in the middle that stores the messages until consumers read them. Kafka is a broker (really a cluster of brokers). So are RabbitMQ and Amazon SQS.
  • A topic (Kafka) or queue (RabbitMQ, SQS) is a named mailbox inside the broker, like orders.

Click Next or Play to watch the same checkout done both ways, including what happens when the email service crashes.

  1. Sync call chain. The order service calls payment, inventory and email in turn, and the user waits for all of them.
  2. A downstream service fails. Email is down. The order service waits until its timeout fires (often tens of seconds).
  3. The failure cascades. The user sees an error for an order that half happened. Worse, they retry - and you risk a double charge.
  4. Async: one write. The order service saves the order and writes one OrderPlaced event to the orders topic. That is the only thing it waits for.
  5. Instant answer. The user hears "Order placed!" right away. The slow work happens in the background.
  6. Independent readers. Payment, inventory and warehouse each read the same event at their own speed. Email is down, and that is fine: the event waits.
  7. Recovery. When email restarts, it reads from its saved position (its offset) and sends the confirmation. A short outage became a short delay, not a lost sale.

From your world

The sync chain is a blocking system call: the thread sleeps in the kernel until the disk or the peer answers. The async version is how a NIC driver works: the producer (NIC) writes descriptors into a DMA ring and moves on; the consumer (driver, via NAPI polling) drains the ring when it gets CPU time. Neither side waits for the other as long as the ring has room. Kafka is that ring, stretched across machines and stored on disk. Where this breaks: a NIC ring drops packets when full. Kafka does not drop; it keeps data for days, and if a producer's local buffer fills up, the producer blocks instead (more on that in Producers).

Async is not free. In the async design, "Order placed!" is said before the payment is taken. If the card is later declined, you need another event (PaymentFailed) and a way to tell the user. Many real checkouts take the payment synchronously and make everything after it async. Deciding which steps must be sync is the real design skill.

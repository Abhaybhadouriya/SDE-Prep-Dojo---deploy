Coupling: the hidden cost of direct calls

Coupling means "how much one part has to know about, or depend on, another part". Two services that are tightly coupled must change together, deploy together and be healthy together. Messaging loosens three kinds of coupling:

Kind of couplingWith direct callsWith a message broker
Time - must both be running now?Yes. If the receiver is down, the call fails.No. The message waits in the broker until the receiver is back.
Location - must the sender know where the receiver is?Yes. It needs the address (and load balancer) of each receiver.No. The sender knows only the topic name. Receivers can move, scale or be added.
Number - must the sender know how many receivers there are?Yes. Adding a receiver means changing the sender's code.No. A new team starts reading the topic. The producer does not change.
Speed - must they work at the same rate?Yes. The sender can only go as fast as the slowest receiver.No. The broker buffers the difference.

The "number" row is the one that changes how a company works. When the fraud team wants to see every order, they do not file a ticket with the order team. They start a new consumer on the orders topic, and the order service never knows. This is why messaging is the backbone of event-driven architecture: services announce facts ("an order was placed") instead of giving orders to each other ("send an email").

A great phrase for interviews: "commands vs events". A command says "do this" and expects one specific receiver ("charge this card"). An event says "this happened" and does not care who listens ("order o-981 was placed"). Events decouple; commands couple. Most systems use both.

The problem: a checkout that calls everyone

First, a few words. A service is a long-running program with a network API, for example an order service that accepts orders. When one service asks another to do something over the network and waits for the reply, that is a synchronous call, usually an HTTP request (often called a REST call) or a gRPC call. You already know this as a blocking RPC.

Now picture the "Buy now" button on a shopping site. When you click it, the order service has to make sure that the payment is taken, the stock is reduced, a confirmation email is sent, the warehouse is told to pack the box, and the analytics team counts the sale. The simplest code calls each of those services one after another and waits for each answer:

# the "phone call" version: every step waits for the one before it
def place_order(order):
    save_to_database(order)          # ~20 ms
    payment.charge(order)            # waits for the payment service
    inventory.reserve(order.items)   # waits for the inventory service
    email.send_confirmation(order)   # waits for the email service
    warehouse.create_pick_job(order) # waits for the warehouse service
    analytics.record_sale(order)     # waits for the analytics service
    return "Order placed!"

This works on day one. Then three problems appear, and they get worse as the company grows.

  1. Waits add up (latency). Latency is how long something takes. In a chain, the user's wait is the sum of every service's time. Add a sixth service next year, and every user waits longer.
  2. One failure breaks everything (availability). If the email service is down for five minutes, nobody can buy anything for five minutes - even though the email is not needed to take the money. If each service is up 99.9% of the time, a chain of five needs all five up at once, so the whole chain is up less often than any one of them. This is called a cascading failure: one sick part makes the whole body sick.
  3. Everyone knows everyone (coupling). The order service has to know the address of five other services, their API formats and their retry rules. When the analytics team wants a new field, the order team has to change and redeploy. Teams cannot move independently.

Rule of thumb, not a measurement: the probability that a chain of independent services is all up is roughly the product of their availabilities. Five services at 99.9% each gives about 0.9995 = 99.5%. Small numbers, but 0.5% of a year is about 44 hours.

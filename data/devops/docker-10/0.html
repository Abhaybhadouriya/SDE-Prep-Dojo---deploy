How Docker shows up in a real interview loop

Explain like I'm 5

Think of a school sports day. There is no "Docker race". But in the relay race, the long jump and the obstacle course, the teacher keeps checking: can you tie your shoes properly? Docker is the shoelaces. It rarely gets its own event, but if your laces come undone in the middle of the relay, everyone notices.

Here is the honest picture from public interview reports. At Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Flipkart and Walmart, SDE-1 and SDE-2 loops are mostly data structures and algorithms, low-level design (LLD), high-level design (HLD) and behavioural rounds. Docker usually appears as a follow-up: in a project deep-dive ("you said you containerized it; how big was the image, how did you handle config?"), in an HLD round ("how is this deployed?"), or in an LLD round. One Walmart SDE-2 candidate reported follow-ups on Docker Compose and advice to know recent Docker vulnerabilities. Platform, infra, DevOps and SRE teams go much deeper, sometimes with a round that is mostly Docker and Kubernetes.

About the company chips. A company chip means one of two things: a public candidate report names that company for this kind of question (Walmart, Microland, Tigera, Apple, Intuit: these are "reported in interviews" chips), or the question is about that company's own published engineering story. Those chips carry a "Blog:" prefix (Blog: Uber, Blog: Netflix, Blog: Shopify, Blog: Swiggy, Blog: AWS, Blog: Yelp) so the company filter never mixes "they asked it" with "they wrote about it". Typical means it is a staple of public Docker question banks and commonly asked, but we found no company-specific report. Never tell an interviewer "this is always asked at X".

What about Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Flipkart? We looked for public candidate reports that name Docker questions at these four and found none specific enough to tag a question honestly. Public reports for their SDE-1/2 loops describe DSA, LLD and HLD rounds, and Docker comes up as a follow-up inside a project deep-dive or a design round ("how would you deploy this?", "why is your image 1 GB?"). So for those loops, prioritise the 60-second answers and the Level 2-3 "Typical" questions, and use the Level 4-5 blocks to sound senior in follow-ups.

From your world: your background is an advantage here

Most candidates know Docker commands. Few can explain what the kernel does. You can. When asked "what is a container?", say "a process with clone flags", name the namespaces and the cgroup files, and you instantly sound senior. Translate your work too: "I debugged a line card daemon that ignored SIGTERM during ISSU" becomes "I understand why PID 1 in a container must handle signals". "I wrote the iptables NAT rules for a management VRF" becomes "I can explain -p 8080:80 at the packet level". The risk for you is the opposite one: going too deep too early. Lead with the one-sentence answer, then offer depth.

The story: the lunchbox goes to a big school

Explain like I'm 5

At home, you can leave your lunchbox open on the table. Nobody will touch it. But at a big school with a thousand children, things change. You put your name on it. You lock it. You never put your house key inside it. The kitchen checks every lunchbox for anything dangerous before it goes into the dining hall. And the kitchen keeps a list of exactly what was packed in each one, so if one ingredient turns out to be bad, they know which lunchboxes to throw away.

Production is the big school. Your container is the lunchbox. Locking it is hardening. Not putting the house key inside is keeping secrets out of images. The kitchen check is a vulnerability scan. The ingredient list is an SBOM (software bill of materials). And the name tag that nobody can fake is an image signature.

On the Internals page you saw that a container is a process fenced by namespaces and cgroups, sharing the host kernel. That fact drives everything on this page. If the fence is thin, you add more fences (drop privileges, filter syscalls), you make the process inside harmless even if it escapes (non-root, rootless, user namespaces), and you make sure the thing you run is exactly the thing you built and checked (digests, scans, signatures).

We will climb in four steps: a safe image, a safe runtime, a safe host and daemon, and a safe pipeline that produces all of it automatically.

From your world

This is the same thinking as protecting a router's control plane: an ACL on the box (pipeline and host rules), Control Plane Policing to rate-limit what reaches the CPU (cgroups and seccomp), separate privilege levels for operators (capabilities and non-root), and signed software images that the boot loader verifies before it loads them (image signing and verification). Where it breaks: a router image is one vendor's binary; a container image pulls in hundreds of open-source packages, so the supply chain is a much bigger part of the risk.

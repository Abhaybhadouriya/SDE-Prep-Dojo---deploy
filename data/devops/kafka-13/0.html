How Kafka shows up in a real interview loop

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a spelling test where the teacher does not just ask "spell cat". She asks "why is there a c and not a k?" and then "what if the word was cats?" Kafka questions work the same way. The first question is easy. The marks are in the second and third question. So for every answer on this page we also practise the "why" and the "what if".

Here is the honest picture from public interview reports gathered in our research. Kafka almost never gets its own round at Microsoft, Amazon, Google or Flipkart. It shows up in three places:

  1. The HLD round. You draw a queue between two services and the interviewer asks "which one, why, and what if the consumer is slow?" Order pipelines, notifications, clickstream and payments are the usual hosts.
  2. The project deep-dive. If Kafka is on your resume, expect "how many partitions did you have, how did you pick the key, what happened on a rebalance?" Reports from Blinkit, Delivery Hero and Oracle backend loops describe exactly this probing.
  3. The "tech" round at Kafka-heavy companies. Walmart Global Tech reports are the clearest: "how does Kafka partitioning work and how would you scale it", "make sure events go to the same partition", "persist and handle failed messages", even "design Kafka".

About the company chips. A chip such as Walmart or Microsoft means a public candidate report (LeetCode Discuss, Glassdoor) names that company for this kind of question, or the question is about that company's own published engineering story (Uber's DLQ design, Walmart's messaging proxy, Slack's job queue). Typical means it is a staple of public Kafka question banks with no company-specific report found. Read every chip as "commonly reported at", never "always asked at".

From your world: your background is an advantage

Most candidates memorise acks=all. Few can explain why Kafka is fast. You already know sendfile(2), the page cache, interrupt coalescing, ECMP flow hashing and redundancy groups. Use them, briefly: "a partition key works like ECMP flow hashing, same key, same path, so order holds; adding a partition re-hashes flows". Translate your stories too: "I debugged packet drops from buffer exhaustion on a line card" becomes "I debugged a back-pressure and queue-depth problem", which is exactly consumer lag. "I built HA failover between two route processors" becomes "I understand leader election, in-sync standbys and split-brain", which is exactly ISR and unclean election.

Where this breaks: the risk for you is going too deep too early. Lead with the one-sentence answer about the user or the guarantee, then offer the internals.

If they say: "You have no web or Kafka experience"

This comes up in hiring-manager and bar-raiser rounds. It is not a trap; they want to see whether you can map what you know onto what they need. Do not apologise and do not over-claim. Use a three-part reply: agree with the fact, name the overlap, prove it with one story.

Reply script (about 40 seconds): "That's fair, I haven't run Kafka in production at a web company. What I have done for years is build systems with the same hard parts: queues that fill up, ordering that must hold under load, failover that must not lose data, and upgrades with no downtime. For example, [one translation story below]. I've also built a 3-broker Kafka cluster with a producer, a consumer group and failure drills, so I've seen these behaviours myself, not just read about them. I expect to learn the ecosystem fast because the fundamentals are familiar."

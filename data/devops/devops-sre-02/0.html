The story: the electricity bill at home

Explain like I'm 5

Your family gets one electricity bill each month. It is very big, and nobody knows why. So you put a little meter on every room. Now you can see: the fridge is fine, but your brother leaves his big heater on all day, even when he is at school. A light in the garage has been on for a year, and nobody goes there. Dad agrees with the power company: "we will always use at least this much, so give us a cheaper price for that part." For the washing machine, you run it at night, when power is cheap, because it does not matter exactly when the clothes get washed. The bill goes down, and nobody is any colder.

That is all of FinOps. The meters on every room are tags and cost allocation. The heater left on is an over-provisioned server you can right-size. The garage light is an idle or orphaned resource. Dad's deal is a commitment discount (Savings Plans or Reserved Instances). Washing at night is Spot capacity: cheap, but it can be taken away, so you only use it for work that can wait or restart. FinOps (cloud financial operations) is the habit of doing this every month as an engineering team, not once a year as a finance exercise.

From your world

You already think this way about bandwidth. A Metro Ethernet contract has a committed information rate (CIR) that you pay for whether you use it or not, plus a cheaper best-effort burst above it that the carrier may drop. Cloud compute works the same way: the Savings Plan is your CIR, On-Demand is the expensive flexible rate, and Spot is the best-effort class that gets preempted first under congestion. And the bill itself is basically NetFlow accounting: every byte and every second is metered against a key (account, service, tag), and your job is to choose good keys.

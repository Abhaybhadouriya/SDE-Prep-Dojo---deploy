read_committed and the last stable offset (LSO)

good to know

Transactional records are written to the log immediately; the COMMIT or ABORT marker arrives later. A consumer chooses how to treat them with isolation.level:

  • read_uncommitted (the default!): read everything up to the high watermark, including records of open and aborted transactions. With this setting your consumer sees aborted data - transactions give you nothing on the read side.
  • read_committed: read only up to the LSO (last stable offset) = the offset of the first record that belongs to a still-open transaction. Records of aborted transactions are filtered out (the broker sends an "aborted transactions" list with each fetch so the client can skip them).

Three practical consequences:

  1. Offsets have gaps. Markers take up offsets, and aborted records are skipped. Never assume "next offset = last + 1", and never compute "number of messages" by subtracting offsets on a transactional topic.
  2. Latency grows with transaction length. A read_committed consumer sees data only after the commit. If your producer commits every 5 seconds, downstream end-to-end latency is at least that.
  3. A stuck transaction stalls readers. An open transaction is aborted by the coordinator after the producer's transaction.timeout.ms (default 60 s; the broker caps it with transaction.max.timeout.ms, 15 min). Until then, readers wait at the LSO, and consumer lag climbs even though the producer looks healthy.

From your world

The LSO is like a reorder buffer's commit point in a CPU, or a filesystem journal that only replays complete transactions: later entries may already be written, but nothing past the oldest unfinished entry is "architecturally visible". In-order visibility is the price of atomicity.

Quick quiz

The control plane is completely down for 10 minutes. What happens to running pods?

  1. They are all stopped
  2. They keep running; nothing new can be scheduled or changed
  3. They move to another cluster
The kubelet keeps local containers alive and kube-proxy's rules stay in the kernel. Only decisions (scheduling, scaling, healing across nodes) stop.

A topic has 8 partitions. KEDA scales the consumer Deployment to 12 pods. How many pods consume?

  1. 12
  2. 4
  3. 8
In a consumer group each partition is assigned to at most one consumer, so 8 work and 4 are idle.

Which component writes the binding that assigns a pod to a node?

  1. kube-scheduler
  2. kubelet
  3. kube-proxy
The scheduler filters and scores nodes, then writes a Binding through the API server. The kubelet on that node then starts the pod.

With default settings, how long before pods on a dead node are evicted?

  1. About 10 seconds
  2. About 5-6 minutes (grace period plus 300 s toleration)
  3. Never
About 50 s to mark the node NotReady, then a 300 s default toleration before eviction.

Why do users see errors at the end of a rolling update even with readiness probes?

  1. The scheduler is slow
  2. etcd is full
  3. SIGTERM and endpoint removal happen in parallel, so a pod may get new requests after it starts shutting down
A preStop delay plus graceful SIGTERM handling closes this race.

Why does etcd usually run with 3 or 5 members and not 4?

  1. Raft needs a majority; 4 tolerates only 1 failure, the same as 3, at higher cost
  2. etcd cannot count to 4
  3. Kubernetes only supports odd numbers of nodes
Quorum is floor(n/2)+1. For n=4 that is 3, so only one member may fail, same as n=3.

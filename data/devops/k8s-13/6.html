Design and judgement questions (SDE-2 HLD rounds)

Design the deployment architecture for a food-delivery backend on Kubernetes.

"Start with requirements: dinner-time peaks several times the daily average, high availability, many teams. I would run a regional managed cluster across three availability zones. Stateless services (API, cart, order, rider-matching) as Deployments with HPA, spread across zones with topology spread constraints and protected by PodDisruptionBudgets. One Gateway API entry point with TLS, then services talk over ClusterIP. Stateful pieces: a managed relational database outside the cluster, Redis for caching, and Kafka for order events, either managed or via the Strimzi operator. Consumers scale with KEDA on lag. Observability with Prometheus, central logs and traces. Deploys through GitOps with canaries. Cost: stateless pools on spot or preemptible nodes, Karpenter for node scaling, and scheduled pre-scaling before meal peaks."

How to answer

Requirements first, then traffic path, then state, then scaling, then operations and cost. Keep Kubernetes as the tool, the product as the focus. Practise with the HLD designs.

Likely follow-ups
  • A whole zone fails. What happens?
  • Why is the database outside the cluster?
Would you run a database or Kafka on Kubernetes?

"It depends on team skills and constraints. For: one platform and toolchain for everything, mature operators like Strimzi and CloudNativePG that automate upgrades and failover, and portability. Flipkart, for example, has written about running stateful workloads on Kubernetes. Against: stateful systems need careful storage (local NVMe vs network disks), stable network identities, anti-affinity across zones, PDBs and drain discipline; mistakes lose data, not just requests. Managed services move that burden to the cloud provider. My default for a team without strong platform skills is managed services for the data layer and Kubernetes for stateless services; with a strong platform team, operators are a sound choice."

How to answer

Never a flat yes or no. Give both sides, then a default and the condition that changes it.

Likely follow-ups
  • What are the hard parts of Kafka on Kubernetes? (Advertised listeners, storage, rack awareness, rolling restarts without under-replication.)
Your service must survive a 10x spike at the start of a big sale (Big Billion Days, Prime Day). Plan it.

"Autoscaling alone is too slow for a spike that arrives in seconds: new nodes take minutes and images must be pulled. So: load test beforehand to know capacity per pod. Pre-scale on a schedule before the sale starts, with KEDA's cron scaler or a raised HPA minimum. Keep headroom with low-priority placeholder pods that real pods can preempt instantly. Pre-pull images on nodes. Scale on leading signals like request rate or queue depth instead of CPU. Protect the system with rate limiting and load shedding at the gateway, and queue non-critical work through Kafka. Raise quotas with the cloud provider in advance. Run chaos drills; Flipkart's public CNCF case study describes running chaos experiments for their sale."

How to answer

Start by explaining why reactive autoscaling is not enough. Then before, during and protection measures.

Likely follow-ups
  • The database is the bottleneck, not the pods. Now what?
  • How do you scale back down safely?
How would you autoscale Kafka consumers running on Kubernetes?

"CPU is the wrong signal; consumer lag is the right one, because lag is the queue depth that users feel as delay. I would use KEDA's Kafka scaler on the consumer group's lag, with a target lag per replica. The key limit: in a consumer group each partition goes to at most one consumer, so replicas beyond the partition count sit idle; set maxReplicas to the partition count. Scaling triggers rebalances, so use the cooperative rebalancing protocol and a stabilization window to avoid flapping. On Kafka 4.x, set group.protocol=consumer (KIP-848). The broker then assigns partitions incrementally, with no group-wide stop-the-world rebalance, so rolling updates and KEDA scale events barely pause consumption. If lag still grows at max replicas, the fix is more partitions or faster processing, not more pods."

How to answer

Signal (lag), mechanism (KEDA), the hard limit (partitions), the side effect (rebalances). Links to Consumer groups and the pipeline on Kubernetes.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can you scale to zero? (Yes with KEDA, at the cost of cold-start latency.)
  • What if one partition is hot?
One cluster or many? How would you run multi-region?

"Many clusters is the norm at scale: one or more per region and environment, to limit blast radius, respect data residency, and keep each control plane within tested limits. Clusters do not usually share state; each is managed declaratively from git with Argo CD or Flux, so they stay consistent. A global load balancer or DNS steers users to the nearest healthy region. The hard part is not Kubernetes, it is data: databases and Kafka topics must replicate across regions, and you choose active-passive or active-active based on consistency needs. Uber's migration used a federation layer so developers did not care which cluster ran their service."

How to answer

Why many clusters, how they are kept consistent, how traffic is steered, then "the data layer is the real problem".

Likely follow-ups
  • How does a service in region A call one in region B?
  • What is your recovery plan if a whole region is lost? See Reliability & DR.
Design a CI/CD platform on Kubernetes. Why is GitOps pull-based?

"CI: each build runs in an ephemeral pod, for example with Tekton or Argo Workflows or a runner controller, on autoscaled nodes, spot instances to save cost, with build caches, and untrusted builds isolated on separate nodes or sandboxed. Output is a signed, scanned image in our registry. CD: a GitOps controller like Argo CD runs inside each cluster and pulls the desired manifests from git, applying and continuously correcting drift. Pull-based means CI never holds cluster credentials, which removes the most dangerous secret, and the cluster always converges to git. Rollout policy (canary, automated analysis) sits on top."

How to answer

Split CI and CD. For GitOps, say the security win (no push credentials) and the reliability win (drift correction).

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you promote from staging to prod? (A git change: bump the image tag in the prod folder via PR.)
How would you isolate many teams on a shared cluster?

"Namespace per team, RBAC from identity-provider groups, ResourceQuota and LimitRange, default-deny NetworkPolicies, Pod Security restricted, policy-as-code for registries and labels, and dedicated node pools with taints for sensitive or noisy teams. And the honest caveat: that is soft isolation, because pods on one node share a kernel and some objects like CRDs are cluster-wide. For untrusted tenants I would give separate clusters."

How to answer

Checklist plus caveat. Full version with a diagram on Security & RBAC.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop one team overloading the API server?
An attacker has a shell in one pod. Walk me through what stops them.

"Step by step from their point of view. Calling the API: no token mounted, or a ServiceAccount with minimal RBAC. Moving sideways: default-deny NetworkPolicies, and blocked access to the cloud metadata endpoint. Escaping to the node: non-root, all capabilities dropped, seccomp, read-only root filesystem, user namespaces, and sandboxed runtimes for untrusted code. Reaching cloud resources: workload identity scoped to this one app's role. Staying hidden: audit logs and runtime detection. Each layer turns a full compromise into a contained one."

How to answer

Attacker's path, one control per step. Shows security thinking without memorising a checklist.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does privileged: true give the attacker?
How would you reduce the cost of a Kubernetes platform?

"First measure: compare requested resources with actual usage per team, because over-requested resources are the biggest waste and the scheduler packs by requests. Right-size requests using VPA recommendations. Then bin-pack better with Karpenter consolidation or a tuned Cluster Autoscaler. Use spot or preemptible nodes for stateless and batch work, with PDBs and multiple instance types to survive interruptions. Scale to zero for idle dev environments and event-driven workers with KEDA. Schedule non-urgent batch at off-peak. And show teams their own cost, because visibility changes behaviour."

How to answer

Measure, right-size, pack, buy cheaper capacity, turn off idle. In that order.

Likely follow-ups
  • What breaks when spot nodes disappear?
"You come from networking and embedded work. You have not run Kubernetes in production. Why should we trust your answers?"

"Fair question. My background is control planes and data planes, which is what Kubernetes is. I have spent years on reconciliation between a routing daemon and the forwarding table, failure detection with hello and dead timers, ECMP hashing, NAT, VXLAN and BGP fabrics, and cgroup and memory limits on embedded Linux. In Kubernetes, the controllers are that reconciliation loop, node heartbeats are hold-down timers, a ClusterIP is DNAT in every node's kernel, and CNI overlays are VXLAN. I have run these ideas locally on kind clusters, broken them on purpose and debugged them. What I still need is scale experience with many teams, and I learn that fastest by being on call next to people who have it."

How to answer

Do not apologise. Translate three or four concrete things, show you have practised hands-on, and name the honest gap with a plan. For Amazon, this is also a "Learn and Be Curious" story.

Likely follow-ups
  • Tell me about a hard debugging problem from your current job, in our language.

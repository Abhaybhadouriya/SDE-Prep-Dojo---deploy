Production scenario drills

These are based on real public incidents and common failure stories. Read the situation, answer out loud for two minutes, then compare. Notice the pattern in every strong answer: protect users first, then gather evidence, then fix the root cause, then prevent it next time. More drills in the Interview Hub.

Scenario 1: the upgrade that broke all pod networking

You upgrade your most important cluster from one minor version to the next. Control plane and nodes come up green, but pods cannot reach pods on other nodes. You use Calico in BGP mode with route reflectors. (Based on Reddit's 2023 Pi-Day outage.)

Weak answer: "I would downgrade back to the old version."

Strong answer: "Kubernetes has no supported downgrade, so the realistic options are fix forward or restore the control plane from an etcd backup. First I check the data plane bottom-up: are BGP sessions established, do nodes have routes to other nodes' pod CIDRs? If routes are missing, I look at what selects the route reflectors. In Reddit's case they were chosen by the node label node-role.kubernetes.io/master, which was removed in that version in favour of control-plane, so no node was a route reflector anymore. The fix is to update the selector. Prevention: read release notes for removals, keep every piece of network config in git so nothing is hidden, upgrade a less critical cluster first, and practise restores."

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you have caught this in a test cluster?
  • Why are route reflectors needed at all? (Full iBGP mesh does not scale.)

Scenario 2: every node loses pod networking after an OS update

Overnight, pods across several regions lose network connectivity. Nobody deployed anything. The nodes use Cilium. Hosts got automatic Ubuntu security updates. (Based on Datadog's March 2023 incident.)

Weak answer: "Restart all the pods."

Strong answer: "No deploy plus many nodes at once means a shared node-level change. I check what changed on hosts: package updates. On a broken node I compare ip rule and routing tables with a healthy one. In Datadog's case an update restarted systemd-networkd, which flushed policy-routing rules it did not create, including Cilium's. Mitigation: restore the rules (restarting the CNI agent re-creates them) and stop the automatic updates. Note that cloud auto-healing replacing many nodes at once can make recovery slower for stateful workloads with local disks. Prevention: treat OS updates as a staged rollout like any deploy, and configure networkd not to manage foreign rules."

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stage OS updates on a fleet?

Scenario 3: a dead node, and pods took six minutes to move

A node lost power. Your payment service had 2 of its 4 pods on it. Customers saw errors for several minutes. The team asks why Kubernetes was so slow.

Weak answer: "Kubernetes is slow; we should move to something else."

Strong answer: "That timing is the default design. The node lifecycle controller waits about 50 seconds of missed heartbeats before marking the node NotReady, then pods tolerate the not-ready taint for 300 seconds before eviction, then replacements must schedule and start. But traffic should not have failed for that long: once the node is NotReady its pods' endpoints are removed. So I would check three things: whether two of four replicas on one node was the real problem (use topology spread across nodes and zones), whether clients retried on another pod, and whether we want shorter tolerationSeconds for this service. I would also run 6 replicas across 3 zones so one node is never half the capacity."

Likely follow-ups
  • What is the downside of very short tolerationSeconds? (Flapping on short network blips.)

Scenario 4: the database blipped and every pod restarted

The database had a 30-second failover. Afterwards, all 40 pods of the API restarted within a minute, and the API was down for 5 minutes, much longer than the database.

Weak answer: "Increase the database's availability."

Strong answer: "The amplification points to the liveness probe. If /healthz checks the database, the kubelet saw failures during the failover and restarted every container. Then all 40 pods cold-started at once, hit the database together, and CrashLoopBackOff delays stretched the outage. Fix: liveness only checks that the process itself is responsive; dependency health belongs in readiness at most, or in the app's own retry and circuit-breaker logic. Add a startup probe so cold starts are not killed. Verify by checking events for 'Liveness probe failed' at that time."

Likely follow-ups
  • Should readiness fail when the database is down? (If it does, all pods go unready together and the Service has no endpoints; often better to stay ready and return fast errors.)

Scenario 5: the sale starts and pods are stuck Pending

At 8 p.m. the sale begins. The HPA scales the checkout service from 20 to 80 pods, but 50 are Pending for 4 minutes while users see timeouts.

Weak answer: "Set the HPA maximum higher."

Strong answer: "The HPA did its job; the cluster had no room, and adding nodes takes minutes: provisioning, joining, pulling images. Short term: manually add node capacity and pre-scale. Long term, for a known event: pre-scale on a schedule before it starts, keep headroom with low-priority placeholder pods that get preempted instantly, pre-pull images, use Karpenter with several instance types so capacity is found faster, and check cloud quotas in advance. Also rate-limit and shed load at the gateway so a spike degrades gracefully instead of timing out everyone."

Likely follow-ups
  • How do placeholder pods work? (A low PriorityClass; real pods preempt them, and the autoscaler then adds nodes for the evicted placeholders.)

Scenario 6: twenty consumer pods, and lag still grows

An order-processing consumer on Kubernetes falls behind. You scale from 12 to 20 pods. Lag keeps growing. The topic has 12 partitions.

Weak answer: "Scale to 50 pods."

Strong answer: "With 12 partitions, at most 12 consumers in the group do work; the other 8 are idle, and scaling triggered rebalances that paused consumption. So more pods cannot help. I would check whether one partition is hot (a skewed key), and what each consumer spends time on: a slow downstream call, small batches, synchronous commits. Fixes: speed up processing (batching, async I/O, more threads per consumer with ordering per key preserved), increase partitions if the key design allows it (remember it changes key-to-partition mapping for new messages), and cap autoscaling at the partition count, scaling on lag with KEDA."

Likely follow-ups
  • Why does adding partitions affect ordering?
  • See Consumers & groups.

Scenario 7: the API server starts rejecting all writes

Deploys fail. kubectl apply returns errors mentioning etcdserver: mvcc: database space exceeded. Running apps are fine.

Weak answer: "Restart the API server."

Strong answer: "etcd has hit its storage quota (2 GB by default) and raised a NOSPACE alarm, so it refuses writes. Running pods are fine because the data plane does not need etcd. Recovery: take a snapshot first, compact old revisions, defragment each member to actually free space, then disarm the alarm. Then find out what filled it: often huge numbers of Events, many objects from a runaway controller or CI creating ConfigMaps, or very large objects. Prevention: monitor etcd database size against the quota, keep Events short-lived or in a separate etcd (as OpenAI did), and fix the noisy client."

Likely follow-ups
  • Why compact and then defragment, not just one?
  • Would raising the quota fix it? (Short-term only; the etcd docs advise staying under about 8 GB.)

Scenario 8: slow responses every few seconds, no errors anywhere

A Java service's p99 latency jumped from 40 ms to 400 ms after you moved it to Kubernetes with limits.cpu: 1. Average CPU is 50%. Nothing is restarting.

Weak answer: "Add more replicas."

Strong answer: "Classic CFS throttling. Modern JVMs (JDK 10+ and 8u191+) are container-aware and size GC threads from the CPU limit, but the app's own pools (for example a 200-thread Tomcat pool, ForkJoin or Netty workers sized in config) and any old JDK that ignores cgroups still run many runnable threads. In a burst they burn the 100 ms quota early in each period and then everything pauses. I would confirm with the throttled-periods ratio and GC logs. Fixes: raise or remove the CPU limit (keep the request), set -XX:ActiveProcessorCount or right-size the thread pools to match the limit, and re-test. More replicas would reduce load per pod but leave the throttling pattern in place."

Likely follow-ups
  • Why did this not happen on the VM? (No CFS quota per process group there.)

Scenario 9: kubectl delete namespace is stuck in Terminating

A teammate deleted a test namespace an hour ago. It still shows Terminating and blocks recreating it.

Weak answer: "Edit etcd directly and remove it."

Strong answer: "A namespace finishes deleting only when everything inside it is gone. Something is waiting on a finalizer, a hook that says 'do not delete until my controller cleans up'. I check the namespace's status conditions, which usually name the blocker: resources with finalizers whose controller is uninstalled, or an unavailable aggregated API (for example a broken metrics API) that stops discovery. The fix is to bring the controller or API back so cleanup completes, or, knowing the external resource it guards, remove that object's finalizer deliberately. Editing etcd directly is never the first move."

Likely follow-ups
  • What is a finalizer for? (Cleaning up external things like cloud load balancers or disks before the object vanishes.)

Scenario 10: random 5-second delays on outbound calls

Some HTTP calls from pods to an external payment API take just over 5 seconds, then succeed. It happens to a small percentage of requests, more under load.

Weak answer: "The payment provider is slow."

Strong answer: "A delay of almost exactly 5 seconds smells like a DNS timeout and retry, since 5 s is the default resolver timeout. Pods use ndots:5 and several search domains, so one external lookup becomes many queries, A and AAAA in parallel over UDP; under load, conntrack races on those parallel UDP packets have been reported to drop one, and the resolver waits out the timeout. I would confirm with traces showing time spent before the connection and with a capture on port 53. Fixes: use the fully qualified name with a trailing dot, lower ndots for this pod, deploy NodeLocal DNSCache, and scale CoreDNS."

Likely follow-ups
  • How does NodeLocal DNSCache help? (Lookups go to a cache on the same node, avoiding the conntrack path to CoreDNS.)

Scenario 11: "You have never run Kubernetes in production"

The interviewer looks at your resume (line cards, control-plane software, kernel drivers) and says: "This is a cloud-native platform role. You have no web or Kubernetes production experience. Why should we hire you?" This is not a trick; they want to see how you map what you know.

Weak answer: "That's true, but I'm a fast learner and I did a course." (Apologetic, vague, and it gives them nothing to check.)

Strong answer (the reply script): "That's fair: I have not been on call for a Kubernetes cluster. What I have done for years is the same class of problem one layer down: keeping a distributed control plane converged with the hardware, rolling software across live systems without dropping traffic, and debugging state that disagrees between components. Kubernetes is a control-plane-plus-agents system, so I map each idea to something I have run. Let me give you three short examples." Then tell two or three of the stories below in STAR form (Situation, Task, Action, Result), one minute each, and end with: "On a kind cluster I have reproduced rolling updates, probe failures and node loss, so I know where my mapping holds and where it breaks."

Story 1 - ISSU becomes a rolling update. S: A chassis with 8 line cards needed a software upgrade with no traffic loss. T: I owned the upgrade sequencing. A: We upgraded one card at a time, waited for it to be fully up (ports up, FIB programmed) before draining the next, and kept enough capacity to carry full load. R: Zero dropped sessions. Bridge: "That is exactly maxUnavailable: 0, maxSurge: 1 and a readiness gate: never remove capacity until the new copy proves it can serve."

Story 2 - a hardware watchdog becomes a liveness probe. S: A process occasionally hung in a deadlock while still holding its PID. T: Detect and recover without a human. A: We added a watchdog the process had to kick every few seconds; a missed kick triggered a restart, with back-off so a crash loop could not thrash the box. R: Mean time to recover fell from hours (waiting for a ticket) to seconds. Bridge: "That is a liveness probe plus CrashLoopBackOff. And I learned the same lesson Kubernetes users learn: the watchdog must check the process, not its dependencies, or one slow database restarts everything."

Story 3 - ECMP member withdrawal becomes readiness. S: When a next hop went unhealthy, traffic kept hashing to it for seconds. T: Stop the black-holing. A: We tied BFD to the ECMP group so an unhealthy member was withdrawn before traffic moved, and re-added only after it was stable. R: Loss during failures dropped to the BFD detection time. Bridge: "That is a readiness probe removing a pod from the EndpointSlice: it stays alive, but stops getting new traffic."

Story 4 (optional) - a route-table reconciler becomes a controller. "Our control-plane agent compared desired routes from the RIB with what the hardware FIB actually had and fixed the difference, on a loop, and after every restart. Kubernetes controllers are the same level-triggered reconciliation loop, with the API server as the RIB."

Likely follow-ups
  • Where does your analogy break? (Pods are disposable and get new IPs; line cards keep identity. StatefulSets are the closer match for that.)
  • What would you do in your first 30 days on our platform team? (Read the runbooks and recent postmortems, shadow on-call, build a kind cluster that mirrors prod's CNI and ingress.)
  • What part of Kubernetes do you find hardest? (Be honest; networking and security policy are good, credible answers for your background.)

Interview questions

How would you set up on-call for a new team of 7 engineers?

Weekly primary and secondary rotation, handed off on a weekday morning with a written handoff note. Escalation: primary paged, secondary after 5 minutes without ack, engineering manager after another 10. Only symptom-based, actionable alerts page (SLO burn rates on the key user journeys); everything else becomes a ticket or a dashboard. Every paging alert links to a runbook. New joiners shadow, then reverse-shadow, before going solo. I would track pages per shift and review noisy alerts weekly, aiming for no more than a couple of incidents per shift as Google's SRE book suggests. With 7 people on one site, each is primary about one week in seven; if night pages are frequent, I would split shifts or partner with a team in another time zone for follow-the-sun. Comp time after rough nights.

How to answer

Cover rotation, escalation, what pages, runbooks, onboarding, load measurement and humane policies. Numbers (5-minute ack, 2 incidents per shift) show you know the reference material.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the team gets 20 pages a week?
  • Should developers be on call for their own services?
What should page a human, and what should not?

A page must be urgent, actionable and tied to user impact, now or very soon. So I page on symptoms: SLO burn-rate alerts on availability and latency of user journeys, plus a few "about to hurt" conditions like a disk that will be full in two hours. I do not page on causes such as high CPU or a single pod restart; those go to dashboards, or to tickets when they need attention within days. Slow budget burns are tickets. Alertmanager-style routing sends severity=page to the pager and the rest to a chat or ticket queue, with inhibition so one regional failure does not trigger fifty pages. Any alert that pages and turns out not actionable gets fixed or deleted in the weekly review.

How to answer

State the three-part rule, then symptoms vs causes, then give two examples of each side, then mention noise review. Linking to error budgets earns points.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you alert on a low-traffic service?
  • What is a multi-window burn-rate alert?
Walk me through how you would run a SEV-1 from the first page.

Ack the page, look at the SLO dashboard to confirm impact and scope, and declare the incident early: severity, channel, incident doc, bridge. I name an IC (myself until someone takes over) and quickly assign an ops lead and a comms lead. Comms posts the first update within minutes, with impact and next-update time, and updates the status page. Ops asks "what changed?": deploys, flags, config, infrastructure, dependency status. We mitigate first: roll back, disable the flag, fail over or shed load, even before we are sure of the cause. Only the ops lead's group touches production. The scribe keeps a timestamped timeline. Once SLIs are healthy and stable, we resolve, list temporary hacks to revert, give the all-clear, and schedule the postmortem within a few days.

How to answer

Narrate the lifecycle (detect, triage, mitigate, resolve, postmortem) and name the roles. Emphasise "mitigate before root cause" and "communicate on a cadence".

Likely follow-ups
  • The rollback did not help. Now what?
  • An executive joins the channel and starts giving orders. What do you do?
  • How do you hand off at the end of your shift mid-incident?
What does the incident commander do, and why should they not debug?

The IC owns the overall state of the incident: they set severity, assign roles, decide on risky actions (failover, rollback), remove blockers, keep the incident doc current and make sure communication happens. In the SRE book's model they hold any role they have not delegated. They should not debug because debugging takes all of your attention; the moment the IC is reading logs, nobody notices that two people are changing the same system, that stakeholders have had no update for 40 minutes, or that the shift is ending. If the IC is also the only expert, they should hand the IC role to someone else and go debug.

How to answer

Define, list duties, explain the "attention not hands" principle, mention handing off the role.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is in the live incident document?
  • Who can be IC? Does it have to be a manager? (No: trained engineers rotate.)
What is a blameless postmortem, and how do you keep it from becoming toothless?

A blameless postmortem documents impact, timeline, causes, detection, resolution and action items, while assuming everyone acted in good faith with what they knew, and looking for systemic contributing factors rather than a person to blame. It matters because blame makes people hide information, and the honest account of what someone did and why it looked safe is the most valuable data. It stays sharp by being specific about systems: "the deploy tool allowed an all-region push with one approval" is a hard finding. Action items must have owners, priorities, tickets and due dates, be a mix of prevent, detect and mitigate, and be tracked in a regular review. Senior reviewers check depth (did we stop at "human error"?). Repeat incidents with the same cause are the signal that items are not being finished.

How to answer

Definition, why blameless, then the accountability mechanisms. Use a concrete before/after sentence ("Ravi pushed bad config" vs "the pipeline had no per-region validation").

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the same engineer causes three incidents?
  • Which incidents deserve a postmortem?
Root cause analysis: is "5 whys" enough? What are its weaknesses?

5 whys is a good habit to push past the trigger to something changeable, but it tends to produce a single linear chain and stop at the first plausible answer, and different people get different chains. Real outages usually need several conditions at once: a bad change, a gap in testing, a canary that missed a region, a slow alert. So I use 5 whys to explore each branch and then list contributing factors explicitly (the Swiss cheese view), producing action items that close holes in several layers: prevention, detection and mitigation. I also ask "what went well" and "where did we get lucky", because luck hides latent risk.

How to answer

Credit the technique, then name the linear-chain weakness and offer contributing factors as the fix. A short example of a branching "why" makes it concrete.

Likely follow-ups
  • Give an example where fixing the "root cause" alone would not have been enough.
Your team gets 40 pages a week and people are burning out. What do you do?

First measure: export a month of pages, group by alert, and mark each as actionable or not and user-impacting or not. Typically a handful of alerts cause most pages. Then: delete or downgrade non-actionable alerts to tickets or dashboards; replace cause alerts (CPU, single-host errors) with SLO burn-rate symptom alerts; add grouping and inhibition so one failure is one page; automate the fixes the runbooks repeat (auto-restart, auto-scale, disk cleanup); and file engineering work for the top real causes, protected by the error budget policy (if the budget is spent, reliability work takes priority over features). Meanwhile protect people: split night shifts, add a secondary, give comp time. Track pages per shift weekly until it is below about two incidents per shift.

How to answer

Data first, then remove noise, then fix causes, then automation, then human protection. Showing you would measure before changing things is what interviewers listen for.

Likely follow-ups
  • Product says there is no time for reliability work. How do you argue?
Tell me about an incident you handled. (Behavioural)

Use a structure: context and impact in numbers, how it was detected, your role, the key decision (usually a mitigation under uncertainty), what you learned in the postmortem, and the action item you owned and finished. For a networking background, a strong story is a field escalation: "A customer's core router rebooted every few hours after an upgrade; I led the debug, we rolled the image back as mitigation within the maintenance window, the crash dump showed a race in the RX ring teardown, I wrote the RCA without naming the developer, and we added a stress test and a faster watchdog." Then map it to web vocabulary: severity, rollback, RCA, prevent/detect/mitigate action items.

How to answer

STAR format, with numbers and with a blameless tone. See the postmortem storytelling template.

Likely follow-ups
  • What would you do differently?
  • How did you communicate with the customer?

Scenario: paged at 3 am, the owner is unclear

You are the secondary on-call for the platform team. At 03:07 a page fires: "checkout 5xx rate 8% for 10 minutes". The primary has not acknowledged. The alert names a service your team does not own, and its listed owner left the company.

Weak answer: "Start reading the checkout code to find the bug."

Strong answer: Acknowledge, then declare an incident and set a severity: 8% of checkouts failing is customer-facing revenue loss, so SEV-2 or higher. Take incident commander until someone better placed arrives, and open the incident channel. Ownership comes second to mitigation: check what changed (deploys, flags, config, infra events in the last hour) and the dashboards for checkout's dependencies. Page the escalation path for checkout through the service catalogue, and the payments team if errors are downstream. Post a first status update within 15 minutes, even if it says "investigating". Afterwards, fix the routing: an alert must map to an owning team, not a person.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if you cannot reach anyone on the owning team? (Escalate to the engineering manager or the incident-management rota; the IC can authorise a rollback.)
  • When do you update the status page? (As soon as customer impact is confirmed, then on a fixed cadence.)

Scenario: a bad deploy found in the middle of the incident

Twenty minutes in, someone notices that payment-api v1.42 rolled out 5 minutes before the errors started. The team wants to find the root cause in the diff before doing anything.

Weak answer: "Let us understand the bug first, so we fix it properly."

Strong answer: Mitigate first, diagnose later. A deploy that lines up with the start of impact is the most likely cause, and rolling back is cheap and reversible, so roll back to v1.41 now (or turn off the feature flag) and watch the error rate. If it recovers, impact ends and the root cause can be found calmly in the postmortem. If not, you have removed one variable. Check that the rollback is safe first: a schema migration that v1.41 cannot read changes the plan (that is why migrations follow expand-migrate-contract, see Zero-Downtime Releases). Freeze other deploys until the incident is closed.

Likely follow-ups
  • When would you not roll back? (An irreversible migration, or evidence the deploy is unrelated.)
  • How do you make this decision faster next time? (Deploy markers on dashboards, automatic rollback on SLO burn.)

Scenario: the postmortem says "root cause: human error"

The draft postmortem for a 40-minute outage reads: "Root cause: engineer ran the cleanup script against prod instead of staging. Action item: be more careful."

Weak answer: "Agree, and add a reminder in the runbook to double-check the environment."

Strong answer: Blameless means asking why the system let a normal mistake become an outage. Rewrite it: the script took the environment from an ambient profile, prod credentials were available on a laptop by default, the script had no dry-run or confirmation, and nothing limited how much it could delete. Action items become concrete and owned: the script requires an explicit --env and prints a plan, prod access goes through a short-lived elevated role, destructive operations are rate-limited and backed up, and an alert fires on mass deletions. "Be more careful" is not an action item because it cannot be verified. Thank the engineer for the detailed timeline.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you keep postmortems blameless when leadership wants a name? (Focus the review on the system and the decision context; separate any performance issue from the incident process.)

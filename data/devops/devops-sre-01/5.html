Running an incident: roles, lifecycle and comms

Must know (SDE-1)

The biggest mistake in incidents is not technical. It is everyone debugging, nobody coordinating: five engineers change things at once, nobody knows what was tried, customers hear nothing and executives keep asking in the channel. The fix, borrowed from firefighters (the Incident Command System), is named roles.

  • Incident Commander (IC): holds the high-level state, assigns work, removes blockers, decides (roll back now? fail over the region?). The IC deliberately does not type commands; their job is attention, not hands.
  • Operations lead: leads the people actually changing systems. A key rule from the SRE book: during the incident only the ops team modifies production, so changes do not collide.
  • Communications lead: the public face. Sends regular updates to stakeholders, updates the status page, shields engineers from "any update?" pings.
  • Planning: longer-term support: files bugs, arranges handoffs when shifts end, tracks temporary hacks (a disabled feature flag, a scaled-up cluster) so they get reverted.
  • Scribe: writes the timeline with timestamps in the live incident document. Gold for the postmortem.

The incident lifecycle

  1. Detect. Ideally a symptom alert, not a customer tweet. Time from start to detection is MTTD (mean time to detect).
  2. Triage. The on-call acks (MTTA, mean time to acknowledge), checks impact, sets a severity and, if it meets the bar, declares an incident: opens a channel like #inc-2026-11-23-checkout, a live incident doc and a video bridge, and names an IC.
  3. Mitigate. Stop the user pain first, understand later. Roll back the last deploy, flip the feature flag off, fail over to the other region, shed load, scale out. A good IC asks within minutes: "What changed recently, and can we undo it?"
  4. Resolve. Confirm the SLIs are back to normal for a while, revert temporary hacks or file tickets for them, give the all-clear.
  5. Postmortem. Within a few days, written blamelessly, reviewed, with owned action items.

Interviewers love this: "Mitigate first, root-cause later." If a deploy went out 10 minutes before errors started, roll it back now even if you are not sure it is the cause. A rollback is cheap and reversible; an hour of debugging while customers fail is not.

Communication: short, regular, honest

Stakeholders fear silence more than bad news. The comms lead posts on a fixed cadence (for example every 30 minutes for SEV-1, even when the update is "no change"), always saying when the next update comes. A simple template:

[SEV-2] Checkout errors - update #3 - 14:30 UTC
Impact:   ~8% of checkout attempts in ap-south-1 failing with HTTP 503 since 13:52 UTC.
Status:   Mitigating. Rolled back checkout-api v412 -> v411 at 14:21; error rate falling (8% -> 2%).
Next:     Watching for 15 min; investigating why the canary did not catch it.
IC: Asha  |  Ops: Dev  |  Comms: Sam  |  Channel: #inc-20261123-checkout
Next update: 15:00 UTC or sooner if anything changes.

From your world

A large network outage bridge works the same way: a duty manager runs the call, one engineer has the console on the affected router, others gather show-tech output, and someone keeps the customer's account team updated. The rule "only the ops lead changes production" is the same as "one person on the CLI during the fix" so that two engineers do not issue conflicting clear and shutdown commands.

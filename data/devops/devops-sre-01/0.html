The story: the fire station

Explain like I'm 5

A town has a fire station. Every week, a different firefighter keeps the big red phone next to their bed. That is their turn. If the phone rings and nobody answers in five minutes, it rings at the next firefighter's house, and then at the chief's. When there is a fire, one firefighter becomes the boss of that fire. The boss does not hold a hose. The boss says "you, water on the left side", "you, tell the neighbours what is happening", and keeps a notebook of what was done and when. On the wall there is a card for every kind of fire: "kitchen fire: turn off the gas first". After the fire is out, everyone sits down with cocoa and asks: "Why did the fire start, and why did it spread?" Nobody gets yelled at. If they yelled, next time people would hide the matches instead of telling the truth. They write down three fixes, put a name next to each one, and check them next week.

That is this whole page. The red phone rotating each week is an on-call rotation. Ringing the next person if nobody answers is an escalation policy. The fire boss who does not hold a hose is the incident commander. The person telling the neighbours is the communications lead. The notebook is the incident timeline. The cards on the wall are runbooks. The cocoa meeting is a blameless postmortem, and the fixes with names next to them are owned action items.

From your world

You already know this machinery from networking vendors. Cisco-style TAC cases have priorities P1 to P4 with response targets and an escalation path to engineering and duty managers: that is a severity scheme plus an escalation policy. Syslog has severity levels 0 (emergency) to 7 (debug), and you would never page someone for a level-6 informational message: that is the page-vs-ticket rule. And when a box hits a kernel panic, you pull the kdump vmcore, walk the stack in crash, and write an RCA that says which code path and which missing check let it happen, not "Ravi typed the wrong command". That is a blameless postmortem. The difference in web services: the "box" is hundreds of services owned by different teams, so coordination and communication become as hard as the debugging.

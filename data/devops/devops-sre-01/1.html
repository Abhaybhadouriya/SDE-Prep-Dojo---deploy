On-call: who holds the pager, and for how long

Must know (SDE-1)

On-call means: for a fixed window, one named engineer is responsible for responding to urgent alerts ("pages") for a set of services, usually from a phone app such as PagerDuty or Opsgenie. The goal is simple: when production breaks at 3 a.m., exactly one person knows it is their job, has the access to act, and has the documents to know what to do.

Numbers from Google's SRE book

≤ 25%of an SRE's time on-call
≤ 50%of time on all ops work
2incidents per 12-h shift, max
5 minresponse for user-facing pages

Google's SRE book argues that on-call must be sustainable, or quality drops. It caps operational work at 50% of an SRE's time and on-call at 25%. It estimates an incident (handling, cleanup, postmortem) takes about 6 hours, so at most 2 incidents per 12-hour shift; beyond that the team must fix the sources of pages. It quotes 5-minute response for user-facing or time-critical services and 30 minutes for less time-sensitive ones. For a single-site team to keep these limits with week-long shifts, it suggests at least 8 engineers; for a dual-site follow-the-sun team, at least 6 per site.

A healthy on-call checklist

  • Clear ownership: every alert routes to exactly one rotation. "Shared" alerts are nobody's alerts.
  • Access before the shift: VPN, prod read access, break-glass role, dashboards bookmarked, phone app tested with a test page.
  • A handoff ritual: outgoing engineer writes what is open, what was silenced and why, what deploys are risky.
  • Compensation and recovery: time off after a bad night, on-call pay or comp days. Exhaustion causes mistakes.
  • Pager-load review: weekly look at "pages per shift" and "which alerts were noise". Every noisy alert gets fixed or deleted.
  • Shadowing for new joiners: a new engineer shadows a shift, then reverse-shadows (they lead, a veteran watches) before going solo.

The story in one minute

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine the school office has one giant notebook. When anything happens - a kid arrives, a lunch is ordered, a library book is returned - someone writes one line at the bottom of the notebook. Nobody ever rubs out a line. Nobody tears out a page.

Now many people can use that notebook. The cook reads the lunch lines to know what to make. The nurse reads the "kid arrived" lines to count who is at school. The librarian reads the book lines. Each of them keeps a bookmark, so each one reads at their own speed. If the cook is sick for a day, the lines just wait. Tomorrow the cook opens the notebook at the bookmark and catches up.

That notebook is Kafka. The people writing lines are producers. The people reading are consumers. Each line is an event (also called a message or a record). The bookmark is an offset.

In the real world, the "school" is a company like LinkedIn, Uber, Netflix, Walmart or PhonePe. The "lines" are things like a user clicked a button, an order was placed, a payment succeeded, a driver moved 20 metres. Hundreds of programs want to know about these events, and they all work at different speeds. Kafka sits in the middle, keeps every event safely on disk for days (or forever), and lets every reader catch up in its own time.

Apache Kafka is free, open-source software. It was built at LinkedIn around 2010 by Jay Kreps, Neha Narkhede and Jun Rao, open-sourced in early 2011, and became a top-level Apache project in October 2012. The current release line in late 2026 is Kafka 4.4, and since 4.0 Kafka no longer needs a separate helper system called ZooKeeper (you will meet the replacement, KRaft, on page 08).

From your world

You already know this shape. A Kafka partition is an append-only log, very close to a ring buffer or a filesystem journal: writers add at the tail, and each reader keeps its own read index (the offset), like several consumers indexing into a shared DMA ring. A producer that has to wait because the broker is slow is like a socket send buffer that is full. A consumer that falls behind is a queue that is getting deeper. Where this breaks: a ring buffer overwrites old slots when it wraps. Kafka never overwrites. It deletes whole old files (segments) only when they pass a time or size limit, which is 7 days by default.

  1. Three events are already in the log at offsets 0, 1 and 2. Both readers start at offset 0.
  2. The producer appends event 3 at the end. Nothing is ever inserted in the middle. The cook reads fast; the nurse is slower.
  3. The nurse stops reading for a while. Nothing is lost and the cook is not slowed down: the events simply stay on disk.
  4. Event 4 arrives. The nurse comes back, starts from its saved offset and catches up. How far a reader is behind is called consumer lag.

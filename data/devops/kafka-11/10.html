Interview questions

60-second answer - "How do you choose the number of partitions?" "I start from throughput. I measure what one partition can take on the producer side and, more importantly, what one consumer can process. Partitions should be at least the target throughput divided by the smaller of those, and at least the maximum number of consumers I want in a group. Then I add headroom for one or two years, because adding partitions later re-maps keys and breaks per-key ordering for a while. I avoid going huge, since every partition costs file handles, memory and recovery time. For example, 100 MB/s with consumers doing 5 MB/s each gives 20; I would create about 40."

How do you decide the number of partitions for a topic? Can you change it later?

Use partitions >= max(T/p, T/c), where T is target throughput and p, c are measured per-partition producer and consumer throughput. Also make it at least the largest consumer-group size you need, and check that you have many more distinct keys than partitions. Add growth headroom. You can increase the count later but never decrease it, and increasing it changes hash(key) % N, so keyed data loses ordering until old events drain. Too many partitions cost files, memory, replication and failover time.

How to answer

Give the formula, a worked number, the "only up" rule with the key-remap consequence, then the cost of going too high. Saying "I would measure p and c with the perf tools" shows you are practical.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you grow a keyed topic safely? (Drain first, or new topic and migrate.)
  • What if one key is bigger than one partition can handle? (Salt the key, re-aggregate downstream.)
Which Kafka metrics would you alert on, and why?

Page on OfflinePartitionsCount > 0 (outage), UnderMinIsrPartitionCount > 0 (acks=all writes failing), and ActiveControllerCount not equal to 1. Warn on UnderReplicatedPartitions > 0 for more than a few minutes, ISR shrink rate, low request-handler or network idle percent, rising p99 produce/fetch latency, and disk usage. For consumers, alert on lag growth and on time-behind compared with the SLA and with retention.

How to answer

Split into "page now" and "look today". Explain what each one means for users, not just its name. Mention you alert on lag trend, not a single spike.

Likely follow-ups
  • URP is 0 but users see slow produces - where do you look? (TotalTimeMs broken into queue/local/remote, handler idle percent, disk latency, GC.)
  • How do you measure lag without touching the app? (kafka_exporter or Burrow reading committed offsets.)
How would you tune a producer for throughput, and what do you give up?

Raise batch.size (for example 128 KiB), raise linger.ms (10-50 ms), turn on lz4 or zstd compression, and give enough buffer.memory. Keep acks=all and idempotence on for safety. The cost is latency (up to linger.ms per message) and CPU for compression. I would prove it with kafka-producer-perf-test.sh and compare p99 latency before and after.

How to answer

Name the knob, the direction, the reason (fuller batches compress better and need fewer requests) and the trade-off. Finish with "and I would measure it".

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not just use acks=1 for speed? (It can lose acknowledged data on leader failure; batching gives most of the gain safely.)
  • What happens when buffer.memory fills? (send() blocks up to max.block.ms, then throws - that is backpressure.)
How do you run Kafka on Kubernetes, and what is hard about it?

Use an operator such as Strimzi. You declare Kafka, KafkaNodePool, KafkaTopic and KafkaUser resources; the operator creates broker and controller pods with persistent volumes, TLS, services and config, and does safe rolling restarts. The hard parts: fast persistent storage, keeping pods from being evicted carelessly (PodDisruptionBudgets), rack awareness across zones, rebalancing after scaling (Cruise Control via KafkaRebalance), and external access, because clients must reach each broker directly through its advertised listener.

How to answer

Operator first, then the per-broker addressing gotcha, then "if the team does not have Kubernetes and Kafka skills, I would use a managed service". That last line shows judgment.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not a plain Deployment? (Brokers need stable identity and their own disk.)
  • How does a client outside the cluster connect? (Per-broker LoadBalancer/NodePort/Ingress listeners.)
You added two brokers but the old brokers are still overloaded. Why?

Kafka does not move existing partitions onto new brokers by itself. New brokers only receive partitions of new topics or of explicit reassignments. Generate a reassignment plan with kafka-reassign-partitions.sh or let Cruise Control compute one against goals, run it with a replication throttle, verify, then run a preferred leader election so leadership spreads too.

How to answer

State the surprising fact first, then the fix and the safety measure (throttle). Mention leadership balance, not just replica placement.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does the throttle protect? (Client traffic from being starved by copy traffic.)
  • How does tiered storage change this? (Less local data to copy, so moves are faster.)

Scenario: lag climbs on one partition during a sale

It is a big sale day. Lag for the orders topic in group inventory-service grows from near 0 to 400,000 in 20 minutes, but only on partition 11. All consumer pods look healthy. The product manager asks you to "just add more pods".

Weak answer: "Scale the deployment from 20 to 40 pods." (With 40 partitions and one stuck partition, extra pods either sit idle or trigger a rebalance that pauses everyone.)

Strong answer: "Lag on one partition means a hot key or one slow consumer, not a lack of consumers. I would check kafka-consumer-groups.sh --describe to see who owns partition 11, then look at the key distribution: probably one huge seller's SKU. Short term: make that consumer faster (batch DB writes) or temporarily route the hot key to its own topic. Longer term: salt the key for that seller (sku#0..7) and re-aggregate, or use a share group if strict per-key order is not needed. Meanwhile I confirm retention is far longer than the time-behind, so we lose nothing, and I tell the PM what we are doing and when it will catch up."

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you have caught this earlier? (Per-partition lag dashboards, key-skew metrics, load test with real key distribution.)
  • What if order per SKU matters? (Salting breaks it; serialize per key inside the consumer instead.)

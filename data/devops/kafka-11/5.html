What to monitor and alert on

Brokers publish hundreds of metrics through JMX (Java Management Extensions, the JVM's built-in metrics interface). Most teams export them to Prometheus (a time-series database that scrapes metrics over HTTP) and draw them in Grafana, with Alertmanager paging a human. Consumer lag usually comes from a separate exporter such as kafka_exporter or LinkedIn's Burrow.

SLO vs SLA: an SLO (service level objective) is the internal target you alert on, such as "99% of order events processed within 60 s". An SLA (service level agreement) is the promise to a customer, usually looser than the SLO and with a penalty if it is missed. Good alerts fire on the SLO, so you fix things before the SLA is at risk. See the glossary.

Consumer lag: what number is "bad"?

You asked this directly. The honest answer: there is no magic number. Lag is counted in messages (log-end offset minus committed offset, per partition), and "50,000 messages behind" means nothing until you turn it into time:

time behind  =  lag (messages)  /  consume rate (messages per second)

example: 50,000 messages / 2,000 msg/s  =  25 seconds behind
  • Compare with your promise. If fraud checks must happen within 5 seconds, 25 seconds is an incident. If it is a nightly analytics feed, 25 minutes is fine.
  • Alert on trend, not on a spike. Lag jumps during a deploy or a rebalance and then drains. A lag that keeps growing over 10-15 minutes means the drain is slower than the fill. Burrow works exactly this way: it judges a window of lag samples, not one point.
  • Watch the cliff. If time-behind approaches the topic's retention, the broker will delete segments your consumer has not read yet. That is silent data loss for that consumer.

Read it per partition. Here only partition 1 is far behind. That points at one slow consumer or one hot key (a single key that gets far more traffic), not at "we need more consumers". Adding consumers will not help a single hot partition.

From your world

Consumer lag is queue depth on an egress interface. A deep queue that is stable is just buffering; a queue that grows steadily means the drain rate is below the arrival rate and drops are coming - here the "drop" is retention deleting unread data. Under-replicated partitions are like a redundancy group with a standby that is not synced: you are still forwarding, but one more failure and you lose state. Where this breaks: a router queue overflows in milliseconds; Kafka's "buffer" is days of disk, so you have time to react - if you alert on the trend.

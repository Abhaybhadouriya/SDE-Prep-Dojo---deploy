The story: the giant locker warehouse

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a huge warehouse full of lockers. You rent a row of lockers and give it a name nobody else in the whole world is using, like "surbhi-toys". Every locker has a label on it, like "cars/red-car.jpg". You can put anything in a locker, but you cannot open the locker halfway and change one toy - you take the whole box out and put a new box in.

The warehouse has a guard. By default the guard lets nobody in except you. You can write rules on a card at the door: "my friend Asha may look in the cars lockers", or "nobody may come in without showing ID". If you want a friend to drop one box in, you can give them a ticket that works for 10 minutes for one locker only.

You can also tell the warehouse: "after 30 days, move old boxes to the cheap basement; after a year, throw them away". And: "every time I put a box here, send a copy to our other warehouse in another city". If a box is too big to carry, you cut it into pieces, carry each piece separately, and the warehouse glues them together.

That warehouse is Amazon S3 (Simple Storage Service), an object store. The row of lockers is a bucket, the label is a key, the box is an object. The guard's card is a bucket policy, the 10-minute ticket is a pre-signed URL, the "move to the basement" rule is a lifecycle rule, the copy to another city is replication, and cutting a big box into pieces is a multipart upload.

S3 is where almost every AWS system keeps its blobs: user uploads, logs, backups, build artifacts, Terraform state (see Terraform State) and data-lake files. The HLD pages S3-like Object Storage and Handling Large Blobs explain how such a system is built. This page is about using the real one well, safely and cheaply.

Why build it this way? (and not with direct calls)

A beginner's first design for an online shop is often a chain of direct calls: the API calls the pricing service, which calls the database, which calls the analytics service. It works on day one. It breaks on day one hundred. The picture below shows why.

What the event pipeline gives you
  • The API answers fast: one durable write, not four calls.
  • A slow or broken reader does not break ordering for customers; it only builds up lag, which Kafka stores on disk.
  • New readers (fraud checks, emails, search index) can be added later without touching the API.
  • You can replay: reset a reader's bookmark and recompute after a bug fix.
What it costs
  • The customer gets "accepted", not "done". The final result appears a little later (eventual consistency: the data becomes correct after a short delay).
  • You must handle duplicates, retries, poison messages and ordering yourself.
  • More moving parts to run and monitor: a Kafka cluster is a real system.
  • Debugging spans many programs; you need good logs and metrics.

Every one of those costs has a box in ShopStream that deals with it. That is the real lesson of this track: not "use Kafka", but "here is every problem Kafka creates, and the standard fix for each". For the general case of sync vs async, see Why Messaging?.

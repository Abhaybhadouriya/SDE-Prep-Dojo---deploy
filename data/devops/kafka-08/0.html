The story: the class picks its own monitor

Explain like I'm 5

A big class has many helpers. Each helper looks after some shelves of notebooks. Somebody has to keep the big class list: which helper looks after which shelf, who is sick today, which new shelves were added. That person is the monitor.

In the old days, the class asked a teacher from another building to keep the list. Every time something changed, a runner had to go across the yard, tell that teacher, and come back. If a new monitor was needed, the new one had to walk over and read the whole list from the other building before doing anything. Slow!

Now the class keeps the list itself. Three kids are on the monitor team. They vote for one leader. The leader writes every change in a diary, line by line, and the other two copy each line. A change counts only when at least two of the three have written it. Every helper in the class reads the diary to stay up to date. If the leader gets sick, the other two vote again in about a second, and they already have the whole diary.

In Kafka words: the helpers are brokers (servers that store partitions and serve clients). The big class list is the cluster metadata. The teacher in another building was Apache ZooKeeper, a separate coordination service Kafka depended on until version 3.x. The monitor team is the KRaft controller quorum, and the diary is a special internal log called __cluster_metadata, kept consistent with the Raft consensus algorithm. "KRaft" just means "Kafka + Raft".

Since Kafka 4.0 (March 2025) ZooKeeper is gone completely. Every 4.x cluster runs KRaft. If an interviewer asks "how does Kafka use ZooKeeper?", the 2026 answer is: "It used to. Since 4.0 it uses its own Raft-based controller quorum. Let me explain both, because many companies still run older clusters."

From your world

This is the control plane vs data plane split you know from routers. The controllers are the route processors: they decide who leads each partition (like computing the RIB). Brokers are line cards: they move the actual bytes, and they learn decisions by replicating the controller's metadata log (like line cards receiving FIB updates). Just like non-stop forwarding, brokers keep serving reads and writes for existing leaders even if the controller quorum is briefly unavailable; what stops is change (new topics, leader moves).

Where the analogy breaks: on a router the RP pushes updates. In KRaft, brokers pull the metadata log, the same way followers pull partition data.

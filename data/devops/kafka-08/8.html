Interview questions

What is KRaft and why did Kafka remove ZooKeeper?

KRaft is Kafka's built-in metadata management: a small quorum of controller nodes stores all cluster metadata (topics, partitions, leaders, ISR, configs, ACLs) in an internal log, __cluster_metadata, replicated with a Raft-style consensus protocol. Brokers replicate that log to stay current. It replaced ZooKeeper because running two distributed systems was operationally heavy, controller failover was slow (a new controller had to load everything from ZooKeeper), push-based metadata updates could leave brokers inconsistent, and all this capped the number of partitions. With KRaft, standbys already hold the metadata, failover is fast and there is one system to secure and run. ZooKeeper was removed in Kafka 4.0.

How to answer

Definition in one line, then "three problems it fixed" (ops, failover, scale), then the version fact (gone in 4.0, 3.9 is the bridge). Draw the 3-controller box with brokers fetching.

Likely follow-ups
  • How many controllers would you run and why? (3 or 5; 2N+1 for N failures.)
  • Do brokers vote? (No, they are observers.)
  • How would you migrate an old cluster?
A broker crashes. Walk me through exactly what happens in a KRaft cluster.

The broker stops heartbeating to the active controller. After broker.session.timeout.ms (9 s by default) its lease expires and the controller fences it by writing a record to the metadata log. For each partition it led, the controller picks a new leader from the ISR (or ELR) and writes partition-change records with a new leader epoch. Once committed by the controller quorum, every broker fetches those records and the new leaders start serving. Producers and consumers get "not leader or follower" errors, refresh metadata and retry. The partitions are now under-replicated until the broker comes back and catches up, or its replicas are reassigned. With acks=all and min.insync.replicas=2 on RF 3, no acknowledged data is lost.

How to answer

Name the actor at each step: broker (heartbeat), controller (fence, elect, write), quorum (commit), brokers (apply), clients (refresh and retry). Give the timeout. Close with the durability guarantee and the alert you would watch.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not fail over instantly? (You cannot tell slow from dead without a timeout; too short causes flapping.)
  • What is the leader epoch for? (Fencing stale leaders and log truncation.)
  • What if the ISR is empty? (Partition offline unless unclean election is allowed.)
Two of your three controllers are down. What still works?

The data plane keeps working for partitions whose leaders are healthy: producers can write and consumers can read, because brokers already have the metadata they need. What stops is anything that changes metadata: creating or deleting topics, config changes, and crucially leader elections. If a broker fails now, its partitions cannot get new leaders and go offline. The fix is to restore a majority as fast as possible; prevention is placing controllers in three different failure domains, or running 5.

How to answer

Split into "data plane" and "control plane" like a router. That framing is clear and shows you understand why the quorum matters.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why can't the last controller just become leader alone? (No majority; it might be missing committed data, and a partitioned pair might also elect a leader: split brain.)
Controller, group coordinator, transaction coordinator: what is the difference?

The controller (in KRaft, the active node of the controller quorum) manages cluster metadata: brokers, topics, partition leaders and ISR. The group coordinator is a regular broker, chosen per consumer group by hashing the group id onto a partition of __consumer_offsets; it runs rebalances and stores committed offsets. The transaction coordinator is likewise a broker chosen by hashing the transactional.id onto __transaction_state; it runs the transaction protocol. Spreading coordinators across brokers by hashing avoids a single hot spot.

How to answer

Three rows: who, what state, where stored. Mention that coordinators move with partition leadership, so a broker failure moves them too.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does a consumer find its coordinator? (FindCoordinator request to any broker.)
How does Raft prevent split brain, and how is Kafka's version different from textbook Raft?

A leader needs votes from a strict majority of voters, each voter votes at most once per epoch, and it votes only for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own. Any two majorities overlap, so two leaders cannot be elected in the same epoch, and a newly elected leader always has all committed entries. Anything from an older epoch is rejected. Kafka's KRaft uses the same ideas but is pull-based: followers and observers fetch from the leader (reusing Kafka's replication and log machinery) instead of the leader pushing AppendEntries. A leader that stops receiving fetches from a majority within the fetch timeout resigns.

How to answer

State the three rules (majority, one vote per term, log up-to-date check), give the overlap argument in one sentence, then the Kafka twist (pull, observers). This links nicely to consensus in HLD.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why add randomness to election timeouts? (Avoid split votes.)
  • Where else is Raft used? (etcd in Kubernetes, Consul, CockroachDB.)

Scenario: clients keep getting NOT_LEADER errors from one broker

After a rolling restart, producers that connect through broker 6 keep getting "not leader" errors and retrying, while other brokers are fine.

Weak answer: "Restart the producers."

Strong answer: "That smells like broker 6 has a stale metadata image, so it gives clients old leader information. I would run kafka-metadata-quorum.sh describe --replication and check broker 6's lag as an observer. If it is behind, look for why it cannot fetch from the active controller: controller listener reachability, TLS/SASL errors, GC pauses, or disk issues on its metadata dir. Fix the cause, and if needed restart broker 6 so it reloads the latest snapshot. Then add an alert on metadata fetch lag per broker."

Likely follow-ups
  • Which metric would you graph? (Metadata apply lag, request error rates by broker.)

60-second answer: "How does Kafka manage cluster metadata?"

"Since Kafka 4.0 there is no ZooKeeper. A small quorum of controller nodes, usually three or five, keeps all cluster metadata, topics, partitions, leaders, ISR, configs and ACLs, as an ordered log called __cluster_metadata, and they agree on it with a Raft-style protocol: one active controller is elected by majority, it appends changes, and a change is committed once a majority has it. Brokers don't vote; they continuously fetch that log and apply it, so every broker converges on the same view. Brokers heartbeat to the controller; if one misses its lease, about nine seconds by default, the controller fences it and elects new leaders from the in-sync replicas. Because standbys already have the whole log, controller failover is fast, and the design supports far more partitions than ZooKeeper did."

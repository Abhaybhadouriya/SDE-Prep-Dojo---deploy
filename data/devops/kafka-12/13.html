Interviewers love this: a comparison answer that ends in a decision, not a feature list. Use this pattern every time: "I would pick X because A. The cost is B. If C changes, I would switch to Y." For example: "I would pick SQS because these are independent jobs and nobody needs replay. The cost is no history and no second reader. If a second team needs the same events, or we need to replay a month, I would switch to Kafka (MSK)." It shows you know the trade-off and the trigger that flips it.

Say it in 60 seconds: Kafka vs RabbitMQ vs SQS

"These solve two different shapes. RabbitMQ and SQS are queues: a message is work, one consumer handles it, it is deleted after the ack. Kafka is a log: messages are kept for a retention period, each consumer group keeps its own offset, so many teams read the same data and can replay it. Inside the queue family, RabbitMQ gives rich routing, priorities and per-message delays, but you run it yourself; SQS gives zero operations and pay-per-request, with fewer features and no replay. Kafka gives the highest throughput, per-key ordering through partitions, replay and a big ecosystem (Connect, Streams, Debezium), at the cost of capacity planning and running a cluster, or paying for MSK or Confluent. So: background jobs on AWS, SQS. Complex routing at moderate volume, RabbitMQ. Event streams with several consumers, CDC or replay, Kafka. And the lines are blurring: RabbitMQ has Streams, Kafka has share groups."

Scenario: SQS + Lambda today, analytics wants 30 days of replay

Your team runs order processing on SNS + SQS + Lambda: about 300 orders per second at peak, five consumer Lambdas, and it works. A new analytics team wants every order event for the last 30 days, and wants to re-read them whenever they change their model. Your manager asks: "Do we move to Kafka?"

Weak answer: "Yes, Kafka is the industry standard and scales better, so we should migrate everything." (No requirement analysis, a risky big-bang migration of a working system, and it ignores that 300 orders/s is small.)

Strong answer: "First, what is actually new? Replay and history, which SQS cannot give: a message is gone after delete, and retention tops out at 14 days. The existing consumers do not need either. So I would not rip out SQS. Options, cheapest first: (1) Add an archive path: an SNS subscription to Firehose that writes every order to S3; analytics replays from S3 with Athena or Spark. Little new to operate, but replay is batch, not a stream. (2) If analytics wants a stream they can rewind, or more teams are coming, add a log: MSK (or Kinesis) with 30-day retention, keyed by order ID, fed from the same SNS topic or from the database through CDC. Existing Lambdas stay on SQS; new consumers read the log. (3) Only later, if the log becomes the source of truth for everything, move the Lambdas to read Kafka. I would pick option 1 if they only need daily reports, because it is the least to operate. The cost is that replay is batch. If they need minute-level replay or a third team appears, I would switch to option 2."

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you keep the archive and the queue from disagreeing? (Feed both from one source: the SNS topic, or the database through an outbox / CDC, never two writes from the app.)
  • How many partitions for 300 orders/s? (Throughput is tiny; pick by consumer parallelism and future growth, for example 12, keyed by order ID.)
  • What does 30 days of retention cost? (Average event size x rate x 30 days x replication factor; at 1 KB x 300/s that is about 0.8 TB per copy, so about 2.3 TB with RF 3, or much less with tiered storage.)
Kafka vs RabbitMQ: when would you pick each?

Kafka is a distributed, partitioned log: messages are kept for a retention period, consumers pull and track offsets, many consumer groups read the same data independently, and you can replay. RabbitMQ is a broker with queues: exchanges route messages into queues by rules, the broker pushes to consumers, and a message is deleted once acked. I pick Kafka for event streams with several consumers, replay, CDC or analytics, and very high throughput. I pick RabbitMQ for task distribution, rich routing, priorities, and moderate volume where low operational effort matters. I would also mention that RabbitMQ Streams adds a log type and Kafka share groups add queue semantics, so the line is blurring.

How to answer

Lead with the shape (log vs queue), then three differentiators (replay, routing, scaling unit), then a concrete example for each, then the modern nuance. Avoid "Kafka is faster" as your main point.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does each handle a slow consumer? (Kafka: lag grows, data waits on disk; RabbitMQ: queue grows, memory and disk pressure, flow control.)
  • How does ordering differ? (Kafka per partition; RabbitMQ per queue with a single consumer.)
Kafka vs Kinesis vs SQS for an AWS-based system?

SQS is a queue: zero ops, per-message delete, no replay, best for decoupling jobs. Kinesis is a managed log: shards with fixed limits (1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s in, 2 MB/s out per shard), retention up to 365 days, replay, good for AWS-native streaming at moderate scale. Kafka (self-run or MSK) is a log with the biggest ecosystem and the most control over retention, partitioning and tuning, at the cost of capacity planning. I would choose SQS for work items, Kinesis or MSK for event streams, and lean to MSK if we need Kafka Connect, Debezium or Kafka Streams, or if per-shard pricing gets expensive at volume, which is part of why Atlassian moved from Kinesis to Kafka.

How to answer

Split into "queue" (SQS) vs "log" (Kinesis, Kafka), then compare the two logs on ecosystem, limits and cost. Mention one real migration.

Likely follow-ups
  • How many shards for 20 MB/s of writes? (At least 20, more for headroom and hot keys.)
  • Where does SNS fit? (Fan-out to many SQS queues.)
What is the difference between a message queue and an event log, and why does it matter for design?

A queue models work: each message should be handled once by one worker and then disappears. An event log models history: facts are appended and kept, and any number of readers process them at their own pace, including readers added later. It matters because it changes how you add a new feature: with a log, a new service subscribes and backfills from history; with a queue, you must change the producer or add another queue to get a copy, and history is gone. It also changes scaling: queues scale by adding workers; logs scale by adding partitions, and the partition count caps consumers per group.

How to answer

Define both in one line each, then give two design consequences (adding consumers, scaling). The commands-vs-events framing is a bonus.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can you get queue behaviour from Kafka? (Share groups, KIP-932.)
  • Can RabbitMQ replay? (Only with Streams.)
Why might a company move from Kafka to Pulsar, or the other way round?

Towards Pulsar: a platform team serving many tenants wants built-in tenancy and quotas, geo-replication without extra tools, and elastic brokers because storage lives in BookKeeper, so scaling does not require copying partitions. Towards Kafka: a much bigger ecosystem (Connect, Streams, Debezium, managed services), a larger hiring pool, and one system to run instead of brokers plus bookies plus a metadata store; and Kafka has narrowed Pulsar's advantages with tiered storage, KRaft and share groups. Flipkart has spoken about moving parts of its async bus to Pulsar; the reverse also happens. I would decide on operational skills and tenancy needs, not hype.

How to answer

Give both directions, tie each to a concrete need, and finish with how you would decide. Never invent migration numbers.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you migrate with zero downtime? (Bridge or dual publish, shadow consumers, compare, cut over consumers, then producers.)
When would you NOT use Kafka?

When the volume is low and the team is small: a database table polled by workers, SQS or RabbitMQ costs far less to run. When the caller needs an immediate answer: use a synchronous API. When each message needs its own delay, priority or complex routing: RabbitMQ or SQS fit better. When you need strict global ordering of everything: Kafka only orders within a partition, so you would need a single partition, which kills parallelism. And when nobody can operate it: then managed Kafka, or not Kafka at all.

How to answer

Give four or five crisp cases with an alternative for each. This question tests maturity, so a calm "Kafka is not always the answer" scores well.

Likely follow-ups
  • What changes if you already run Kafka for other things? (The marginal cost drops; share groups may cover the queue case.)
SQS Standard says at-least-once and best-effort order. How do you build a reliable order pipeline on it?

Either switch to SQS FIFO with MessageGroupId = orderId so events for one order stay in sequence, accepting the lower throughput, or keep Standard and make the consumer robust: an idempotency key per event stored in a dedup table with a TTL, a version or sequence number per order so an older update never overwrites a newer one, a visibility timeout longer than the worst-case processing time (or extend it while working), and a redrive policy to a DLQ with an alarm on its depth. If several services need the same order events, I would put SNS in front so each has its own queue, or move to Kafka.

How to answer

Name the two weaknesses (duplicates, order), fix each, then add operations (DLQ, alarms).

Likely follow-ups
  • What does the visibility timeout protect against, and what goes wrong if it is too short?
Size a Kinesis stream for 50 MB/s in, read by three consumer apps. How many shards?

Do the write side and the read side separately and take the larger. Write: each shard accepts 1 MB/s or 1,000 records/s, so 50 MB/s needs at least 50 shards; if records are small (say 200 bytes) check the record limit too: 50 MB/s / 200 B = 250,000 records/s, which needs 250 shards, so the record limit wins. Read: each shard gives 2 MB/s shared by all standard consumers, so three apps reading 50 MB/s each means 150 MB/s of reads, which needs 75 shards, or use enhanced fan-out, which gives each registered consumer its own 2 MB/s per shard. Then add headroom (30-50%) for peaks and hot keys, because one hot partition key lands on one shard. Or use on-demand mode and let AWS scale it, at a higher price per GB.

How to answer

Show the arithmetic out loud: bytes limit, records limit, read limit, then headroom. Mention that Kafka has no fixed per-partition quota, which is why the same math there is about broker capacity, not a hard cap.

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens if you exceed a shard's limit? (Writes are throttled with a "throughput exceeded" error; the producer must retry with backoff.)
  • How is a shard like a Kafka partition, and how is it different? (Both are ordered, keyed units of parallelism; a shard has fixed AWS limits and you pay per shard-hour.)
Would you consider Redpanda or a "diskless" Kafka on S3 instead of Apache Kafka?

Because they speak the Kafka protocol, the clients and code stay the same, so this is a deployment decision. Redpanda (C++, thread-per-core, Raft per partition, no JVM) targets lower tail latency and simpler operations on fewer nodes; I would benchmark it on our real workload rather than trust vendor numbers, and check the licence and support model. Diskless designs (AutoMQ, WarpStream-style, or Pinterest's in-house MemQ idea) keep data in object storage, which removes broker disks and cross-zone replication traffic, which is often the biggest cloud cost, at the price of higher produce latency (object-store writes take tens to hundreds of milliseconds). So: latency-sensitive payments, stay on Kafka or Redpanda; huge log and telemetry volume where a second of delay is fine, diskless is attractive. Kafka's own tiered storage is the middle ground.

How to answer

Say "same API, so the engine is swappable", give one strength and one cost for each, and tie the choice to a latency budget and a cost driver.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which Kafka features might differ? (Exactly-once, transactions, compaction and Connect support vary by engine and version; test them.)
  • Why is cross-AZ traffic so expensive for Kafka on AWS? (Every byte is replicated to two other zones and consumers often read across zones too.)
When does SNS + SQS beat Kafka for fan-out?

When you have a handful of subscribers, moderate volume, no need for replay, and you are already on AWS. SNS copies each message into one SQS queue per subscriber, so each team gets its own queue with its own retries, visibility timeout, DLQ and scaling (Lambda or workers), with nothing to run and per-request pricing that is cheap at low volume. Subscription filter policies even let each queue receive only the event types it wants. Kafka wins when there are many consumers (copies multiply the cost with SNS + SQS, while Kafka stores the data once), when ordering per key matters at high volume, when new consumers must read history, or when you need stream processing and connectors.

How to answer

Contrast "fan-out by copying" (SNS + SQS) with "fan-out by offsets" (Kafka), then give the crossover signals: number of consumers, volume, replay, ordering.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you keep order with SNS + SQS? (SNS FIFO topics into SQS FIFO queues with a message group ID, at lower throughput.)
  • What happens to a new subscriber? (It only sees messages published after it subscribed; there is no history.)

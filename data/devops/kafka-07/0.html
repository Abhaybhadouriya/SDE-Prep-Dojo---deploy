The story: a notebook with chapters

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine the class has one giant notebook where everybody writes news. You may only write on the next empty line. You can never rub anything out. When a notebook gets full, the teacher starts a fresh notebook and puts the full one on a shelf. Each notebook has a sticker on the front saying which line number it starts at: "starts at line 0", "starts at line 1,000", "starts at line 2,000".

If a friend asks "what was written on line 1,368?", you do not read every notebook. You look at the stickers, grab the "starts at 1,000" notebook, then open its little table of contents in the back that says "line 1,300 is on page 40". You jump to page 40 and read a few lines. Fast!

Every week the teacher throws away the oldest notebooks on the shelf. And for one special notebook, "everybody's favourite colour", the teacher keeps only the newest line for each kid, because only the latest answer matters.

That is almost exactly how Kafka stores data. The notebook is a partition log. Each full notebook on the shelf is a segment file. The sticker is the segment's base offset (an offset is the line number of a message). The table of contents in the back is the index file. Throwing old notebooks away is retention. Keeping only the newest line per kid is log compaction.

The surprising part is speed. People expect "writes to disk" to mean "slow". Yet a small Kafka cluster can move hundreds of megabytes per second. On this page you will see that Kafka is fast because of how it uses the disk and the Linux kernel, not in spite of it. If you have written drivers or worked on a packet path, you already know most of the tricks. You just have not seen them used by a database-like system before.

From your world

A partition is an append-only journal, much like a filesystem journal or the kernel's dmesg ring: writers only append at the tail, and each reader keeps its own read index (the consumer's offset). Consumers are like several readers of a shared ring, each with a private read pointer.

Where the analogy breaks: a ring buffer overwrites old slots in place when it wraps. Kafka never overwrites. Old data is removed only by deleting whole segment files once they pass the retention time or size.

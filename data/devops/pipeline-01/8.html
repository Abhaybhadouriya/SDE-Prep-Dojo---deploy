Processing: exactly-once in the enricher

The enricher is the heart of the pipeline. It is a read-process-write loop: read from orders.created, compute, write to another topic. The danger in any such loop is a crash in the middle: outputs written but input position not saved (duplicates on restart), or position saved but outputs not written (lost orders). The enricher closes that gap with a Kafka transaction per batch.

One term first: read_committed is a consumer setting (isolation.level) that hides records from transactions that are still open or were aborted. Every ShopStream consumer uses it.

In one sentence: the enricher writes its outputs and "I have read up to here" in one Kafka transaction, so either both happen or neither does. That sentence is enough for most SDE-1 interviews.

Stop here for SDE-1. The stepper below and the rest of this section show the transaction step by step. It is Level 4 material that page 03 (exactly-once inside Kafka) explains slowly and fully. Skim it now, or skip to the guarantee table and come back after page 03.

  1. Consume a batch. consumer.consume(num_messages=BATCH_SIZE, timeout=1.0) returns up to 200 records (here offsets 100 to 299 of one partition).
  2. Begin. producer.begin_transaction(). The producer has a stable transactional.id (enricher-<hostname>) registered with a broker-side transaction coordinator.
  3. Produce outputs. Each record is routed to orders.enriched, orders.retry or orders.dlq. They are written to the logs now, but marked as part of an open transaction, so read_committed consumers do not see them yet.
  4. Add the input position to the transaction. send_offsets_to_transaction(consumer.position(...)) puts "this group has read up to 300" into the same transaction. The commit of the read position is now part of the write.
  5. Commit. commit_transaction(). The coordinator writes commit markers; all outputs and the new offset become visible together.
  6. Or crash. If anything fails before commit, the transaction is aborted (or times out after transaction.timeout.ms, 60 s here), the outputs stay invisible forever, and the enricher (or its replacement) re-reads from the last committed offset. No loss, no duplicates inside Kafka.

Three kinds of outcome per record

OutcomeExampleException in codeGoes to
SuccessValid order, all products knownnoneorders.enriched
Transient failurePrice lookup timed out, catalog DB unreachableTransientErrororders.retry with attempt +1 and a backoff time; after 4 retries (MAX_ATTEMPTS; 5 tries in total), orders.dlq
PoisonNot JSON, missing order_id, empty items, bad quantity, unknown productPoisonMessageorders.dlq immediately

Backoff is exponential with full jitter: a random wait between 0 and 2 s x 2^(attempt-1), capped at 60 s. With the default MAX_ATTEMPTS=4 the caps are 2, 4, 8 and 16 s, so the whole ladder waits at most about 30 s (about 15 s on average) and the 60 s cap is never reached. That is short: a database restart longer than that sends good orders to the DLQ (page 03 discusses the fix). Jitter spreads retries out so a recovering dependency is not hit by every retry at the same instant (the "thundering herd").

The retry-worker reads orders.retry. If a record's retry-at-ms is still in the future, it pauses that partition and seeks back to the record, instead of sleeping. Sleeping inside the poll loop for minutes would exceed max.poll.interval.ms (5 minutes by default) and get the worker kicked out of its group. When the time comes, it resumes, and sends the original bytes back to orders.created, again inside a transaction with its offset.

Interviewers love this. Kafka's exactly-once covers Kafka to Kafka only. It cannot cover a database write, an email or a payment call. Say this before they ask. For the Postgres hop, ShopStream uses a different technique (next section). Candidates who claim "Kafka gives us exactly-once end to end" lose points.

Batch or streaming?

Could all this be a nightly batch job? The Postgres table could be: nobody would notice orders appearing once a night in a report. But revenue per minute, low-stock alerts, fraud checks and "your order is confirmed" emails all need seconds, not hours. A useful rule: if the answer is only needed tomorrow, batch is simpler and cheaper; if someone or something acts on it within minutes, stream it. Many companies do both from the same Kafka topic: a streaming consumer for the live view and a sink to a data lake for the nightly heavy queries.

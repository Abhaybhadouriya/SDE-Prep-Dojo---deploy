The story

Explain like I'm 5

A big school has one office that tells families things. Teachers drop notes in the office tray: "Riya won the drawing prize", "School is closed tomorrow because of rain", "Bake sale on Friday". The office helper reads each note, checks how each family likes to be told (some want a phone call, some a letter, some a message on the class app), writes it nicely, and sends it. Two rules keep everyone happy. First, the "school closed" note is urgent: it jumps ahead of the 2,000 bake-sale flyers. Second, the helper keeps a list of what was already sent, so if she gets confused and picks up the same note twice, no family gets two phone calls.

A notification system is that office for an app. Other services (orders, payments, login, marketing) hand it a request like "tell user 42 that order 981 shipped". The notification system decides which channels to use, respects the user's choices (opt-outs, quiet hours), fills a template, and delivers through outside providers:

  • Push to phones: Apple Push Notification service (APNs) for iOS, Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM) for Android and web.
  • SMS through an SMS gateway (Twilio, or Indian operators' gateways; in India, commercial SMS templates must be registered on the DLT platform under TRAI rules).
  • Email through Amazon SES, SendGrid and similar.
  • In-app: the bell icon and inbox inside the app, which we store ourselves.

We do not deliver the last mile ourselves: Apple and Google own the connection to each phone. Our job is to be a reliable, fast, polite dispatcher in front of those providers.

From your world

Think of it as a router with QoS queues: traffic is classified (OTP = expedited forwarding, order updates = assured forwarding, marketing = best effort), each class gets its own queue and scheduler, and a flood of best-effort traffic must never starve the priority queue. Retries with backoff are like retransmission timers, and the dead-letter queue is the punt path / drop queue for packets that cannot be processed. Where it breaks: a router drops excess best-effort packets in microseconds; here, a "packet" is a message to a person, so we hold it for minutes or hours, retry, and track whether it was finally delivered.

Interviewers love this: "Don't let a campaign of 100 million promos delay OTPs" is the classic point. If you mention priority isolation, user preferences, dedupe and retries without being asked, you are already above average. Commonly reported at Amazon, Flipkart, Swiggy and Atlassian.

The story

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine the biggest library in the world, with a billion books and no librarian. You want every book that talks about "red pandas". Opening every book would take forever.

So, every night, a team of robots reads every book and writes a giant list at the back: "red: book 7, book 92, book 1,004 ... panda: book 92, book 300 ...". When you ask for "red panda", a helper looks at both lists and finds books that appear on both: book 92! Then the helper puts the books that other books point to most often (the "famous" ones) at the top. The list is too big for one helper, so a thousand helpers each keep the list for a thousand shelves, and all of them search at the same time.

A web search engine has three stages, as Google's own docs describe them: crawling (fetch pages and discover links), indexing (understand pages, group duplicates, pick a canonical URL, and store signals), and serving (find matching pages and rank them). Google says its index covers "hundreds of billions of webpages" and is "well over 100,000,000 gigabytes".

The gap analysis lists "Google Search engine" in three of our source lists (system design primer-style hot questions, AlgoMaster, and the checkcheckzz list). No interview report names it at a specific company, but it is the natural Level 5 follow-up to the Web Crawler design (which only covers the first stage) and it shares a core with product search at Amazon and Flipkart (see Product Catalog Search) and with Microsoft's Bing. Tags here mean "companies that run this kind of system", not reported asks.

From your world

An inverted index is a giant forwarding table built offline: the "lookup key" is a word and the "result" is a sorted list of document IDs. Building it is like compiling a FIB from a RIB: expensive, batched, then swapped in atomically. Query serving is scatter-gather across many line cards (shards), and tail latency is your old enemy, jitter, multiplied by fan-out.

New words, once each: an inverted index maps each word to the documents that contain it. QPS is queries per second. A shard is one slice of the data held by its own group of servers. Fan-out is how many servers one request is copied to. p99 means "99 out of 100 are faster than this", and tail latency is that slow end. gRPC is a fast binary RPC protocol between internal services. Cache is a fast copy of recent answers. More search words are in the box before the estimation, and the rest are in the glossary.

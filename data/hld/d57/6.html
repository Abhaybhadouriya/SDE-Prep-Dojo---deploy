Deep dives

1. Building the index

  1. Parse and render. Extract text, title, headings, links and anchor text. Google renders pages with a recent Chrome so content produced by JavaScript is seen.
  2. Deduplicate. Exact duplicates by content hash; near-duplicates by a fingerprint like SimHash. Group duplicates into a cluster and choose one canonical URL to index; the rest are alternates (Google's docs describe exactly this). Not every crawled page gets indexed.
  3. Forward index. For each document: list of (termID, positions, hit type). Brin and Page split this into 64 "barrels" by termID range.
  4. Invert. Sort by termID, then docID: a distributed sort (MapReduce: map emits (term, doc, hits), reduce writes the posting list). In 1998, sorting took about 24 hours on four machines.
  5. Attach anchor text. Text of links pointing to a page is indexed as part of that page. It describes pages better than they describe themselves and lets you index pages you never fetched. The 1998 prototype had over 259 million anchors for 24 million pages.
  6. Build shards and push. Write immutable per-shard files with a fixed docID range assignment; replicas load the new version and are switched in one by one (divert traffic, swap, rejoin).

2. Sharding: by document or by term?

Google's 2003 paper describes document partitioning: each index shard holds "a randomly chosen subset of documents", a pool of replicas serves each shard, and an intermediate load balancer picks one replica per shard. Adding replicas adds throughput; adding shards absorbs index growth. Random assignment also spreads important documents evenly, so no shard is "the shard for news". The same trade-off appears in Post Search and Elasticsearch, which also shards by document.

3. Ranking: cheap first, smart later

  • Query-independent (static) score, computed offline: PageRank, spam score, site quality. Brin and Page's PageRank: PR(A) = (1 - d) + d x sum over pages T linking to A of PR(T)/C(T), where C(T) is T's out-link count and d (damping) is usually 0.85. Think of a random surfer who follows a link with probability d and jumps to a random page otherwise. In 1998, PageRank for 26 million pages took a few hours on one workstation; today it is an iterative graph job (MapReduce or Pregel-style) over billions of nodes.
  • Query-dependent score at the leaf: term frequency and inverse document frequency (TF-IDF / BM25), whether terms appear in title or anchor text, proximity of the terms. Brin and Page note they weight proximity heavily.
  • Two-phase ranking: leaves score millions of matches cheaply and each returns its top ~10-20 with scores; intermediate mixers (each covering ~20-30 leaves) merge and keep the best; the root takes the merged top few hundred and applies the expensive ML re-ranker. Do the arithmetic: if 600 leaves each sent 50, the root would face 30,000 candidates, which is why the mixer tier and a small per-leaf k matter. Google's docs say ranking uses "hundreds of factors" including location, language and device.
  • Early termination: order each posting list by static rank inside tiers, so a leaf can stop after finding enough high-quality matches.

4. Tail latency at 600-way fan-out

Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" is the must-quote source. If each server is fast (10 ms) but has a 1-second 99th percentile, a request touching 100 such servers is slow 63% of the time. In a real Google service, the 99th-percentile latency for one leaf was 10 ms, but for all leaves to finish it was 140 ms, and the slowest 5% of leaves accounted for half of that. Techniques:

TechniqueIdeaPublished result
Hedged requestsIf a shard has not replied after the ~95th-percentile latency, send the same request to another replica; use whichever answers firstExtra load limited to about 5%; a BigTable benchmark cut 99.9th-percentile from 1,800 ms to 74 ms with 2% more requests
Tied requestsEnqueue on two replicas; the first to start tells the other to cancelReduces both median and tail in Google's file system
Micro-partitionsMany more partitions than machines (say 20 per machine)Shed load in ~5% steps; faster recovery after failures
Selective replicationMore replicas for hot or important shards/documentsAbsorbs skew
Latency-induced probationTemporarily stop sending to a slow replica, keep probing itRemoves interference victims
Good-enough resultsReturn when most shards have repliedChance a given leaf holds the best result is under 1 in 1,000 queries
Canary requestsSend to one or two leaves first; fan out only if they surviveStops a query-of-death from crashing thousands of servers

5. Freshness: batch, incremental and tiers

  • Batch rebuild (MapReduce over the repository): simple and efficient, but a page waits for the next full cycle.
  • Incremental indexing: Google's Percolator (OSDI 2010) processes changes as they arrive with transactions and notifications over a Bigtable-like store. Google reported the same documents per day with average document age in results cut by 50%, on tens of petabytes and billions of updates per day.
  • Tiered serving: a small real-time tier (minutes old, fed by a stream), a main tier (the bulk), and maybe a cold tier for the long tail. The root queries tiers in order of value and merges.
  • Crawl scheduling: Google's crawler learns how often a page changes and revisits accordingly; news sites get minutes, static pages weeks. Politeness limits per host and backs off on server errors. Details in Web Crawler.

Trap: do not propose updating posting lists in place on serving machines. Immutable segments plus a small fresh tier (or periodic merges, like Lucene/Elasticsearch segments) keep reads lock-free and cache-friendly.

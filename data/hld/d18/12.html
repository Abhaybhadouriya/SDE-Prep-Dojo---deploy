Quick quiz

A consumer reads a message from a Kafka topic. What happens to the message?

  1. It is deleted once the offset is committed
  2. It moves to an internal "consumed" topic
  3. Nothing; retention or compaction removes it later
  4. It is hidden from every other consumer group
Reading only moves that group's offset; the record stays until retention or compaction. Kafka does not delete on read or on commit, a very common misconception. There is no "consumed" topic. Other groups have their own offsets and still see it.

Which offset may consumers read up to?

  1. The high watermark
  2. The leader's log-end offset
  3. The fastest follower's log-end offset
Only records below the HW are on every ISR member and safe from truncation after a leader change. The leader's LEO includes records not yet replicated. A follower's LEO is only that follower's view and may be ahead of the others.

RF 3, min.insync.replicas 2, acks=all. Two brokers holding a partition die. What happens to writes?

  1. They succeed on the one remaining replica
  2. They fail with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS
  3. They are buffered by the KRaft controller
  4. They go to another partition of the topic
Kafka refuses writes it cannot replicate to min ISR, choosing durability over availability. Writing to one replica is what min ISR forbids. Controllers hold metadata, never data. The producer chose the partition; Kafka never redirects to another one.

In our estimate, what decided the number of brokers?

  1. CPU for compression
  2. Network ingress and egress
  3. The number of topics
  4. Disk for retention x RF
About 5 brokers by network but about 20 by disk; tiered storage changes that. Compression runs mostly on producers. Network was the smaller number in our math. Topic count affects metadata, not broker count at this scale.

Why can Kafka use sendfile() zero-copy for plain-text consumers?

  1. The broker runs inside the kernel
  2. On-disk batches equal the wire format
  3. Kafka sends records over UDP
The broker forwards stored bytes unchanged, so the kernel can copy page cache to socket directly. Kafka is a normal JVM user-space process. It uses TCP. With TLS (unless kernel TLS is used) it must encrypt in user space and zero-copy is lost.

Where are a consumer group's committed offsets stored?

  1. In the compacted topic __consumer_offsets
  2. On each consumer's local disk
  3. In the KRaft metadata log
  4. In a header on each record
It is keyed by (group, topic, partition), and compaction keeps the latest offset per key. Local disk would be lost with the pod. The metadata log holds topics, partitions and leaders, not group offsets. Records are immutable, so offsets cannot live in them.

A group has 12 consumers on a topic with 8 partitions. What happens?

  1. Each partition is split between 1.5 consumers
  2. The extra 4 consumers sit idle
  3. Kafka adds 4 partitions automatically
  4. All 12 share every partition round-robin
In a classic group each partition has exactly one owner, so 4 consumers get nothing. Kafka never splits a partition or adds partitions by itself. Round-robin sharing of a partition is what share groups do, not classic groups.

What does exactly-once in Kafka NOT cover?

  1. Writes to several partitions in one transaction
  2. Committing offsets atomically with output
  3. Sending an email from the consumer
Transactions cover Kafka-to-Kafka work, including offsets, but an email or a database write outside Kafka needs idempotency keys, upserts or the outbox pattern. Multi-partition writes and atomic offset commits are exactly what transactions provide.

What do share groups (KIP-932) add?

  1. Many consumers per partition, per-record acks
  2. Global ordering across all partitions
  3. Bring back ZooKeeper for old clusters
  4. Exactly-once delivery to outside systems
Queue semantics on Kafka, production-ready in 4.2, at the cost of per-partition ordering. They make ordering weaker, not global. ZooKeeper was removed in 4.0. Records can still be redelivered, so outside systems still need idempotency.

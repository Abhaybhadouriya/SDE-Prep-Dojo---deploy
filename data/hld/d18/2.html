Back-of-envelope estimation

Learner question answered: "What does scale actually mean in numbers?" Watch which resource ends up deciding the cluster size; it is not the one most people guess.

  1. Ingress. 1,000,000 msg/s x 1 KB = 1 GB/s in from producers.
  2. Replication. Replication factor (RF) 3 means each message is copied to 2 followers: another 2 GB/s of broker-to-broker traffic, and 3 GB/s of total disk writes.
  3. Egress. 3 consumer groups x 1 GB/s = 3 GB/s to consumers. Total broker egress = 3 + 2 (followers fetching) = 5 GB/s.
  4. Storage. 1 GB/s x 86,400 s = about 86 TB per day. 7 days = about 605 TB. x RF 3 = about 1.8 PB uncompressed. With producer-side compression (lz4/zstd), assume about 3x for typical JSON (assumption; measure your data), so about 600 TB on disk.
  5. Brokers by network. Assume 25 Gbit/s NICs (about 3 GB/s) and plan to use at most about 40% (failover headroom): about 1.2 GB/s per broker. 5 GB/s egress / 1.2 is about 5 brokers (fewer with compression).
  6. Brokers by disk. Assume about 30 TB usable per broker (assumption, for example 12 x 4 TB drives at 60-70% fill). 600 TB / 30 TB = about 20 brokers. Storage, not throughput, decides the cluster size. That is exactly why tiered storage (KIP-405; a KIP is a Kafka Improvement Proposal, the numbered design document for each change) exists: keep 1 day locally and move older segments to object storage, and local disk drops about 7x.
  7. Partitions. Partitions are the unit of parallelism. If one consumer instance can process about 5 MB/s (assumption; depends on your processing), a group needs 1 GB/s / 5 MB/s = 200 partitions to keep up. Pick a little more (for example 240-256) because adding partitions later changes which partition a key maps to.
  8. Page cache. Consumers usually read the last few seconds or minutes of data. One minute of traffic is 60 GB across the cluster, a few GB per broker, so tailing reads are served from RAM, not disk.
  9. Offsets. Offsets are 64-bit. Even at 1 million messages per second into a single partition, it would take about 290,000 years to overflow. Never a concern; say it in one sentence if asked.

Real numbers to anchor on (always say the year): Kafka's own operations docs describe one of LinkedIn's busiest reference clusters at peak as 60 brokers, 50,000 partitions (RF 2), 800,000 messages/s in, 300 MB/s in and over 1 GB/s out. LinkedIn wrote in 2019 that it ran over 100 clusters and more than 4,000 brokers handling 7 trillion messages a day. In 2014 Jay Kreps benchmarked 2 million writes per second on three cheap machines.

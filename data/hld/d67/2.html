Back-of-envelope estimation

Why this section matters here. In one October 2026 Amazon SDE2 report of this exact question (post), the candidate said they fumbled capacity estimation and rated their own HLD as No Hire. The fix is not memorising numbers: it is writing assumptions on the board, rounding hard, and ending every line with a conclusion ("so one Postgres primary per state is enough").

Real anchor numbers (as reported in 2021)

  • When registration opened for the 18-44 age group on 28 April 2021, more than 80 lakh (8 million) people registered in the first three hours, and the government reported about 55,000 hits per second on the platform (Business Today, Business Standard). Our design assumes about 1M availability reads per second, about 20x that peak, which is why the read path goes through a CDN and a cache tier rather than the database.
  • Before that, the National Health Authority said load tests reached about 10,000 transactions per second while live traffic was well below that (Deccan Herald).

Use these as sanity checks. In the interview, build your own numbers from assumptions like the ones below.

CoWIN: inventory and bookings

  1. Slot rows. Assume 100,000 centres, slots published 15 days ahead, 16 half-hour slots per day. 100,000 x 15 x 16 = 24 million slot rows. At about 100 bytes each: about 2.4 GB. Conclusion: the whole live inventory fits in memory on one database server; we shard for write throughput and blast radius, not for size.
  2. Bookings per day. Assume 10 million doses a day at the peak of the drive (1 crore). Each booking covers 1.5 people on average: about 7 million bookings a day. Divide by 100,000 seconds (a day is 86,400; round up): about 70 bookings per second on average.
  3. Peak bookings. Bookings cluster when slots open (say 4 pm). Assume 30% of a day's bookings land in the first 10 minutes after release: 2.1 million / 600 s = about 3,500 bookings per second, each at most a couple of row writes. Conclusion: one Postgres primary can do a few thousand small transactions a second on good hardware, but all of them would hit the same few hot rows (popular centres). So: shard by district or state, and put a queue in front of the hot ones.
  4. Search and availability reads. Assume 5 million people are online at release time and each app refreshes every 5 seconds: 5,000,000 / 5 = 1 million reads per second. Conclusion: reads must never reach the booking database. They come from a cache (CDN for the district-level JSON, Redis for per-centre counts), refreshed every 1-5 seconds.
  5. Holds. Many holds never convert (payment abandoned, user picked another centre). Assume 2 holds per booking: about 7,000 hold writes a second at peak. Holds are short-lived, so their storage is tiny, but they are the main write load.
  6. Storage over time. 7 million bookings a day x 500 bytes (booking + people + audit) = 3.5 GB a day, about 1.3 TB a year. Conclusion: keep 30-60 days hot in Postgres, archive older bookings to object storage (S3 + Parquet) for reports and certificates.

Adventure park: tiny supply, big crowd

  1. Supply. Assume 20 parks x 15 rides x 40 slots a day x 8 seats = 96,000 seats a day. That is all there is to sell.
  2. Demand. The prompt said 1M users per minute and 10K concurrent bookers. 1,000,000 / 60 = about 17,000 requests per second.
  3. Contention. If 10,000 concurrent users target the 10 most popular ride slots, that is 10,000 people for 80 seats: 99% of them must lose. Conclusion: the problem is not throughput (17K reads/s is easy for a cache) but contention on a handful of counters. You need a fast, fair "no" (a queue or an admission gate), not a bigger database.

Say the conclusions, not the arithmetic. "Inventory is gigabytes, so it is a correctness problem, not a storage problem. Reads are a million per second, so they live in caches. Writes are thousands per second on a few hot rows, so I shard by region and add a queue or admission gate for the hottest slots."

The story

Explain like I'm 5

The doctor in your town gives flu shots on Saturday. She can do 10 shots every half hour. So on her door there is a sheet of paper: 9:00 has 10 boxes, 9:30 has 10 boxes, and so on. When your mum wants a shot for you, she writes your name in an empty box. When all 10 boxes at 9:00 are full, nobody else can write there. They have to pick 9:30.

Now the whole country wants shots, from thousands of doctors, and everyone is using phones at the same moment. We cannot have one paper sheet. We need a computer sheet that never lets two people write in the same last box, that lets you "pencil in" a box for five minutes while you pay, and that rubs the pencil out if you walk away.

The paper sheet is the slot inventory: for each place (a vaccine centre, a ride, a service bay, a meeting room) and each time window, a capacity (10 shots, 8 seats, 3 bays, 1 room). Writing in pencil is a hold: a temporary claim with a TTL (time to live). Writing in pen is a confirmed booking. The rule that matters more than anything else: never confirm more bookings for a slot than its capacity.

This page is a sibling of Ticket Booking (BookMyShow). The big difference: a cinema seat is a named thing (F7), so you lock that one row. A slot is a counter ("7 of 10 taken"), so you protect a number. Meeting rooms add a third flavour: a time range that must not overlap another range.

One design, four costumes. Interviewers ask the same core problem in different clothes:

  • CoWIN (India's COVID-19 vaccine platform): nationwide, location search, huge bursts when new slots open, optional payment at private centres.
  • Adventure-park ride slots: tiny capacity (8 per ride slot), enormous demand (1M users per minute in one Amazon prompt), so contention is extreme.
  • Car service centre: search nearby centres, capacity = bays x hours, a booking may need several consecutive slots.
  • Meeting / conference room or venue: capacity is 1, the "slot" is any time range, overlap is the enemy, and time zones bite.

New words, once each:

  • Slot = (resource, start time, end time, capacity). Resource = the thing being booked: a centre's vaccine line, a ride, a service bay, a room.
  • Optimistic locking = do not lock; at write time check that nothing changed (a version number or a condition in the WHERE clause), and retry if it did. Pessimistic locking = lock the row first (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE), then work, so others wait.
  • Webhook = the payment provider calls our server when the payment finishes. Saga = a multi-step business process where each step has an "undo" (compensation) instead of one big distributed transaction.
  • Elasticsearch = a search engine. Its geo_point field and geo_distance query answer "centres within 10 km of me".
  • Idempotent = doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once.

From your world

A slot hold with a TTL is a DHCP lease. The server hands out an address (a seat in a slot) for a fixed time; if the client does not renew or confirm, the lease expires and the address goes back to the pool. Our sweeper is the lease-expiry timer, and "confirm after payment" is the client's DHCPREQUEST turning an offer into a binding. ARP-entry ageing is the same idea: entries that are not refreshed age out. The capacity counter is a counting semaphore with a timeout: booked < capacity is the semaphore value, a hold is down(), an expiry or cancel is up(), and the conditional UPDATE is the atomic test-and-decrement, so two requests can never both take the last seat. Where the analogy breaks: a lease server owns its whole pool in one process, but our counters live in a shared database, so the atomic step must happen in that database, not in an app server's memory.

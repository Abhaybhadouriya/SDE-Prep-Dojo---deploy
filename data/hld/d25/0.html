The story

Explain like I'm 5

Your class has a big chore chart. "Water the plants at 9 o'clock." "Feed the fish at 12." "Clean the board every Friday." The teacher has an alarm clock. When it rings, she looks at the chart, finds the chores that are due, and hands each chore card to a helper kid.

Sometimes a helper falls asleep halfway through watering the plants. So every helper has to wave at the teacher every minute ("I'm still working!"). If a helper stops waving, the teacher gives that chore card to another kid. And because the plants might get watered twice, the chore card says: "If the soil is already wet, don't water again."

That is the whole design. The chore chart is a database of jobs (a piece of work plus when to run it). The alarm clock and teacher are the scheduler, which finds jobs that are due. The helper kids are workers (processes that run the job). The waving is a heartbeat, and the "teacher can take the card back" rule is a lease (permission to work on something that expires unless you renew it). The "don't water twice" rule is called idempotency: running the job twice gives the same result as running it once.

Real examples are everywhere: send a reminder email 24 hours before a flight, charge a subscription on the 1st of every month, rebuild a search index every night at 2 a.m., retry a failed payment in 10 minutes, delete a user's data 30 days after they close the account. Interviewers ask this design at Amazon, Microsoft, Flipkart and Razorpay (candidates have reported "design a scheduler service" and "a task scheduler assigning work to worker threads").

From your world

You already know this design from the kernel. The Linux timer wheel (timer_list, add_timer()) fires callbacks when a deadline passes; a hardware watchdog reboots a stuck process if it stops petting the dog; BFD hello timers declare a neighbour dead after N missed hellos. A distributed scheduler is a timer wheel whose callbacks run on other machines, plus a watchdog for each running callback. Where this breaks: in a kernel, one clock and one memory are the truth. Here, clocks drift between machines and a "dead" worker might only be slow, so you can never be 100% sure it stopped. That one fact drives the hardest part of the design.

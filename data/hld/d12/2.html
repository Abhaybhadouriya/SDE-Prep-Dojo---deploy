Back-of-envelope estimation

Inputs are assumptions. A useful sanity check: Netflix said in 2016 that Open Connect delivered over 125 million hours of viewing per day, at "tens of terabits per second" of peak traffic. Our made-up numbers should land in that kind of range.

  1. Viewing per day. Assume 100M daily viewers x 2 hours = 200M hours/day.
  2. Peak concurrent streams. Evening prime time; assume 15% of daily viewers watch at the same moment: 15M concurrent streams.
  3. Average bitrate. Mix of phones and TVs; assume 5 Mbps average.
  4. Peak egress. 15M x 5 Mbps = 75,000,000 Mbps = 75 Tbps. That matches "tens of terabits per second".
  5. Can the cloud serve it? 75 Tbps out of a handful of cloud regions would mean enormous transit links and bills, and every byte crossing the internet's busiest paths at prime time. Conclusion: serve from the edge, inside or next to ISPs.
  6. How many boxes? Netflix has written about serving 100 Gbps from a single OCA (2017, a flash appliance). Netflix engineers later presented 400 Gb/s (2021) and then 800 Gb/s (2022) from one server. Using the conservative 2017 figure: 75,000 Gbps / 100 Gbps = 750 boxes at full speed. Running at about 50% for headroom and with many smaller or storage-heavy boxes, think thousands of appliances across about a thousand locations.
  7. Storage per title. A 2-hour film; assume the ladder per codec family sums to about 30 Mbps across all renditions, and 3 codec families: 90 Mbps x 7,200 s = 648,000 Mb = 81 GB, plus audio tracks and subtitles, call it about 85 GB per title.
  8. Whole catalogue. Assume the equivalent of 10,000 two-hour titles: 10,000 x 85 GB = about 850 TB. One OCA cannot hold all of it, so each box holds the subset its region is likely to watch. Viewing is long-tailed (a small share of titles gets most views, a rule of thumb), which makes this work.
  9. Control-plane traffic. 200M hours / 1-hour sessions = 200M play starts/day = about 2,300/s, maybe 7,000/s at peak. Browsing: about 30 API calls per session = 6B/day = 70,000/s average, 200,000/s peak. Big, but normal for a microservice fleet with caching.
  10. Telemetry. One heartbeat every 30 s per stream (assumption): 15M / 30 = 500,000 events/s at peak, into Kafka.

What the numbers decided: the data plane (bytes) is roughly a million times heavier than the control plane (API calls). So we design them separately: the control plane as cloud microservices with caches; the data plane as edge caches filled ahead of time.

Deep dives: the hard parts

1. Why build your own CDN, and why push instead of pull

A normal pull CDN waits for the first viewer to ask, misses, fetches from the origin, then caches. That is fine for web pages. For video at Netflix's scale it is wasteful: the first evening request for a new episode would trigger misses at thousands of sites at prime time, exactly when links are busiest. Netflix knows its catalogue and can predict demand, so Open Connect pushes (pre-positions) content in a fill window during off-peak hours, based on predicted popularity per region.

  • Two kinds of boxes: large storage-heavy appliances hold a broad catalogue; flash-heavy ones hold the most popular files and serve very high throughput.
  • Tiered fill: boxes at internet exchange points (IXPs) fill from S3; boxes inside ISPs can fill from nearby peers, saving the ISP's upstream links.
  • Business model: embedded OCAs are given at no charge to qualifying ISPs, and Netflix also peers with ISPs settlement-free at IXPs (Open Connect site). The ISP saves upstream capacity; Netflix gets bytes close to viewers. Netflix said in 2016 that close to 90% of its traffic went over direct connections between Open Connect and residential ISPs.
  • Long tail: a rare title may not be on the local box. Steering then points the player at a box further away that has it. The fill algorithm balances "store what is popular here" against "keep enough variety".

2. Steering: which box should this viewer use?

Open Connect's documentation says ISP partners set up BGP sessions with their embedded OCAs to steer traffic. In plain words: the ISP tells the OCA, through BGP, "these customer IP prefixes are mine and you may serve them". The control plane collects that, plus each box's health, load and file list. When a player starts, steering maps the client's IP to its prefix, finds boxes that advertise it and hold the files, ranks them by proximity and load, and returns a list. The player tries the first and falls back down the list.

From your world

You already know this machinery. It is BGP prefix advertisement used as an application-level routing table: the OCA learns "which customers are behind me" exactly the way a router learns reachability, and steering does something like a longest-prefix match of the client IP against OCA-advertised prefixes. Choosing among several matching boxes by load is like weighted ECMP. Where it breaks: a router forwards each packet by the FIB; here the decision is made once per playback, in the control plane, and carried in URLs.

From your world: "Is a CDN like anycast routing?"

Some CDNs are. Anycast means the same IP address is announced over BGP from many sites (PoPs, points of presence), and normal BGP path selection sends each user to the "nearest" one. Cloudflare and Google's front ends work this way: the routing table does the steering, no extra service needed. Netflix chose the other style: unicast. Every OCA has its own address, and the steering service picks boxes and hands the player their URLs, using the BGP prefixes it learned from the ISP plus each box's health, load and file list. Where this breaks: anycast cannot pin a long session. If routes flap halfway through a 2-hour film, the next TCP connection may land on a different PoP that does not have the file. Anycast also cannot see that a box is full or missing a title, because BGP only knows reachability. Netflix's steering knows both, at the cost of running a control-plane service.

3. Adaptive bitrate (ABR) in the player

The video is cut into segments of a few seconds. Each segment exists at every bitrate on the ladder, aligned on the same time boundaries, so the player can switch between bitrates at any segment edge without a glitch. Before each download, the player decides which bitrate to fetch:

  • Throughput-based ABR picks the highest bitrate below recent measured speed (with a safety margin). Buffer-based ABR picks by how many seconds are buffered: a full buffer allows risk, a thin buffer forces caution. Real players blend both.
  • Per-title encoding: Netflix's per-title optimisation picks a ladder for each title by its complexity. A simple cartoon looks fine at a low bitrate; a grainy action film needs more. The same "1080p" can be very different sizes.
  • Quality metric: Netflix's open-source VMAF scores perceived quality so the ladder can be judged by what eyes see, not just by bitrate.

From your world

ABR is congestion control at the application layer. The player's buffer is a playout buffer, like a jitter buffer in VoIP; the bitrate choice is like a sender choosing its rate from measured RTT and loss. Switching down early is the same instinct as backing off before a queue overflows. Where it breaks: TCP underneath is already doing its own congestion control; ABR sits on top and chooses how much data to ask for, not how fast to send packets.

4. The encoding pipeline

  • Chunked, parallel encoding. Netflix's Cosmos platform splits a title into chunks (by time or by scene) and encodes them in parallel, then assembles them (InfoQ coverage of Cosmos). A 2-hour film becomes hundreds of small jobs that finish in a fraction of the serial time.
  • Idempotent, retryable jobs. Each chunk-rendition is a job with a deterministic output path. If a worker dies, the job is re-run and overwrites the same file. A workflow engine tracks the DAG: inspect, split, encode chunks, assemble, quality check, package, encrypt, publish to the asset catalogue.
  • Many outputs per title: several codecs (AVC for old devices, newer codecs such as VP9, HEVC and AV1 for efficiency), HDR variants, many audio languages, subtitles.
  • Publish then fill: when the asset catalogue says a title is ready, the fill system schedules it into the next fill windows, ahead of its launch date.

5. Resilience of the control plane

  • Keep the critical path short. "Play" depends only on auth, playback, steering and DRM. Recommendations, artwork and "continue watching" are nice-to-have and have fallbacks (a non-personalised popular row if recommendations fail).
  • Circuit breakers and timeouts around every dependency. Netflix's Hystrix library made this famous; it is in maintenance mode now and Resilience4j is the usual modern equivalent (see Microservices & Resilience).
  • Multi-region active-active with the ability to evacuate a region: shift all traffic to the others when one has trouble. The "Mastering Chaos" talk describes this along with EVCache and Chaos Monkey.
  • Chaos engineering: deliberately kill instances (Chaos Monkey) in production, so failure handling is exercised every day, not discovered during an outage.

6. The OCA box itself (your home turf)

Netflix's post on serving 100 Gbps from one Open Connect Appliance describes a FreeBSD server where most of the work is kernel work: files go from the page cache to the socket with sendfile (no copy through user space), TLS encryption is done in the kernel (kTLS) so sendfile still works with HTTPS, and memory and NIC placement are tuned for NUMA so data does not cross sockets needlessly.

From your world

This is the zero-copy data path you know from drivers and NICs: avoid copies, keep data on the local NUMA node, let DMA do the work, offload crypto. It is also why Kafka is fast (see Kafka Storage Internals). If an interviewer asks "how would you make one cache box faster?", this is a strong, honest answer from your background. Where it breaks: the hard part of the whole system is not one box but deciding what thousands of boxes store and who goes where.

Interview: how to drive 45 minutes

  1. 0-5: scope. On-demand streaming (not live), browse + play + resume, content ingestion. Name the non-functionals: fast start, no rebuffering, huge egress.
  2. 5-10: numbers. 15M concurrent x 5 Mbps = 75 Tbps. Say "so bytes must come from the edge".
  3. 10-14: two planes. Draw the control plane vs data plane split first. It organises everything else.
  4. 14-18: API + data. playback/start returning manifest + OCA list + licence; bookmarks keyed by profile+title.
  5. 18-28: play flow. The stepper above. Then the offline pipeline: chunk, encode, package, S3, fill.
  6. 28-40: deep dives. Pick two: push fill vs pull CDN, steering, ABR, or resilience.
  7. 40-45: failures + monitoring. Rebuffer rate, start time, bitrate per ISP/OCA, OCA health; region evacuation.
Why not just stream the video from your servers in the cloud?

Because of the numbers. At around 15M concurrent streams and 5 Mbps each, peak egress is about 75 Tbps. Serving that from a few cloud regions would cost a fortune in bandwidth and push every byte across congested long-haul and transit links at prime time, hurting start time and quality. Putting caches inside or next to ISPs makes the path short, cheap and less congested. The cloud stays the brain: auth, catalogue, recommendations and steering.

How to answer

Lead with the estimate, then the control/data plane split. Numbers make the argument for you.

Likely follow-ups
  • Would you build your own CDN at a startup? (No, use a commercial CDN.)
Push CDN or pull CDN for Netflix? Justify it.

Push. The catalogue is curated and finite, releases are scheduled, and demand is predictable from viewing history. Pre-filling during off-peak hours means prime-time requests are hits and fill traffic uses idle capacity. A pull CDN would take its misses at the worst time and on many sites at once for a new hit episode. For YouTube-style user content with a huge, unpredictable long tail, pull (with push for known-popular videos) makes more sense.

How to answer

Use the pattern "I pick X because A; the cost is B; if C, switch to Y". Cost: storage on every box and a prediction system.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the prediction is wrong for a surprise hit? (Steer to boxes that have it, emergency fill, peer fill.)
How does the player decide which quality to download?

Adaptive bitrate streaming. The title is encoded into a ladder of renditions, each cut into aligned segments of a few seconds and listed in a manifest. Before each segment, the player estimates throughput and checks its buffer, then picks the highest bitrate it can sustain with a safety margin. It starts low for a fast start, climbs as the buffer grows, and steps down before the buffer empties. Switching happens at segment boundaries, so there is no glitch.

How to answer

Mention both throughput-based and buffer-based logic. Draw the step chart if you have a whiteboard.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why are segments not 1 second or 60 seconds long? (Too short: request overhead and worse compression; too long: slow adaptation and start.)
  • What is per-title encoding?
How do you pick which cache server a viewer should use?

A steering service in the control plane. It knows, for each OCA, which client IP prefixes it serves (ISPs advertise them to their embedded OCAs over BGP), its health and load, and which files it holds. At playback start it maps the client IP to a prefix, finds candidate boxes that hold the title, ranks them by proximity and load, and returns several URLs. The player uses the first and falls back down the list without another API call.

How to answer

Contrast with DNS-based CDN steering (resolver location can be wrong). The control plane deciding per session with a list is more precise.

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens if a box gets overloaded mid-evening?
How would you encode a 2-hour 4K film into 30 renditions quickly?

Split it into chunks (by time or at scene changes), and encode each chunk-rendition as an independent job on a pool of workers, then concatenate. With hundreds of workers, wall-clock time drops from many hours to minutes. Jobs are idempotent (deterministic output path, overwrite on retry) and tracked by a workflow engine as a DAG: split, encode, assemble, quality check (VMAF), package, encrypt, publish. On Kubernetes, an Indexed Job with completions = chunks and a chosen parallelism maps naturally.

How to answer

Say "embarrassingly parallel" and "idempotent retries". Mention chunk boundaries must align across renditions for ABR switching.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you handle a chunk that keeps failing? (Retry limit, then alert; do not publish the title.)
How does "continue watching" work across devices?

The player sends a heartbeat with its position every few tens of seconds and on pause or stop. Heartbeats go through Kafka, keyed by playback or profile id, to a consumer that writes the latest position to a wide-column store keyed by (profile_id, title_id). Writes are "last writer by timestamp wins", so duplicates or out-of-order events are harmless. When another device starts the title, playback/start reads that row and returns resume_at.

How to answer

Show the write volume estimate (hundreds of thousands per second) to justify a NoSQL store and async writes.

Likely follow-ups
  • Two devices playing the same profile at once?
An entire cloud region has problems at prime time. What happens to viewers?

Viewers who are already playing mostly notice nothing: their bytes come from OCAs, and the player has buffer and fallback URLs. New requests (browse, play starts) fail in that region until traffic is shifted. With multi-region active-active, the team (or automation) evacuates the region by moving DNS or load-balancer traffic to the healthy regions, which must have spare capacity reserved for exactly this. State such as bookmarks is replicated across regions, eventually consistent.

How to answer

Separate the data plane (unaffected) from the control plane (failover). Mention capacity planning for evacuation.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you test evacuation? (Regular planned drills; chaos engineering.)
How do you keep the "Play" path working when recommendations or other services fail?

Keep the critical path minimal (auth, playback, steering, DRM) and treat everything else as optional with fallbacks: cached or popular rows instead of personalised ones, default artwork, empty "continue watching". Wrap each dependency in timeouts and circuit breakers so a slow service fails fast instead of tying up threads. Use bulkheads (separate thread or connection pools) so one bad dependency cannot starve the others. Practise with chaos experiments.

How to answer

Name graceful degradation, circuit breaker, bulkhead and fallback. Mention Hystrix is in maintenance mode and Resilience4j is the modern equivalent.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you choose timeout values?
How would you make one cache server push as many bytes as possible?

Avoid copies and context switches: sendfile from the page cache to the socket, with TLS done in the kernel (kTLS) so zero-copy still works over HTTPS. Keep the hot set in RAM or fast NVMe, pin NIC queues, memory and storage to the same NUMA node, use large send offloads and many NIC queues, and avoid user-space work per byte. Netflix has written about serving 100 Gbps from one appliance with this kind of FreeBSD tuning.

How to answer

This is your chance to use your kernel background. Keep it brief, then zoom back out to the system.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where is the bottleneck: disk, memory bandwidth, NIC or CPU?
A new season of a huge show drops tonight at 12:30. What do you prepare?

Encode and QC well before launch. Fill it into OCAs in the fill windows of the preceding nights, everywhere demand is predicted, with extra copies on flash boxes. Pre-warm control-plane caches (metadata, artwork) and scale API services ahead of time instead of relying on autoscaling at 12:30. Watch dashboards for start time and rebuffer rate per ISP, and be ready to steer load between boxes. Keep a launch-day runbook and an on-call room.

How to answer

Think in time: days before, the night before, the minute of launch, the hour after.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if one ISP's boxes did not get the fill?

Scenario: rebuffering spikes for one ISP every evening

Quality dashboards show rebuffer rate tripling for viewers on one ISP between 8 and 11 pm; everyone else is fine.

Weak answer: "Add more servers in the cloud."

Strong answer: the cloud is not on the byte path, so look at the data plane for that ISP. Split the telemetry by OCA and by path: are sessions served from the embedded boxes or spilling to an IXP box over a congested peering link? Check those boxes' throughput, cache-hit rate and health. Likely causes: embedded boxes at capacity, missing titles after a failed fill, or a congested interconnect. Fixes: steer some sessions to other boxes, fix the fill, work with the ISP to add appliances or interconnect capacity. ABR will hide some of it as lower bitrate, so also watch average bitrate, not only rebuffers.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which metrics would you alert on, and at what level of aggregation?

Common mistakes: drawing video bytes flowing through the API servers; using one giant video file instead of segments; forgetting the bitrate ladder and ABR; designing a pull CDN without discussing misses at peak; storing bookmarks synchronously in SQL on every heartbeat; ignoring DRM; skipping failure of the edge box; spending 20 minutes on recommendations (a different design: Recommendation System).

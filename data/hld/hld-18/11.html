Interview questions

How does a reCAPTCHA Enterprise-style system tell bots from humans without showing a puzzle?

It runs a script that collects signals passively while the user browses: device and browser environment, consistency between claimed and real browser, interaction behaviour, and network context. On a sensitive action it issues a token bound to that action. The site's backend sends the token to the risk service, which combines those signals with what it sees across many sites and returns a score (0.0 to 1.0, higher is safer) plus reason codes. The site applies its own policy per action: allow, add friction like OTP, or block. Puzzles become a fallback for uncertain cases rather than a gate for everyone, which keeps friction low for real users.

How to answer

Signals, token bound to an action, server-side assessment, score plus reasons, policy per action. Stress that the decision is yours.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you choose thresholds?
  • How would you handle a mobile app?
WAF vs rate limiter vs bot management: why do you need all three?

They catch different things. A WAF inspects one request for malicious content (SQL injection, XSS, path traversal, bad inputs) and enforces simple lists (IP, geo). A rate limiter counts requests per key over time and caps volume, protecting capacity and fairness. Bot management decides whether the client is automated, using fingerprints, challenges and behaviour, which catches valid-looking requests made by scripts at polite rates, like credential stuffing from residential proxies. A credential-stuffing request passes the WAF (it is a valid login) and may pass a per-IP limit (each IP sends a few requests); only bot and risk signals catch it. Layer them cheapest first at the edge.

How to answer

Give one attack each layer catches that the others miss. The credential-stuffing example is the clincher.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where does DDoS protection fit?
  • What key would you rate-limit login on?
You enabled a managed WAF rule set and support tickets about 403 errors spiked. What now, and how should it have been rolled out?

Immediately: switch the offending rule group's override to Count (one Terraform change or console toggle) to stop the bleeding, then use WAF logs or sampled requests to find which specific rule terminated those requests and on which paths. Usually one or two rules match legitimate content, like free-text fields that contain SQL-like words. Fix with a scoped exclusion (disable or count that rule only for that path), not by disabling the whole group. Correct rollout: deploy in Count for a full business cycle, analyse matches per rule and URI, add exclusions, then enforce rule by rule with an alarm on blocked-request rate and a break-glass allow rule.

How to answer

Mitigate first, diagnose by rule ID and URI, scoped fix, then describe the count-first process as the prevention.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you test WAF rules before production?
  • Anomaly scoring vs first-match blocking?
Design bot protection for a flash sale where scalpers buy all the stock in seconds.

Layer it. At the edge: anycast CDN with DDoS shielding, cache everything static, and a WAF with rate-based rules scoped to add-to-cart and checkout. Before the sale: require sign-in, and challenge or score sessions as they join a virtual waiting room, so queue positions go only to clients with a valid, unexpired challenge token. Use bot scores plus clustering on fingerprint, timing and path to challenge or drop coordinated groups. In the app: per-account and per-payment-instrument purchase limits, one active checkout per account, and device or OTP checks on accounts created recently. Asynchronously: stream events to Kafka, detect clusters (many accounts sharing a device, card or address) and cancel or hold suspicious orders before shipping. Measure with false-positive rate and conversion of real users, not just blocked counts.

How to answer

Structure by time (before, during, after the sale) and by layer. Mention that business limits (per account, per card) beat purely technical ones for scalpers.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop one person with 500 accounts?
  • What if the risk service is down at sale start?
Attackers rotate through residential proxies so per-IP limits do nothing. How do you detect them?

Stop keying on IP alone. Key on things that do not rotate as cheaply: the TLS and HTTP fingerprint (JA4, header order), the challenge token (one token seen from many IPs or ASNs means it was shared), device IDs from a mobile SDK, and account or target identifiers (many failed logins against many accounts with the same fingerprint, or many attempts against one account from many IPs). Then cluster: sessions with identical fingerprints, identical navigation paths and machine-regular timing form a dense group no human population produces. Act on the cluster with challenges, and use step-up authentication on the targeted action (OTP on login) so even a successful bot cannot finish the flow.

How to answer

Name better keys, then clustering, then raising the cost of the action itself.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is JA4 and how can it be evaded?
  • How do you avoid catching legitimate users behind the same corporate proxy?
How do edge DDoS shielding, WAF and rate limits fit together during a large attack?

They are ordered by cost per decision. Anycast spreads a volumetric flood across many edge sites, where L3/L4 filters (often XDP/eBPF in the kernel, SYN cookies) drop junk packets before any HTTP parsing; AWS Shield Standard does this automatically for CloudFront and Route 53. HTTP floods that survive are handled by the WAF: rate-based rules on the targeted path, geo or reputation lists, and Challenge actions that scripts cannot pass. Caching absorbs requests for cacheable content. The origin accepts only edge traffic and sheds low-priority routes if it still saturates. Autoscaling is the last resort, not the defence, because the attacker can usually scale faster and cheaper than you.

How to answer

Walk the packet from the anycast edge inward and say which layer drops what. Close with "hide the origin" and "do not autoscale your way out".

Likely follow-ups
  • What is an amplification attack?
  • Why do SYN cookies help?
JA3 vs JA4: what is the difference, and why did JA4 appear?

Both fingerprint the TLS ClientHello so you can recognise the client library regardless of the User-Agent it claims. JA3 hashes the fields in the order sent, so when Chrome started randomising the order of TLS extensions, every Chrome connection got a different JA3 and the fingerprint lost its value. JA4 sorts extensions and ciphers before hashing and keeps a readable prefix (protocol, TLS version, SNI present, counts, ALPN), so it is stable across that randomisation and easy to filter on. The JA4+ family adds fingerprints for HTTP, TLS servers and TCP. Use them as one signal: a "Chrome" User-Agent with a Python or Go TLS fingerprint is suspicious, but a fingerprint alone does not identify a person.

How to answer

Say what is hashed, why order randomisation broke JA3, and that it is one signal among many.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can a bot fake JA4? (Yes, with libraries such as curl-impersonate or uTLS, which is why behaviour and reputation still matter.)
How do you rate-limit fairly when thousands of real users share one IP behind carrier-grade NAT?

Per-IP limits alone punish whole mobile networks: in India many users on one carrier share a few public IPs. Key limits on something closer to the user: a session or device token, an account ID after login, or an API key, and keep per-IP limits only as a loose outer guard with high thresholds and per-ASN awareness. For anonymous traffic, issue a token after a silent challenge and rate-limit per token. Combine with reputation: residential and mobile ASNs get more headroom than data-centre ranges. Measure collateral damage with challenge-pass rates per ASN.

How to answer

Say "key on identity, not IP", then the layered fallback, then how you measure false positives.

Likely follow-ups
  • What about IPv6? (Limit per /64 or /56 prefix, not per address, because one device can rotate addresses.)
Should a risk-scoring service fail open or fail closed?

Decide per action, in advance. For browsing and search, fail open: if the scoring service times out, allow the request, because blocking every user is worse than letting some bots through for a minute. For high-value actions (payouts, gift-card redemption, password reset), fail to a safer default such as step-up authentication or a queue for review, rather than a hard allow or a hard block. Protect the scoring call with a tight timeout and a circuit breaker so its outage never becomes your outage, cache recent verdicts, and alert on the fallback rate so an attacker cannot hide behind a deliberate overload.

How to answer

Answer "it depends on the action" and give one example each way, plus the timeout and circuit breaker.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would an attacker exploit fail-open? (Overload the risk service so everything is allowed; rate-limit and alert on fallback.)
How do you let good bots (Googlebot, uptime monitors, partners) through while blocking bad ones?

Never trust the User-Agent. Verify good bots cryptographically or by network: reverse-DNS plus forward-confirm for search engines, published IP ranges, or signed requests (Web Bot Auth / HTTP message signatures that some bot operators now use). Managed bot rules keep a verified-bot list. Partners and monitors get API keys or mTLS and their own rate limits. Then put the verified label into the policy: allow verified crawlers on public pages, but still rate-limit them and keep them away from login and checkout.

How to answer

Say "verify, do not trust the User-Agent", then the three verification methods.

Likely follow-ups
  • What do you do about AI crawlers? (Same rule: verify, then decide per path with robots.txt and WAF rules.)
Mobile app: device attestation or a JavaScript challenge?

A mobile app cannot run a browser JS challenge in a meaningful way, and its API calls are easy to replay from a script. Use platform attestation instead: Play Integrity on Android and App Attest / DeviceCheck on iOS prove the request comes from a genuine install of your app on a real device. The app gets a token, the backend verifies it with Google or Apple and binds it to the session. Treat it as a strong signal, not a guarantee (rooted devices and device farms exist), and keep server-side rate limits and behaviour scoring. Rotate and check token freshness so tokens cannot be harvested and replayed.

How to answer

Contrast browser vs app, name both platform APIs, and say it is a signal plus server-side checks.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you roll it out without locking out old app versions? (Monitor mode first, then enforce for new versions.)
How do you measure false positives in a bot-defence system?

You cannot label every request, so combine several measurements. Run new rules in count or monitor mode and compare their matches with known-good traffic (logged-in customers with purchase history). Track challenge outcomes: a high pass rate on a challenge means you are challenging humans. Watch business metrics by segment (conversion, login success) when a rule is switched on, and use holdout groups. Sample blocked requests for manual review, and give users a support path whose reports feed back into labels. Report false-positive rate alongside catch rate, because a rule that blocks 99% of bots and 2% of customers may lose more money than it saves.

How to answer

Name count mode, challenge pass rate, business metrics with holdouts, and manual review.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is a reasonable target? (It depends on the action: near zero on checkout, more tolerance on scraping-heavy pages.)

Scenario: credential stuffing during a sale

During a flash sale, login attempts jump from 2,000/min to 90,000/min, 97% failing. Requests come from tens of thousands of residential IPs, each trying only a few times, with real-looking Chrome User-Agents.

Weak answer: "Block the top IPs and add a CAPTCHA to every login."

Strong answer: Per-IP blocking fails because the attack is spread across residential proxies, and a CAPTCHA on every login hurts real buyers during the sale. Layer it: at the edge, managed bot rules and the account-takeover rule group, JA4 and HTTP/2 fingerprints that do not match the claimed browser, and a silent challenge for clients without a token. In the app, score each attempt on credential-level signals: failures per username across IPs, breached-password checks, and new device plus new location; step up with OTP only for risky attempts. Rate-limit per username and per device token, not per IP. Protect accounts that were hit: force reset where a login succeeded from a stuffing source. Track the failed-login ratio and the challenge pass rate to tune.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you tell stuffing from a real login spike at sale time? (The failure ratio and the spread of usernames per fingerprint.)

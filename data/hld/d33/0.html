The story

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a giant map of the whole world on the classroom floor. You want to find ice-cream shops near your house. Looking at every shop in the world would take forever. So the teacher cuts the map into 32 big squares and writes a letter on each one. Then she cuts each big square into 32 smaller squares and writes a second letter, and so on. Your house is in square "9", then "9q", then "9q8"... Shops near you have names that start with the same letters. To find shops near you, you only look in your little square and the 8 squares touching it.

Now a second game: your friends are walking around with glowing stickers. Every 30 seconds each friend shouts "I'm here now!". You do not want to hear every shout in the world, only the shouts of your friends who are close. So each friend has their own walkie-talkie channel, and you only tune in to your friends' channels.

That is the whole page in two pictures. The first game is a proximity service (Yelp, Google Places, "ATMs near me"). The data is static: a restaurant rarely moves, so we can build a clever index once and read it millions of times. The second game is nearby friends (Facebook Nearby Friends, Snap Map, a delivery app showing riders). The data is dynamic: locations change all the time, so the hard part is the flood of writes and pushing updates to the right people.

Interviewers love this problem because it is the base of many others. Ride hailing finds nearby drivers, food delivery finds nearby restaurants and riders, Tinder finds nearby people, Amazon Locker finds the nearest locker, and Google Maps needs "search this area". Our gap analysis found the Yelp / nearby-friends problem in 7 of the 12 public curricula we counted (ByteByteGo has two chapters on it), the most-listed design that this site was missing.

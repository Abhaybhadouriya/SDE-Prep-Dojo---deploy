Interview: driving the 45 minutes

  1. 0-5 min: requirements. Feed, infinite scroll, redirect; ask about clustering and personalisation. State availability over consistency, 200 ms, 30-minute freshness.
  2. 5-10 min: estimate. 10 writes/s vs 10k-100k reads/s; 64 MB of feeds. Conclude "precompute and cache".
  3. 10-15 min: API and entities. Feed with cursor, story, redirect. Publisher, Article, Story.
  4. 15-25 min: high-level design. Draw both halves: ingestion pipeline and read path, joined by Redis.
  5. 25-40 min: deep dives. Pick two or three: ingestion (pull/push), dedup (SimHash/MinHash), pagination, spikes.
  6. 40-45 min: failure modes and extensions. Personalisation, categories, monitoring freshness.

60-second answer, said out loud: "Writes are tiny, reads are huge, so I precompute and cache. A scheduler polls publishers' RSS feeds with conditional GETs, learns each feed's rhythm, and also accepts WebSub pushes and news sitemaps, always respecting robots.txt and per-host limits. Fetched articles go to Kafka; a parser extracts text and a thumbnail; dedup drops exact copies by URL hash and reprints by 64-bit SimHash within Hamming distance 3; a clusterer groups different reports of the same event into a story with MinHash/LSH or embeddings. A ranker scores stories by freshness, source authority and coverage every minute and writes one sorted set per region and category in Redis. The feed API reads it with a cursor, the CDN caches the first page for 30 seconds, and clicks go through a redirect that logs to Kafka for ranking."

SDE-1 stops here / SDE-2 adds. SDE-1: pull ingestion with a schedule, Kafka between stages, exact dedup by hash, a precomputed feed per region in Redis, cursor pagination, CDN caching and the click redirect. SDE-2 adds: SimHash and the permuted-table search for Hamming distance 3, MinHash/LSH clustering into stories, WebSub and adaptive polling, snapshot cursors when ranks move, the breaking-news surge (coalescing, stale-while-revalidate), and personalisation as re-ranking on top of the shared feed.

You might confuse this with a social news feed (Facebook, Twitter). There, each user's feed is built from the people they follow, so fan-out on write or read is the core problem. Here, almost everyone in a region sees the same feed, so you build one list and cache it. Also do not confuse dedup (same text, drop the copy) with clustering (different text about the same event, group into one story).

Common mistakes

Common mistakes: ranking with a SQL ORDER BY on every request; offset pagination; storing images in the database; dedup with a normal hash (misses reprints); personalising everything and losing the CDN; forgetting robots.txt and per-host limits; no story concept at all, so the feed shows the same event 40 times.

Design Google News. Walk me through the high-level design.

Two halves. Write side: a scheduler polls about 50,000 feeds adaptively with conditional GETs (and receives WebSub pushes), puts items on Kafka, a parser canonicalises URLs and drops exact duplicates, a clusterer uses SimHash/MinHash to attach each article to a story, and articles and stories go to a KV store with thumbnails in object storage. A ranker every 30-60 seconds writes the top 1,000 story ids per region and category to Redis. Read side: the feed API reads a slice from Redis, hydrates 20 cards and returns them with a cursor; the CDN caches first pages for 30-60 s. Clicks go through a 302 redirect that logs to Kafka.

How to answer

Lead with the read/write ratio (1,000:1) so precomputation feels inevitable. Draw the two halves and say where they meet.

Likely follow-ups

  • What happens to readers if the clusterer is down for an hour?
How do you make sure new articles appear within 30 minutes without hammering publishers?

Adaptive polling plus push. Each feed has a next_poll_at: busy feeds go down to 1 minute, quiet feeds back off to 1-2 hours, with jitter. Every poll is a conditional GET (ETag / If-Modified-Since), so an unchanged feed costs a 304 and a few hundred bytes. Where the feed advertises a WebSub hub, subscribe and get pushes within seconds, keeping a slow safety poll. Respect robots.txt (cache it at most 24 hours) and per-host concurrency limits. Monitor freshness directly: time from published_at to "on the board".

How to answer

Show you know the HTTP mechanics (304, ETag) and the standard (WebSub), then the scheduler data structure.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you shard the scheduler across machines?
  • A publisher has no feed at all - now what?
How do you detect that two articles from different publishers are the same story?

Layered. Same URL: canonicalise and hash. Same text with different boilerplate: 64-bit SimHash, near-duplicate if the Hamming distance is 3 or less (the setting Google's crawler validated on 8 billion pages). Same event in different words: MinHash over 5-word shingles estimates Jaccard similarity, LSH banding finds candidates without comparing all pairs, and we add named-entity overlap and a time window of 24-48 hours. Attach to the best-matching recent story or start a new one; an hourly batch job merges and splits clusters.

How to answer

Separate "duplicate" (drop) from "same story" (group). Explain why a cryptographic hash fails.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you search 30M fingerprints for Hamming distance 3 quickly?
  • What about the same story in Hindi and English?
How do you find all fingerprints within Hamming distance 3 without a full scan?

Pigeonhole: split the 64 bits into, say, 4 blocks of 16. If two fingerprints differ in at most 3 bits, at least one block is identical. Keep one table per block, keyed (or sorted) by that block; look up the query's block in each table and check the few candidates with XOR + popcount. Manku et al. generalise this with several permuted, sorted, compressed tables, trading memory for fewer candidates. At our size (30M fingerprints, 240 MB) the tables fit in RAM on one machine.

How to answer

Say "pigeonhole principle" and the block trick; mention the memory cost of multiple tables.

Likely follow-ups

Why not offset pagination for infinite scroll? What do you use instead?

New stories arrive at the top, so "skip 20" points somewhere new and the reader sees repeats or misses items. Use a cursor: either a keyset cursor (last score and id, then "items after this") or a snapshot cursor (a version of the ranked list kept for 10-15 minutes plus an offset into it). The snapshot works even when the ranker reshuffles scores every minute. Show new arrivals with a "new stories" pill rather than shifting the list.

How to answer

Draw the repeat problem in three boxes, then the fix.

Likely follow-ups

A huge event breaks. Read traffic goes 10x in two minutes. What happens?

First pages are served by the CDN with a 30 s max-age and stale-while-revalidate, so most of the surge never reaches us. Expiries are coalesced into one origin request. Behind it, Redis replicas serve the precomputed lists, and the feed API autoscales, though autoscaling is too slow for the first minute - so we keep headroom. On the write side, the wire feeds are on 1-minute polling or push, and a fast-lane ranker pass promotes stories whose source count climbs quickly. If anything is overloaded we shed personalisation first and serve the shared feed.

How to answer

Cover both: read surge (CDN, coalescing, headroom) and write freshness (fast lane).

Likely follow-ups
  • What is your cache-key and TTL choice, and why not 5 minutes?
How would you add personalisation without breaking latency?

Keep the shared feed as the candidate source. For a signed-in user, take the top 200 candidates from the region and categories they follow, and re-rank with a light model using recent clicks and topic affinities stored in a KV store keyed by user id. This is a classic candidate generation + ranking split. Google's 2007 system combined MinHash user clusters, PLSI and co-visitation counts, and had only a few hundred milliseconds of budget. Separate the click-statistics writer from the serving path, so recommendations survive if the writer is down. Personal pages skip the CDN, so cache the user profile, not the page.

How to answer

Name the CDN trade-off explicitly, then the two-stage approach.

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you avoid a filter bubble? (Google keeps Full Coverage unpersonalised.)
Why does the click go through your server instead of linking straight to the publisher?

The redirect logs the click (ranking and personalisation signal, publisher analytics) and lets us fix the target if a publisher changes URLs. Use 302 so the browser does not cache the redirect permanently. Keep it fast: the redirect service only looks up the URL (cached) and writes an async event to Kafka. Cost: one extra round trip for the reader; an alternative is a direct link with a ping attribute or a beacon.

How to answer

Give the reason and the cost; show you know 301 vs 302.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop click fraud from boosting a story?
One publisher suddenly sends 10,000 near-duplicate articles in an hour. What happens, and how do you protect the system?

First, the pipeline survives: Kafka absorbs the burst, and per-publisher quotas in the fetcher and parser stop one source from starving the others (a token bucket per publisher, plus a separate partition key so its backlog does not block other feeds). Second, the copies are cheap to drop: each one is fingerprinted with SimHash and checked against the recent fingerprint tables; anything within Hamming distance 3 of an existing article is attached to that story as a duplicate, not indexed again. Third, it must not distort ranking: a story's "coverage" score counts distinct publishers, not articles, so 10,000 copies from one source add nothing. Finally, flag the source: a sudden jump in articles per hour or in duplicate ratio is a spam or misconfiguration signal, so lower its authority score and alert a human.

How to answer

Answer in three layers: protect the pipeline (quotas, Kafka), drop the copies cheaply (fingerprints), and protect ranking (count publishers, not articles). Then add detection.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the copies are rewritten by a spinner so SimHash misses them? (MinHash on shingles or embeddings, and a per-source cap per story.)
  • How do you give a legitimate wire service high volume without trusting spammers?
Why not precompute a separate feed for every user, the way a social news feed does?

Because almost all of the value is shared. Users in one region and category see the same top stories, so one sorted set per (region, category) serves millions of readers and fits in about 64 MB. A feed per user would multiply storage and ranking work by the number of users, would have to be rebuilt every minute as stories move, and would destroy the CDN hit rate because no two responses would be the same. The better split is candidate generation plus re-ranking: take the top ~200 shared candidates for the user's region and followed categories, then re-rank them at request time with a small model and the user's recent clicks from a key-value store. Unsigned users and the first page of each feed stay fully cached.

How to answer

Contrast with the social feed: there the inputs differ per user (who you follow); here they are shared. Then name the CDN cost and give the two-stage alternative.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where would you store the per-user signals, and how fresh must they be?
  • How do you measure whether personalisation helps? (An A/B test on click-through and distinct stories read.)

Scenario: the same story fills the whole feed

After a launch, product says the top 10 cards on the Tech feed are all about one phone, from 10 different papers.
Weak answer: "Add a rule: at most 3 articles per topic." (It hides the bug and the next event breaks it again.)
Strong answer: The clustering step is not grouping them. Check the near-duplicate threshold and whether the MinHash/LSH bands are too strict for articles with different wording. Add entity overlap ("Phone X", the company) within a 24-hour window as a signal. Run the offline merge job. Add a ranker rule that one story occupies one card, and a metric: "distinct stories in top 20".

Scenario: news is 40 minutes late

An article published at 10:00 shows up at 10:40. The SLO is 30 minutes.
Weak answer: "Poll every feed every minute."
Strong answer: Break the latency into stages with timestamps: publish to fetch (poll interval or push), fetch to Kafka, consumer lag on the parser and clusterer, ranker interval, CDN TTL. Fix the biggest one. Often it is the poll interval on a feed whose rhythm the scheduler learned wrongly, or clusterer lag after a traffic burst; scale the consumers on lag and give high-authority feeds a 1-minute floor.

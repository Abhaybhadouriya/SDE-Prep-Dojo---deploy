Architecture: an article's journey

Click through the stepper. The left half is the slow, write-side pipeline. The right half is the fast read path. They meet only at the precomputed feed in Redis.

  1. The scheduler picks feeds whose next_poll_at has passed and hands them to stateless fetchers. Each fetcher sends a conditional GET with the saved ETag; most replies are a cheap 304 Not Modified.
  2. Publishers that support WebSub push new items to our callback within seconds, so we can poll them rarely (a safety net in case a push is lost).
  3. Every new or changed feed item becomes a message on the Kafka topic raw-articles. Kafka decouples bursty fetching from processing and lets us replay if a parser bug ships.
  4. The parser extracts title, snippet, time, language and image, canonicalises the URL and hashes it. An article id we have already seen is dropped here (exact duplicates).
  5. The clusterer computes a SimHash of the text, looks for a near-identical article (re-published wire copy) and for a similar story (same event, different words). It attaches the article to an existing story or creates a new one.
  6. Articles and stories go to the KV store; a resized thumbnail goes to object storage, served later by the CDN.
  7. Every 30-60 seconds the ranker scores recent stories (freshness, number of sources, source authority, clicks) and writes the top 1,000 story ids per region and category into Redis sorted sets.
  8. The feed API reads a slice of story ids from Redis, hydrates the 20 cards (batch get, also cached) and returns JSON. The CDN caches the whole response for 30-60 seconds per region and category.
  9. A click goes through /r/{articleId}: a 302 to the publisher plus a click event to Kafka, which feeds the ranker and personalisation.

Every box on the write side can be slow or down for minutes and readers will not notice: they keep getting the last good feed from Redis and the CDN. That is "availability over consistency" turned into a diagram.

The story

Explain like I'm 5

There is a mango stall in the school fair. Kids who want to buy stand on the left and shout "I'll pay 10!" or "I'll pay 12!". Kids who want to sell mangoes stand on the right: "I'll sell for 13!" "I'll sell for 15!".

The shopkeeper has two rules. Rule 1: the buyer who pays the most and the seller who asks the least go first. Rule 2: if two kids shout the same price, the one who came first goes first. When a buyer says "I'll pay 13!" and a seller is already asking 13, the shopkeeper shakes their hands: a trade. The shopkeeper writes every shout in a notebook with a number: 1, 2, 3... If the shopkeeper faints, a helper reads the notebook from line 1 and knows exactly who is waiting and what was sold.

The two lines of kids are the order book: buy orders are bids, sell orders are asks (or offers). The shopkeeper is the matching engine. The two rules are price-time priority. The numbered notebook is the sequenced journal, and reading it again from line 1 is event sourcing: the state is rebuilt by replaying events. The gap between the best bid (12) and the best ask (13) is the spread.

Interviewers like this question (it is a staple of system design prep channels, candidates have reported "design a stock broking alert system" at Uber, and Zerodha-style broker designs are common in Indian loops) because it breaks the usual web playbook. A web system scales by adding stateless servers around a shared database. An exchange needs one exact global order of events per stock, and it gets speed by doing less coordination, not more.

You might confuse this with: a broker (Zerodha, Robinhood, Groww). A broker is a normal web app: user accounts, a mobile app, a portfolio page, and an order management system that forwards orders to the exchange (NSE, BSE, NYSE, Nasdaq). The exchange owns the order book and the matching. Ask the interviewer which one they mean in the first minute. This page designs the exchange, and the broker side appears where it touches.

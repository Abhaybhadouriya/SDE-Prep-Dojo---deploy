Architecture, step by step

Click Next or Play. The top half is the write/indexing path; the bottom half is the search read path.

  1. Write to the truth. A seller updates a product; the catalog DB commits it. Price and stock live in their own services with their own databases.
  2. Capture the change. Change data capture (CDC) reads each database's commit log and publishes a change event to Kafka, keyed by product_id so all changes for one product stay in order. Flipkart built AESOP for exactly this (MySQL/HBase to Elasticsearch/Kafka).
  3. Build the search document. The indexer gets a partial change ("price of listing L9 is now 24,999"). It merges it with a stored snapshot of the full document so it does not call every service again. Flipkart Sherlock keeps this snapshot in HBase.
  4. Write to search. Mostly-static text goes into the inverted index (as segments: small immutable mini-indexes that are merged in the background, refreshed about every second). Fast-changing price/stock goes to a separate near-real-time (NRT) store, the trick Flipkart presented at Lucene Revolution 2016.
  5. Query arrives. The search API checks a short-TTL result cache first; Flipkart's numbers suggest roughly 3 in 4 searches never reach the engine.
  6. Understand the query. Spell-correct, expand synonyms ("tee" = "t-shirt"), detect category and attributes ("red" -> color filter), then scatter the query to all shards.
  7. Rank. Each shard returns its top K by a cheap score (BM25, the standard keyword relevance score that rewards rare words appearing often in a document, plus boosts such as "in stock"); the coordinator merges them and an ML ranker re-orders the top few hundred using fresh signals (price, stock, rating, CTR = click-through rate, the share of people who clicked a result after seeing it).
  8. Hydrate and return. The engine returns only ids and scores; to hydrate means to fill those ids with display data (title, image URL, rating summary) from caches. Then return results plus facet counts (how many matches each filter value has, for example "Size 9 (312)"). Images load from the CDN.

From your world

Lucene segments are a log-structured design, the same idea as an LSM tree or a log-structured file system. New documents go to a fresh small segment; nothing is edited in place; a background merge combines small segments into bigger ones and drops deleted entries, like compaction. That is why changing one price means "delete + re-add the document", and why very frequent updates create merge pressure.

From your world

CDC is the database's write-ahead log being tailed, the same idea as reading a journal or a ring buffer of events. Keying Kafka by product_id is like hashing a 5-tuple to one queue so a flow's packets stay in order; ordering is per key, not global.

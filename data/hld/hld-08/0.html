The story: the kitchen with many containers

Explain like I'm 5

In a kitchen nothing goes into one giant box. Spoons go in a drawer with little slots: you know the slot, you grab the spoon. That is a key-value store.

Recipes go in a folder, one full page per dish, with everything for that dish on the page. That is a document store.

The fridge has shelves by day: Monday's leftovers, Tuesday's leftovers, newest at the front. That is a time-series or wide-column store.

Mum keeps a notebook that says "the word chocolate appears in recipes 3, 9 and 12". That is a search index.

Big things like the pressure cooker go in the store room, in labelled boxes, and you fetch them rarely. That is blob storage.

And the family tree on the wall shows who is whose cousin. That is a graph.

A good cook does not ask "what is the best container?" She asks "what am I storing and how will I grab it?"

From your world

You already pick data structures by access pattern: a hash table for exact lookups (the ARP cache), a radix trie for longest-prefix match (the FIB), a ring buffer for ordered append (a NIC queue), and a bitmap for set membership. Choosing a database is the same decision at network scale: the key-value store is the hash table, the wide-column store is a sorted append log per key, the search engine is an inverted index, and the relational database is the one structure that also guarantees transactions.

Interviewers ask "Which database would you use here, and why?" in almost every round. A weak answer names a fashionable product. A strong answer says three things: the shape of the data, the access pattern (how it is read and written, how often), and the guarantee needed (strong consistency or "eventually is fine"). Then it names a product and the price you pay for it.

Data model

TableKey fieldsStore and why
usersuser_id, email (unique, normalised), email_verified, status (active/locked/disabled), created_atPostgres (or another strongly consistent DB). Unique email needs a real constraint; 100 GB is small.
credentialsuser_id, type (password/totp/webauthn), password_hash (Argon2id string with its salt and params), totp_secret (encrypted), webauthn public key + credential id, sign_countSame DB, separate table with tighter access. Secrets encrypted with a key from KMS.
sessions / refresh familiesfamily_id, user_id, device, current_refresh_hash, previous_refresh_hash, rotated_at (for the reuse grace window), created_at, last_used_at, absolute_expiry, revokedStrongly consistent KV or Postgres, replicated per region. Store a hash of the refresh token, never the token.
oauth_clientsclient_id, type (public/confidential), redirect_uris (exact), allowed_scopes, secret_hashPostgres, cached.
auth_codescode_hash, client_id, user_id, code_challenge, redirect_uri, nonce, expires_at (about 1 min), usedRedis with TTL; single use.
signing_keyskid, algorithm, public key, status (next/active/retired)Private keys live in a KMS/HSM; only public halves are published.
denylistsession/family id or jti -> untilRedis, replicated to every region, pushed to services by pub/sub.
login_attempts(user_id), (ip), (ip-range) -> counters with windowsRedis, for rate limiting and lockout.

Why SQL here? Identity data is small, relational, and correctness matters more than write throughput: you want a unique constraint on email, transactions when you add an MFA factor, and strong reads after a password change. The high-volume part (validation) does not touch the database at all. Hot, short-lived data (codes, counters, denylist) goes to Redis with TTLs.

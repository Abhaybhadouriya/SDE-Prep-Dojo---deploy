Memory-based CF: user-based vs item-based

Must know (SDE-1)

User-based CF: to recommend for Eve, find the users whose rows look most like Eve's row (her "neighbours"), then recommend what those neighbours liked that Eve has not seen. Item-based CF: for each item Eve liked, find the items whose columns look most like that item's column ("people who liked X also liked Y"), and recommend those.

Item-based wins in production
  • Item-item similarities change slowly (a book's buyers are stable), so you compute them offline, daily, and serve from a table.
  • Online cost depends only on how many items Eve touched, not on how many users exist.
  • Easy to explain: "Because you bought Dune".
  • Works the moment a new user clicks one item (no history needed beyond that).
Why user-based struggles
  • User tastes drift fast, and new users have nearly empty rows, so neighbours are noisy.
  • Finding neighbours means comparing Eve with up to every other user at request time.
  • Many more users than items in most shops, so the user-user matrix is the bigger one.

Amazon's 2003 paper by Linden, Smith and York ("Amazon.com Recommendations: Item-to-Item Collaborative Filtering") made this choice famous. Amazon Science later wrote that the team picked item-to-item because it "had a lot better scaling and quality characteristics" for online recommendations; IEEE Internet Computing named the paper its test-of-time paper in 2017.

Cosine similarity, worked by hand

Treat each item's column as a vector (one number per user). Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors: dot product divided by the product of their lengths. It is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they share nothing (for non-negative data). Because it divides by length, it ignores how much and looks at pattern: a user who rates everything 5 and one who rates everything 3 can still point the same way.

Dune = (5, 4, 0, 0), Interstellar = (4, 5, 1, 0). Dot product = 20 + 20 = 40. Lengths = sqrt(41) = 6.40 and sqrt(42) = 6.48. Cosine = 40 / 41.5 = 0.96. Dune vs Frozen = (0 + 4 + 0 + 0) / 41.5 = 0.10. The same in NumPy, then scoring for Eve who only rated Dune:

import numpy as np
# rows = users (Asha, Ben, Chen, Dia); cols = items (Dune, Interstellar, Frozen, Moana)
R = np.array([[5, 4, 0, 0],
              [4, 5, 1, 0],
              [0, 1, 5, 4],
              [0, 0, 4, 5]], dtype=float)
norms = np.linalg.norm(R, axis=0)            # one length per item column
S = (R.T @ R) / np.outer(norms, norms)       # item-item cosine
print(np.round(S, 2))
eve = np.array([5, 0, 0, 0], dtype=float)    # Eve rated only Dune
scores = S @ eve
scores[eve > 0] = -np.inf                     # never recommend what she has
print(np.round(scores, 2))

Interstellar wins with 4.82. That is the whole item-based algorithm: score(item) = sum over items the user touched of similarity x the user's weight on that item.

Variations you should name

  • Adjusted cosine / Pearson correlation: subtract each user's average rating first, so a harsh rater's 3 and a generous rater's 5 mean the same "liked it". Pearson centres on the item mean instead.
  • Jaccard for pure 0/1 data: |users who touched both| / |users who touched either|.
  • Shrinkage: two items that share only 2 buyers can get cosine 1.0 by luck. Multiply by n / (n + 50) or similar, where n is the number of co-raters, to pull small-sample similarities down.
  • Top-K neighbours: keep only the 50-200 most similar items per item. This bounds storage and removes noisy long-tail pairs.

From your world

Cosine on interaction vectors is the same normalisation you do when comparing two interfaces' traffic profiles: you divide by total volume first, otherwise the 100G uplink looks "similar" to everything just because its counters are big. Shrinkage is your "do not trust a rate computed from three packets" rule.

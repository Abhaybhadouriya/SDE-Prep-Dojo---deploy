Model-based CF: matrix factorization

Must know (SDE-1)

Memory-based CF only finds items that were literally co-consumed. If nobody bought both "Dune" and an obscure sci-fi novel, their similarity is zero even if they appeal to the same people. Matrix factorization (MF) fixes this by learning hidden "taste dimensions" (latent factors).

The idea: approximate the sparse matrix R (users x items) as the product of two thin matrices, R ~ U x VT. U has one row of k numbers per user, V has one row of k numbers per item, with k small (often 32-256). The predicted score is just a dot product: r(u,i) ~ u . v. Nobody labels the factors; training discovers them. Koren, Bell and Volinsky's 2009 IEEE Computer article (from the team that won the Netflix Prize) shows factors that line up with things like "serious vs escapist" and "aimed at women vs men" without anyone naming them.

The training objective

Minimise the squared error on the observed cells, plus a penalty that keeps vectors small (regularisation, lambda), usually with bias terms:

# prediction with biases (Koren, Bell, Volinsky 2009)
r_hat(u, i) = mu + b_u + b_i + dot(U[u], V[i])
#   mu  = global average rating
#   b_u = how generous user u is      b_i = how good item i is in general
# loss = sum over observed (u,i) of (r(u,i) - r_hat(u,i))^2
#        + lambda * (|U[u]|^2 + |V[i]|^2 + b_u^2 + b_i^2)

Biases matter: a lot of a rating is "this user is generous" and "this movie is good", not taste interaction. Taking those out lets the factors model taste.

Why it is called "SVD" but is not SVD

Classical singular value decomposition factors a complete matrix exactly, and truncating it to k components gives the best rank-k approximation. But our matrix is about 99% unknown. If you fill the unknowns with zeros and run SVD, the model learns that almost everything is zero; Google's recommendation course notes this generalises poorly on sparse data. Simon Funk's famous 2006 Netflix Prize post ("Netflix Update: Try This at Home") instead fitted only the known ratings with gradient descent, one feature at a time. People kept calling it "SVD" (and "Funk SVD"), so in interviews "SVD" usually means "MF trained on observed entries", not the textbook decomposition.

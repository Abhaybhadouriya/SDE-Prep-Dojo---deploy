The story

Explain like I'm 5

In a big hospital, a nurse walks past every bed every minute and writes one number on each child's chart: the temperature. She does not write a story, just a time and a number. Because the numbers are on a chart, the doctor can see a line going up and say "this child is getting a fever" before it is serious. If the line crosses 39 degrees, a bell rings at the nurses' station. But the bell is clever: if ten children in the same room get warm at once because the heater broke, it rings once for the room, not ten times.

That is the full design. The children are targets (servers, pods, databases). The temperature is a metric: a number that changes over time, like CPU use, requests per second, or errors. The chart is a time series: a list of (time, value) pairs. The nurse walking around is scraping (the monitor pulls numbers from each target). The chart room is a time-series database (TSDB). The bell is an alert, and the clever "ring once per room" part is alert grouping and deduplication (Prometheus's Alertmanager).

Metrics are different from logs (Log Aggregation). A log line says "what happened, with details". A metric says "how much / how many, as a number". Metrics are tiny and cheap, so you can keep every one of them for months and draw fast graphs. Logs are big and detailed, so you search them only when a metric tells you where to look.

From your world

You have done this for years. Prometheus scraping is SNMP polling: the manager asks each device for its counters on a timer. Push metrics are SNMP traps or streaming telemetry (gNMI), which Cisco and Ciena gear now favour because polling thousands of counters is slow. And Prometheus counters work like your ifInOctets: they only go up, and you compute a rate by diffing two readings. Prometheus's rate() even handles a counter going back to zero after a process restart, much like your code handles a 32-bit counter wrap. Where the analogy breaks: SNMP uses fixed MIB tables; Prometheus metrics carry free-form labels, and labels are where the danger (cardinality) comes from.

Reported in the wild: "Design a real-time monitoring system" in an Uber SDE-2 bar-raiser round (from candidate reports in the research notes). It also appears as "Design Datadog / Prometheus / CloudWatch" and as the "how would you monitor this?" part of almost every other design.

New words, once each (read these before the requirements):

  • SLI (service level indicator) = the number you measure, for example "the % of checkout requests answered in under 300 ms without a 5xx".
  • SLO (service level objective) = the target for that number, for example "99.9% over 30 days".
  • Error budget = the part you are allowed to fail: with a 99.9% SLO, 0.1% of requests (about 43 minutes of full outage in 30 days).
  • Burn rate = how fast you are spending that budget. Burn rate 1 uses exactly the whole budget in 30 days; burn rate 14.4 would use 2% of it in one hour.
  • Exporter = a small process that turns a device's or app's internal counters into /metrics text, exactly like an SNMP agent answering for a router. node exporter does it for a Linux host (CPU, memory, disks, NICs); the JMX exporter does it for Java apps such as Kafka brokers.
  • kubelet = the Kubernetes agent on every node; it also exposes per-container CPU and memory metrics.
  • Head block = Prometheus's in-memory section holding the most recent ~2 hours of samples, before they are written out as an immutable block on disk.
  • Cardinality = how many different series a metric produces (deep dive 2). p99 = 99 of 100 requests are faster than this.

You might confuse:

  • Counter vs gauge. A counter only goes up (requests served, bytes sent) and you graph its rate(); it resets to 0 on restart. A gauge goes up and down (memory in use, queue length) and you graph it as is. Never take rate() of a gauge.
  • Histogram vs summary. Both describe latency. A histogram exports bucket counters (requests under 100 ms, under 300 ms, ...) that you can add across pods and turn into p99 on the server. A summary computes p99 inside each pod, and percentiles from different pods cannot be averaged.
  • SLO vs SLA. The SLO is your internal target; an SLA is a contract with customers, with money attached, and is usually looser than the SLO.

Scaling the fleet: advanced levers

Model parallelism

A big model may not fit on one GPU. Tensor parallelism splits each layer's matrices across GPUs (they exchange partial results every layer, so they need a very fast interconnect inside one server). Pipeline parallelism puts different layers on different GPUs (less chatter, but "pipeline bubbles" of idle time). Scaling is sublinear: Databricks saw Llama2-70B latency at small batch drop only to about 0.7x when going from 4 to 8 GPUs.

Prefill/decode disaggregation

Because prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound, Microsoft's Splitwise runs them on separate machine pools and ships the KV cache from prefill machines to decode machines over a fast network. Their reported results: 1.4x higher throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35x throughput at the same cost and power. It also stops a long prefill from stalling everyone else's decode steps.

Other levers

  • Quantisation: store weights (and even KV cache) in 8 bits or fewer instead of 16. Less memory to read per step means faster decode and bigger batches, with some quality risk to measure.
  • Speculative decoding: a small draft model guesses several tokens, the big model checks them in one pass, and correct guesses are kept.
  • Model routing / cascades: a small model handles easy or classification-style steps; LinkedIn used smaller models for routing and retrieval and larger ones for final generation.
  • Autoscaling on the right signal: scale GPU replicas on queue depth, KV-cache utilisation or TTFT, not CPU. GPU nodes take minutes to start and load weights, so keep warm headroom. See Kubernetes scheduling and autoscaling for GPU node pools.

Self-hosting models
  • Control over latency, batching, data residency
  • Cheaper at steady high volume
  • Custom or fine-tuned models
Costs of self-hosting
  • Scarce GPUs and slow scale-up
  • You own the serving stack, upgrades and on-call
  • Idle GPUs at low traffic are pure cost

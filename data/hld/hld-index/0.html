The story: from a lemonade stand to a city

Explain like I'm 5

You open a lemonade stand. One table, one jug, one cup. Ten friends come, and it works fine. Then the whole school comes. Now you need more jugs, more helpers, a line so people do not push, a fridge near the table so you do not run home for ice, and a notebook so two helpers do not sell the same last cup. Then the whole city wants lemonade. Now you need stands in every neighbourhood, trucks to carry lemons, and a phone call every evening to count how many cups were sold.

System design is planning the lemonade business for the whole city before it arrives. You decide where the stands go, where the lemons are kept, who counts the money, and what happens if one stand catches fire.

In an interview, "High-Level Design" (HLD) means: someone names a product ("design WhatsApp", "design Flipkart's flash sale"), and you draw the big boxes that make it work for millions of people. You explain what each box does, how data moves between them, and what breaks first. You do not write code in this round. You make decisions and explain the trade-offs.

From your world

You have already designed systems: a forwarding pipeline, a control-plane daemon, a driver with rings and interrupts. HLD is the same thinking one zoom level out. A packet becomes a request, a line card becomes a server, a FIB becomes a cache, ECMP becomes a load balancer, a redundancy pair becomes replication. The big change is the starting point: SDE interviews start from the user and the API, not from the internals. Where this breaks: on one box you control the clock and memory; across 500 servers nothing is shared, and the network can drop or delay anything.

You asked: "Can I skip HLD because I am a systems person?" No. Even embedded and infra loops at big companies often include a web-scale design round. The good news: your instincts about latency, throughput, queues and failure are exactly what interviewers want to hear. This track gives you the vocabulary and the standard designs.

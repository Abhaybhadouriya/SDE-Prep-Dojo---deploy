Back-of-envelope estimation

The reports give the shape, not the numbers, so state assumptions. Method from Back-of-Envelope Estimation.

10K/sticks in
50Mactive subscriptions
~5 GBrules in memory
15Malerts in a crash hour
  1. Ticks Assume 10,000 symbols (a large broker covering several exchanges), one update per second each: 10K ticks per second, each about 50 bytes = 500 KB per second. Tiny bandwidth; the work is in matching.
  2. Subscriptions 10M users x 5 alerts = 50M. About 100 bytes each in memory (id, user, symbol, type, threshold, window, expiry) = 5 GB. Spread over 10 evaluator workers that is 500 MB each: fits easily.
  3. Naive cost Checking every rule of a symbol on every tick: 50M rules / 10K symbols = 5,000 rules per symbol on average, x 10K ticks = 50M checks per second. Possible in memory, terrible as SQL. With sorted thresholds, each tick does one O(log n) lookup plus the rules it fires: about 10K lookups per second in total.
  4. DB-per-tick cost (the flaw) 10K ticks per second = 10K database queries per second, each scanning a symbol's subscriptions, all day, mostly returning nothing. That is the design the Uber interviewer flagged.
  5. Normal alert volume If 1% of subscriptions fire per day: 500K alerts a day, about 20 per second over market hours. Easy.
  6. Crash day A market-wide 5% drop fires most "down 5%" rules within minutes. Assume 30% of all subscriptions: 15M alerts in about 10 minutes = 25K per second into notifications. That burst sizes the notification path, not the evaluator.
  7. History 10K symbols x 6.5 trading hours x 3,600 = 234M ticks a day x about 20 bytes compressed = about 5 GB a day in a time-series store. A year is under 2 TB.

What the numbers tell you: the input is small and steady; the rules fit in RAM; the dangerous moment is the output burst on a big market move, and the subtle part is rolling-window state and recovery.

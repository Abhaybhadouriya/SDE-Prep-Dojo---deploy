Back-of-envelope estimation

Flash sale estimates are unusual: the interesting number is not the average, it is the first few seconds. Work through the funnel layer by layer.

  1. Supply (assumption). 10,000 phones in this deal.
  2. Demand at T0 (real). Flipkart's CPTO said its systems handle about 1.5 million requests per second when the sale opens (Flipkart Stories, 2023). That is site-wide, not one product, but it sets the scale.
  3. Interested users (assumption). 5 million people are on the deal page at 11:59.
  4. Ratio. 10,000 / 5,000,000 = 0.2%. So 499 of every 500 people will not get a phone. The design must make "no" cheap, fast and polite.
  5. Bandwidth if we served the page from our servers. Assume a 100 KB product page: 1.5M x 100 KB = 150 GB/s, about 1.2 Tbps. No origin fleet should do that. The page must be static, pre-rendered and on a CDN, with only a tiny dynamic call for "can I buy?".
  6. What the backend can take (assumption). Say the reservation service plus Redis can safely handle 20,000 attempts per second. The waiting room admits at that rate.
  7. How long the sale lasts. At 20,000 attempts/s, and assuming half of admitted users actually buy, 10,000 units go in about 1 second of admitted traffic. The sale is over almost instantly. What matters is that the "sold out" message then reaches 5 million people quickly.
  8. Orders downstream. 10,000 reservations become orders. Flipkart prepared for over 2,000 orders per second in 2023, so the order pipeline can drain 10,000 in about 5 seconds if we feed it at a controlled rate through Kafka. That is why it can be asynchronous.
  9. Storage. Tiny: 10,000 reservations x 1 KB = 10 MB. A flash sale is a traffic problem, not a data problem.

Aerospike is a fast key-value database that Flipkart uses in its data tier; the 90M QPS figure (from Aerospike's own vendor write-up) shows how much read and write capacity sits behind a sale like this. You do not need to know Aerospike for the interview.

From your world

The funnel is admission control plus policing at the edge, the same thing you do on a router: drop or mark excess traffic at the ingress line card with a token-bucket policer, so it never consumes switch-fabric or CPU resources further in. Dropping a packet at ingress is cheap. Dropping it after it crossed the fabric wasted everything. Where it breaks: a router drops silently, but here a "drop" must be a friendly queue page or a sold-out page, or users retry and multiply the load.

Back-of-envelope estimation

Use 1 day = about 105 seconds. Assumptions are labelled. Real published numbers are labelled with their source.

A. Orders

  1. Daily orders (assumption). 10 million orders on a normal day for a large marketplace.
  2. Average rate. 10,000,000 / 100,000 = 100 orders/s.
  3. Sale-day peak (assumption). 10x the average in the busiest minutes: 1,000 orders/s.
  4. Reality check. Flipkart said that for Big Billion Days 2023 it was prepared to process over 2,000 orders per second on its app (Flipkart Stories Q&A with its CPTO). So design for 2,000 orders/s.

B. What one order costs inside

  1. Line items (assumption). 2.5 items per order on average.
  2. Inventory writes. One reservation per line: 2,000 x 2.5 = 5,000 reservation writes/s at peak, plus as many releases or confirmations later.
  3. Payment calls. One authorisation per order: 2,000/s to payment providers. Providers have their own limits, so plan per-provider rate limits and failover.
  4. Events. About 5 events per order (placed, payment authorised, confirmed, shipped, delivered): 10,000 events/s at peak into Kafka. Kafka handles this easily. The work is in the consumers.
  5. Cart writes (assumption). 10 cart changes per order: 20,000 cart writes/s at peak, which is the biggest write stream. That is why the cart lives in a horizontally scalable key-value store.

C. Storage

  1. Order size (assumption). 5 KB with lines, address snapshot, price breakdown and status history.
  2. Per day. 10M x 5 KB = 50 GB. Per year about 18 TB, about 55 TB with 3 replicas. Orders are kept for years (tax, returns), so shard and move old orders to cheaper storage.
  3. Carts. Assume 100M active carts x 2 KB = 200 GB. Small, but every byte is hot.

The AWS numbers come from its "How AWS powered Prime Day 2024" post: DynamoDB peaked at 146 million requests per second, and 6,311 Aurora database instances processed more than 376 billion transactions. You will not need those numbers in your design, but they show that even the biggest shop in the world plans around spikes and uses both a key-value store and relational databases, each for what it does best.

The story

Explain like I'm 5

You want a toy robot, but it costs too much. Grandma says: "I walk past the toy shop every day. Write your name and the price you can pay on a sticky note, and I will tell you when the price is low enough." Soon lots of children give Grandma notes. Grandma is clever. She does not read every note every day. She keeps the notes for the robot together, sorted from the lowest price to the highest. When the robot's price tag changes from 50 coins to 40 coins, she pulls out every note that says "40 or more" which she has not answered yet, tells those children, and puts a tick on each note so she never tells the same child twice. She also writes the price in a little book every day, so anyone can see if the robot is usually cheaper in the summer. And she walks past the busy shops more often than the quiet ones, so she never annoys the shopkeeper by peeking in all day.

That is the whole design. Grandma's walk is the fetch scheduler and the fetch workers. The little book is the price history, stored in a time-series database. The sorted pile of sticky notes is the threshold index. Pulling out the unticked notes whose price is now reached is crossing detection, and the tick is the armed / disarmed flag: together they alert each person once and only once. Not annoying the shopkeeper is politeness: rate limits, robots.txt, and using official feeds where they exist.

The well-known product is camelcamelcamel, which tracks Amazon prices and has a Chrome extension. The interview cousin is the stock price alert: "tell me when AAPL drops below 180". Uber has asked both shapes in SDE-2 loops, as "price tracking for stock tickers" (a bar-raiser round) and as a "stock-broking alert system", according to self-reported interview experiences collected in our gap analysis. Amazon interviewers like "a problem similar to a real Amazon service", and this one is literally about Amazon product pages.

Say this early: "This is a read-many, write-many pipeline with an inverted lookup in the middle. I collect prices, store history, and for every price change I must find the users whose threshold it just crossed. I index alerts by product and threshold, not by user." That sentence shows you already see the hard part.

From your world

Think of the threshold index like a timer wheel or a routing table. The kernel does not scan every timer on each tick; it keeps them ordered so each tick touches only the timers that expire now. A router does not compare a packet with every route; it walks a sorted structure (a trie) to the matching prefix. Here, each price change is the "tick" or the "packet", and we want to touch only the alerts it fires.

New words, once each: a time-series database stores readings in time order per item and is good at "give me product P from March to May". Kafka is a durable, ordered log that many services read at their own speed. A partition is one ordered slice of a Kafka topic. Idempotent means safe to retry: doing it twice has the same effect as once. A B-tree index keeps keys sorted on disk so a range ("targets from 2,800 up") is one short walk. A token bucket is a rate limiter that allows a steady rate with small bursts. TTL (time to live) means the store deletes the item by itself when it expires. QPS is requests per second. The rest are in the glossary.

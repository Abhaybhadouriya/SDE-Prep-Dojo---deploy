Back-of-envelope estimation

Method from Back-of-Envelope Estimation. We cannot refresh 500M products every hour, so the first estimate is really a design decision: how often do we look at each product? Say each assumption out loud.

~230Mprice fetches per day
~8K/speak fetch rate
~1.2 GBnew history per day
100Mactive watches
  1. Tier the catalogue by interest Most products nobody watches. Assume 5M "hot" products (many watchers, refresh hourly), 45M "warm" (some watchers, refresh daily) and 450M "cold" (no watchers, refresh weekly). The third-party handbook-hld price-tracking case study uses the same three-tier idea (1M hot hourly, 80M warm daily, 19M cold weekly for 100M listings); its numbers are its own assumptions, like ours.
  2. Fetches per day Hot: 5M x 24 = 120M. Warm: 45M x 1 = 45M. Cold: 450M / 7 = 64M. Total = about 230M fetches a day.
  3. Fetch rate 230M / 86,400 s = about 2,650 per second on average. Fetching is bursty (retries, catch-up after an outage), so plan for 3x = about 8K per second at peak.
  4. Download bandwidth If we must fetch a whole product page, assume 200 KB compressed. 230M x 200 KB = 46 TB a day = about 4.3 Gbps on average, all pointed at one shop. That number is the strongest argument for an official product API, partner feeds, or prices reported by the extension: a JSON answer of 2 KB is 100x less traffic for both sides.
  5. Price history writes Prices change rarely. Assume 10% of fetches see a new price: 23M changes a day. Store only changes (plus a daily "still the same" point) at about 50 bytes each (product id, condition, price, currency, timestamp, source). 23M x 50 B = about 1.2 GB a day, or about 420 GB a year before compression. Small. A time-series store with columnar compression shrinks it much further (TimescaleDB documents up to 98% on compressed chunks).
  6. Watches 20M registered users x 5 watches each = 100M watches. At about 100 bytes each that is 10 GB: it fits in one sharded relational database, and the hot part fits in memory.
  7. Chart reads 1M extension users x 20 product pages a day = 20M lookups a day = about 230 per second, peak about 1K per second. Each lookup reads one product's history. Cacheable, because history changes at most once per fetch.
  8. Alerts Assume 2% of watches fire on a normal day: 2M notifications a day, about 25 per second. The case study above uses the same 2M per day, but warns that a flash sale can produce tens of millions of alert events in minutes. That burst, not the average, sizes the alert path.

What the numbers tell you: storage is small. The two real problems are (1) the fetch budget - which products to look at and how often, without being rude or blocked - and (2) the alert burst when one popular product drops and 100,000 watchers must be found and notified at once.

Stock variant numbers

The open write-up "Designing smart notification of stock price changes" (puncsky, on GitHub) sizes it at about 3M users tracking about 5,250 stocks (its assumption). The symbol set is tiny, so all thresholds for all symbols fit in memory. But the price changes many times per second per symbol during market hours, so the match rate is the problem: 5,000 symbols x 10 updates per second (our assumption) = 50K price events per second, each checked against that symbol's thresholds. In-memory sorted structures per symbol make each check O(log n) plus the alerts it fires.

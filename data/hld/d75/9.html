LevelWhat the answer looks like
SDE-1 answer looks like..."RTMP ingest, transcode into several qualities, cut into HLS segments, serve through a CDN, the player switches quality, and a playback API checks the subscription and returns a signed link that expires."
SDE-2 adds..."Latency budget with LL-HLS parts and blocking reload, origin shield with request coalescing and token-free cache keys, signed cookies validated at the edge, scheduled pre-scaling and CDN-cacheable APIs for match start, and a viewer counter aggregated regionally and read via CDN instead of pushed."
How do you generate secure, expiring access links for subscribed users in real time?

At play time the app calls a stateless playback API. It checks the user's entitlement (from a per-user cache filled when the subscription was bought), device limit and region, then signs a policy - path prefix of the match, expiry ~10 minutes, optional IP/device binding - as a signed cookie or token. The CDN edge verifies the signature and expiry locally for every segment. The player refreshes before expiry; revocation is "refuse the next refresh".

How to answer

Stress that the per-segment check is local to the CDN (no backend call) - that is what makes it scale to millions.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why a cookie rather than a signed URL? (HLS = many files; CloudFront recommends cookies for that)
  • How do you stop link sharing? (short TTL, device binding, concurrent-session limits, DRM/watermarking)
30 million users open the app in the 10 minutes before a final. What breaks first and how do you prepare?

Not the video - the CDN carries it. What breaks is the control plane: login, home page, entitlement and token APIs (~50K/s). Prepare by pre-scaling on the schedule with a buffer (reactive scaling is minutes too slow), scaling nodes in steps, moving cacheable APIs (scores, home rails) to the CDN and lowering their refresh rates, pre-issuing playback tokens on the match page with jittered refresh, long-lived sessions to avoid re-login, and a degrade plan that turns off non-essential features first.

How to answer

Separate video plane from control plane in one sentence, then list preparations in time order: days before, minutes before, during.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the forecast is wrong by 2x? (headroom, emergency capacity, admission control)
Give the FR, NFR and estimates for a Twitch-like service.

FR: go live with a stream key, watch with adaptive quality, channel pages, chat, follow/subscribe, recording. NFR: 2-5 s latency, smooth playback, high availability, scale to hundreds of thousands of channels and millions of viewers. Estimates: 100K live channels x 6 Mbps = 600 Gbps ingest; transcoding is the cost centre, so transcode the popular ~10%; egress dominated by a few big channels and served from the CDN; recording at ~6.5 PB/day if everything were kept, so keep only partners long-term.

How to answer

Compute both halves separately and point to the dominant cost (transcoding for Twitch, egress and control plane for cricket).

Likely follow-ups
  • Which microservices? (channel, stream/ingest control, transcoding scheduler, playback/entitlement, chat, counter, recording/VOD)
Describe the microservices and DB architecture for streams and channels.

Channel service (SQL: channels, owners, metadata), stream control (stream sessions, which ingest/origin, state; SQL + cache), stream-key auth (KV point lookups at the edge), transcoding scheduler (capacity per origin), playback/entitlement (SQL truth + KV cache), counter (Redis + regional aggregators), chat (its own store and real-time fan-out), recording (S3 + VOD catalog). Video bytes are on origin servers and object storage, never in a DB. Stream lifecycle events on Kafka connect them loosely.

How to answer

For each service give its store and why. Make "video never touches the DB" explicit.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where does a viewer's quality data go? (QoE beacons to Kafka, dashboards, CDN steering)
Design the concurrent-viewer counter. Read fan-out or write fan-out?

Writes: heartbeats every 30 s (~1.7M/s at 50M viewers) are aggregated per edge/region as "sessions seen in the last 60 s per stream", then summed globally every 5-10 s - sharded counters, never one key. Reads: write fan-out (pushing the number to 50M sockets every 5 s) is ~10M messages/s for a vanity number. Prefer read fan-out through the CDN: publish a tiny cached count object with a 5 s TTL, or piggyback it on the playlist/metadata the player already fetches. Accept approximate, a few seconds stale.

How to answer

Quantify both options, then choose. Explain why you count recent heartbeats, not joins minus leaves.

Likely follow-ups
  • Unique viewers over the match? (HyperLogLog per region, merged)
  • The count must be exact for advertisers? (separate offline pipeline from beacons in Kafka)
How do you get latency down from 20 seconds to about 3 seconds?

Most of the delay is segment duration times the player's buffer. Use short segments with low-latency parts (LL-HLS partial segments or CMAF chunks for LL-DASH), blocking playlist reload so players learn about a new part the moment it exists, preload hints, fast single-pass encoding and low-latency encoder settings, and a smaller player buffer. Keep HTTP so the CDN still works. Accept more rebuffering on poor networks; pick the latency target per product.

How to answer

Draw the latency budget and attack the biggest bar first. Mention WebRTC only to explain why you do not use it for millions.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does LL-HLS cost the CDN? (many more, smaller requests; held playlist requests)
How is live different from VOD (Netflix) in your design?

Transcoding must be real-time and single-pass instead of offline and multi-pass; segments cannot be pre-positioned on edges; playlists slide; traffic arrives synchronized at the same second; failures cannot be retried later, so encoders and paths are redundant; and the key latency is delay behind real time, not startup time.

How to answer

A table of 5-6 rows wins this question fast. Link back to what you would reuse from VOD (ABR, CDN, DRM).

How does the origin survive when a new segment appears and 300K players request it at once?

Layers of caching with request coalescing: the edge holds concurrent misses for the same object and makes one upstream fetch; an origin shield does the same for all edges in a region; so the origin sees about one read per segment per shield. Segments are immutable (long TTL), playlists short TTL. The cache key is the path only - the auth token is checked but not part of the key.

How to answer

Do the arithmetic: 740K viewers to 1 origin read. Name the bug of tokens in the cache key - interviewers love it.

Transcoding every stream is too expensive. What do you do?

Transcode selectively: full ladder for partners and channels above a viewer threshold, source-only (or a short ladder) for the long tail; promote a stream to full transcoding when its audience grows. Use hardware encoders (GPU/ASIC) for density - Twitch reported a hardware transcoder giving 10x capacity at 2x cost. Place streams on origins with spare capacity at start time; never migrate a running stream.

How to answer

Quote the 2-3% historical figure to show the business reality, then give the policy.

Where do chat and reactions fit without hurting the video path?

As a completely separate real-time system: WebSocket gateways, a pub/sub per stream, sampling and aggregation of reactions ("+10K hearts" rather than 10K messages), and rate limits per user. It can fail or degrade (slow mode, sampling) without touching playback. Details in Live Comments & Reactions (d44).

How to answer

Say "separate failure domain" and point at the existing design.

Scenario: kickoff, playback fails for 20% of users

At match start, 20% of viewers get errors when pressing play. CDN dashboards look healthy.

Weak answer: "Add more CDN capacity."

Strong answer: Healthy CDN plus failing "play" points to the control plane - most likely the playback/entitlement API is saturated or the entitlement cache is cold and requests fall through to the subscription DB. Immediate: serve entitlement from cache with a longer TTL, extend existing tokens' refresh grace, shed non-essential APIs, and scale the playback tier. Next time: pre-warm the entitlement cache for all pass holders before the match, pre-issue tokens on the match page, schedule pre-scaling, and load-test the token path at 2x forecast.

Likely follow-ups
  • What metric would have warned you? (token API p99 and error rate, entitlement cache hit ratio)

Scenario: viewers complain the stream is 30 s behind TV

Social media spoils every wicket for app users.

Weak answer: "Switch to WebRTC."

Strong answer: Measure the budget first: encoder buffer, segment length, playlist update, CDN, player buffer. Likely 6 s segments and a 3-segment buffer. Move to 2 s segments with LL-HLS parts and blocking reload, tune encoder low-latency settings, cut the default player buffer, and A/B test rebuffer rate. Keep a "smooth mode" with more buffer for poor networks.

Common mistakes: sending video through your own servers or Kafka; one number for "scale" instead of streams vs viewers; forgetting the control-plane spike at kickoff; signed URL per segment instead of a cookie/token; tokens in the cache key; pushing the viewer count to every client; counting viewers as joins minus leaves; no encoder redundancy; reactive-only autoscaling for a scheduled event.

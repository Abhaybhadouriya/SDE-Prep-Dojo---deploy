Interview: drive the 45 minutes

Google interviewers report this as one of their favourite prompts (often scoped down for L4). Uber asks the ETA half. Microsoft folks built Bing Maps on CRP. Flipkart SDE-2 reports include a "school bus navigation" HLD - the same building blocks.

  1. 0-5 min: scope. Driving directions + live traffic + ETA + map display. Say what you drop.
  2. 5-10 min: numbers. Location ping QPS, tile QPS (CDN), route QPS, graph fits in RAM.
  3. 10-15 min: API + data. z/x/y tiles, POST route, batched locations, push channel.
  4. 15-25 min: high-level diagram. Read path vs write path, like the stepper above.
  5. 25-40 min: deep dives. Pick routing (CH vs CRP) and live traffic/ETA. Mention map matching.
  6. 40-45 min: failures + monitoring. Traffic pipeline lag, ETA error (MAE) dashboards, fallback to history.
LevelWhat the answer looks like
SDE-1 answer looks like..."Tiles come from a CDN, routes come from a routing service that keeps the road graph in memory and uses precomputed shortcuts, and phones send batched GPS points to Kafka, where a stream job turns them into live road speeds."
SDE-2 adds..."I choose CRP over plain CH because traffic re-weights the graph every minute, explain HMM map matching, correct the routing ETA with an ML residual model, shard the graph by region with a coarse overlay, and fall back to historical speeds when the pipeline lags."
Why not just run Dijkstra on the road graph for every request?

Dijkstra explores outward by distance and, on a continental graph with tens of millions of junctions, a long query touches millions of nodes and takes seconds. With tens of thousands of route QPS you would need enormous CPU. Speed-up techniques do heavy work offline (minutes to hours) so the online query visits only a few hundred nodes and finishes in about a millisecond.

How to answer

Give the number (seconds vs milliseconds), name the ladder (A*, bidirectional, CH, CRP), and say the trade: preprocessing time and memory for query speed.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does a shortcut store so you can draw the real path?
  • How does A* pick its heuristic on a road network?
Contraction hierarchies are fast, but traffic changes every minute. How do you handle that?

Separate topology from metric. CH bakes weights into the node order and shortcuts, so traffic would force a slow re-contraction. Customizable Route Planning (and Multi-Level Dijkstra, and customizable CH) do a slow metric-independent step once (graph partition into nested cells), then a fast customization step per metric that recomputes only cell-boundary shortcut costs. The CRP paper reports about 1 s on 12 cores for all of Europe, with queries under 2 ms - fast enough to refresh traffic every minute. Between refreshes you can also overlay a small set of "very changed" edges.

How to answer

State the tension, then the topology/metric split, then the number. Mention it is the published basis of Bing Maps routing.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you swap the new weights in without pausing queries? (double-buffer: build new metric arrays, then atomic pointer swap)
  • How do you support "avoid tolls" or truck routing? (another metric, same topology)
How do you turn raw GPS points into traffic speeds?

Phones send batched points to a stateless ingest service that writes to Kafka keyed by region. A stream job map-matches each trip with an HMM + Viterbi to get the road segment sequence, computes the time spent on each segment, and aggregates per segment per one-minute window (count, mean, percentiles), dropping outliers like parked cars. The results update a live-speed store and a history store. Keys are anonymous trip ids; raw points expire quickly.

How to answer

Walk the pipeline left to right; name map matching explicitly - most candidates forget it.

Likely follow-ups
  • A road has only 2 cars in the window - trust it? (blend with history weighted by sample count)
  • How do you avoid double-counting a phone that retries a batch? (sequence numbers per session; idempotent aggregation)
How would you predict a good ETA, not just a shortest-path time?

Two stages. The routing engine computes a physical ETA from predicted segment speeds (history for this time of week blended with live speeds, with more weight on the recent weeks). Then an ML model predicts the residual between that ETA and real arrivals, using features such as origin and destination cells, time, live traffic and request type. This is Uber's DeepETA pattern; Google adds graph neural networks over Supersegments. Serve the model in a few milliseconds, retrain often, and track mean absolute error by city.

How to answer

Do not jump straight to "use ML". Start from the graph ETA, show where it fails (future build-up, turns, signals), then add the residual model.

Likely follow-ups
  • Is under-predicting worse than over-predicting? (often yes for users - Uber uses an asymmetric loss)
  • How do you handle a new city with no history?
How are map tiles stored and served at global scale?

Pre-generate tiles per zoom level using z/x/y addressing (4z tiles per level), store them in object storage keyed by map version + z/x/y, and serve through a CDN with long TTLs. Vector tiles reduce size and let clients restyle. Updates rebuild only changed tiles and bump their version. Origin load is just cache misses.

How to answer

Do the storage maths (trillions of tiles at deep zoom, most empty), then say "the URL is the cache key".

Likely follow-ups
  • Generate tiles on the fly or precompute? (precompute busy areas, lazily render rare deep-zoom ones)
  • How does the client avoid downloading the same tile twice? (ETag / local cache)
How do you tell 2 million active drivers about a new jam without scanning all of them?

Maintain an index from road segment to active sessions whose upcoming route uses that segment (only the next stretch, refreshed as they drive). When a segment's speed changes beyond a threshold, look up those sessions only, recompute ETA, and reroute if it got meaningfully worse. Push via the connection server that owns each WebSocket. Add hysteresis so routes do not flip-flop.

How to answer

It is an inverted index + pub/sub problem; say "fan-out by segment" and give the threshold idea.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if all rerouted drivers jam the alternative road?
  • What happens when the phone loses signal in a tunnel?
How do you shard the road graph across routing servers?

Shard by geography: each routing cluster holds a region (plus a margin) in memory, and a coarse top-level overlay of the whole world (the top cells in CRP terms) handles long trips. A router in front sends a request to the shard that contains both points, or to the global overlay service if it crosses regions. Replicate each shard for availability; it is read-only between metric updates, so replicas never conflict.

How to answer

Show you checked memory first ("the world fits in tens of GB") - you shard for blast radius and locality, not because you must.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you deploy a new map version? (blue/green per shard, readiness after load)
Geohash, quadtree, S2 or H3 - which spatial index for map data?

For "places near me" and partitioning pings, any cell scheme works: geohash is simple (string prefix = nearby, length 6 is about 1.2 km x 0.6 km), quadtrees adapt to density, S2 and H3 have nicer geometry. Uber picked H3 because hexagon neighbours are all the same distance away, which smooths gradients for surge and supply-demand. For routing itself you do not use these - you use graph partitions.

How to answer

Name the use, then the index. See Geo & Proximity pattern.

Likely follow-ups
  • What breaks at geohash cell edges? (query the 8 neighbours too)
A road is closed or a new road opens. How do the map tiles and the routing graph pick it up?

Treat the change as a new map version, not a hot patch. (1) The edit lands in the map-data store with a version number. (2) An incremental build re-renders only the tiles whose bounding boxes touch the changed segment - at every zoom level where the road is drawn (a handful of tiles at high zoom, one tile at low zoom). (3) New tiles get new versioned URLs (or the CDN entries for the old ones are purged), so phones and edges fetch fresh copies; old cached tiles simply expire by TTL (time to live: how long a cached copy may be served). (4) For routing, a closure is a weight change (infinite cost), so with CRP it goes through the fast customization step in seconds; a new road changes topology, so it waits for the next full preprocessing run (hours) and goes out as a new graph version, rolled region by region.

How to answer

Split it into two questions: "how do cached tiles get invalidated" (versioned URLs, purge only touched tiles) and "how does routing see it" (weight change vs topology change). Name the time scale of each.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why version tile URLs instead of purging the CDN? (purges are slow and global; a new URL is instantly correct)
  • What does the phone do with tiles it already has offline?
How would you support offline maps and offline navigation?

Let the user download a region pack: vector tiles for the area at the zoom levels needed for driving, plus a compact routing graph for that region (with CH-style shortcuts precomputed on the server, so the phone only runs the fast query) and a slice of the place index for search. Packs are versioned; the app checks for updates on Wi-Fi and downloads only the changed parts. Offline routes use historical speeds stored in the pack; when the phone is online again it switches back to live traffic and re-checks the ETA. The pack size is the main trade-off - vector tiles and a stripped graph keep a city to tens or hundreds of MB instead of GB.

How to answer

List what must live on the device (tiles, graph, search index), say who precomputes what (server preprocesses, phone queries), and how packs stay fresh (versions and deltas).

Likely follow-ups
  • Why vector tiles rather than raster for offline packs?
  • How do you keep the phone's graph and tiles at the same map version?

Scenario: the stadium empties

A cricket final ends. 60,000 people start navigating out of one square kilometre at the same moment. Traffic on three roads collapses and ETAs are wildly wrong for 20 minutes.

Weak answer: "Add more servers to the routing service."

Strong answer: Routing CPU is not the problem; freshness and prediction are. (1) The GPS partitions for that cell are hot - split the cell finer or salt the key so several consumers share the area. (2) Live speeds lag reality because nobody has moved yet: feed known events (scheduled matches) as features so the ETA model expects the surge. (3) Spread drivers across alternatives instead of sending everybody down the same "fastest" road. (4) Watch the per-region ETA-error dashboard to confirm.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you learn about events in advance?
  • Would you change the push rate for drivers in that area?

Scenario: the traffic feed is 10 minutes stale at rush hour

At 6 pm on a weekday the live-speed store for one city stops updating. Kafka consumer lag for that region's gps-points partitions climbs to 10 minutes. Routes still come back, but ETAs are 15-20 minutes too optimistic and drivers are sent into jams.

Weak answer: "Restart the traffic service and wait for it to catch up."

Strong answer: First stop the harm, then fix the cause. (1) Detect it: alert on consumer lag and on "age of newest speed per region", not only on errors. (2) Degrade honestly: when a region's speeds are older than a threshold (say 3 minutes), switch its edge weights to historical speeds for this time of week - at rush hour that is much closer to reality than 10-minute-old free-flow speeds - and widen the ETA range shown to users. (3) Find the cause: a hot partition (one city on one key), a slow map-matching deploy or a stuck consumer; scale out or split the region key so several consumers share it. (4) Catch up without lying: when the consumers drain the backlog, skip to the newest minute for live speeds (old pings are still useful for history, so process them into the lake separately).

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you choose the staleness threshold?
  • Should rerouting pushes pause while traffic data is stale?

Common mistakes: storing the road graph in a SQL table and querying it per edge; forgetting map matching; sending every GPS point as its own request; making traffic strongly consistent; skipping the CDN for tiles; using geohash for routing.

The story

Explain like I'm 5

At the school fair there is one big teddy bear for sale. A teacher stands next to it with a whiteboard that shows the highest price so far: "10 coins, Riya". Children shout their prices. The teacher listens to one child at a time: if the new price is higher than the board, she wipes the board and writes the new price and name; if it is not higher, she says "sorry, too low". Because only the teacher writes on the board, two children can never both be "the winner". Everyone in the hall can see the board. At exactly 3 o'clock the teacher rings a bell, writes "SOLD" and nobody can bid any more. If someone shouts at 2:59:59, the teacher may give everyone two more minutes, so nobody wins just by being the last to shout. Then the winner pays, and if they do not pay, the second-highest child gets a chance.

That is the whole design. The whiteboard is the auction's current high bid, stored in one place. "One child at a time" is serialising bids per auction, either with a database conditional update (compare-and-set) or by sending every bid for an auction through one ordered queue partition. The hall watching the board is real-time updates over WebSockets or SSE. The bell is the auction closer, a scheduler that fires at the end time. The extra two minutes is anti-sniping (a soft close). "Winner pays, else second chance" is the settlement flow.

Amazon asked an SDE2 L5 candidate to design an online auction in a 40-minute HLD: requirements, entities, data model, APIs, scale-out and design choices (2026-02). Meta asked it in an E5 full loop (2025-07). Razorpay asked an SDE-2 (selected) to design it with separate bidder and seller sign-ups, seller listings, Elasticsearch search, and bids with concurrency and conflict resolution so the system stays consistent (2024-11). Links are in the interview section.

Say this early: "The browse side is read-heavy and can be eventually consistent. The bid side is a contention problem on a single hot row: for one auction, bids must be accepted in a single order, and a bid is accepted only if it beats the current high bid and the auction is still open. I will make that one check-and-write atomic per auction and keep everything else asynchronous."

From your world

A conditional update is the database version of the compare-and-swap (CAS) instruction you know from lock-free kernel code: "set high_bid to 120 only if it is still 110". If another CPU (bidder) changed it first, the CAS fails and you re-read. Routing every bid of one auction to one Kafka partition is like RSS pinning a flow to one CPU queue: no cross-core locking, because one consumer owns the flow.

New words, once each: proxy bidding (automatic bidding) means a bidder enters a secret maximum and the system bids for them in small steps, only as much as needed to stay ahead. A bid increment is the minimum step above the current price. Sniping is bidding in the last seconds so others cannot respond. A soft close extends the end time when a late bid arrives. Optimistic locking lets writers proceed and rejects a write if the row changed since it was read. Elasticsearch is a search engine built on an inverted index. CDC (change data capture) streams database changes to other systems.

A strong answer often combines two: the job table is the source of truth that you can always poll, and push is an optimisation on top. If the push is lost, polling still works.

Backpressure: what if jobs arrive faster than you finish them?

A queue hides overload instead of failing fast, which is both its gift and its danger. The AWS Builders' Library article on queue backlogs makes the point with numbers: after a one-hour outage you need roughly double capacity for another hour just to catch up, and if an unthrottled 30-minute spike queues 10x what consumers can handle, the backlog takes about 300 minutes to work off (30 min x 10). Meanwhile every new job waits behind the old ones. Their tools:

  • Measure age, not just depth. "50,000 messages" means nothing without the rate. Alarm on the age of the oldest message (SQS exposes ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage) and autoscale workers on it.
  • Limit what you accept. Per-tenant quotas and a cap on queued jobs per user. When the queue is too deep, return 429 or 503 with Retry-After; the system-design-primer calls this back pressure.
  • Isolate noisy tenants. Shuffle sharding: give each customer a small random subset of queues, so one customer's flood mostly lands on queues others barely share.
  • Drop or sideline stale work. If a job is older than its usefulness (a "price changed" alert from 2 hours ago), move it aside or drop it so fresh work goes first.

Little's Law tells you how many workers you need: concurrency = arrival rate x time per job. The AWS article's example: 100 messages a second at 100 ms each needs about 10 threads; if each starts taking 10 seconds, you need about 1,000. That is why a slow dependency turns into a backlog so quickly.

From your world

The job queue is a NIC receive ring. If the CPU drains it slower than packets arrive, it fills, latency climbs, and eventually you drop. Queue depth is ring occupancy; "age of oldest message" is how long the oldest descriptor has waited. Backpressure (429/503) is TCP flow control shrinking the receive window. Shuffle sharding is RSS spreading flows across queues so one elephant flow does not starve the mice.

Mixed workloads: do not let a 3-hour job block a 3-second one

If thumbnails (2 s) and full-movie transcodes (40 min) share one queue and one worker pool, a burst of movies takes every worker and thumbnails wait an hour. Fix it with separate queues and pools per job class (by type, by size, by priority), each with its own autoscaling. Dropbox ATF does this: one SQS queue per (task type, priority) pair, dedicated worker clusters per task type, and per-type quotas, so one team's tasks cannot starve another's. Within a class, chop big jobs into small chunks (transcode a movie as 10-second segments in parallel) so retries are cheap and workers stay balanced.

Database as the queue?

For modest scale (hundreds of jobs a second) you can skip a separate broker: workers run SELECT ... FROM jobs WHERE status='QUEUED' ORDER BY created_at LIMIT 10 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED in Postgres, which lets many workers grab different rows without blocking each other. You get one system and transactional enqueue for free. It breaks when polling load and row churn start hurting the primary database. Then move to a real queue.

Scheduling, cancellation, progress, results

  • Delayed and recurring jobs (send reminder at 9 AM): store next_run_at, and a scheduler loop moves due jobs onto the queue. Dropbox ATF's store consumer polls for due tasks every couple of seconds and hits a target of 95% of tasks starting within 5 seconds of their scheduled time, at about 9,000 tasks a second. Full design on Distributed Job Scheduler.
  • Cancellation: set status=CANCEL_REQUESTED; the worker checks it at each checkpoint or heartbeat and stops cleanly.
  • Progress and resume: long jobs write checkpoints ("rows 0-4M done") so a retry continues instead of restarting.
  • Results: put big outputs in a blob store with a time-limited signed URL (see Handling Large Blobs); clean up old jobs with a TTL (time to live: an expiry time after which the store deletes the entry by itself, like an ARP entry ageing out).

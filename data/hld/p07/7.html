Interview: phrases, traps and questions

Phrases that score

  • "Accept fast, process async: 202 plus a job ID, the job table is the source of truth."
  • "Delivery is at-least-once, so the handler is idempotent: deterministic output keys and a conditional status update."
  • "Lease with heartbeat, not one giant timeout, so a dead worker's job comes back in seconds."
  • "Retries with exponential backoff and jitter, a max attempt count, then the DLQ with an alarm."
  • "Autoscale workers on queue age, not CPU; separate queues for job classes so short jobs are not stuck behind long ones."

Traps

  • Saying "exactly-once" without explaining how. The honest answer is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects.
  • Writing to the queue first and the DB second, with no plan for the crash in between (use the outbox pattern or record the job first).
  • Storing the output bytes in the job row or the queue message. Store a pointer.
  • Forgetting the user: how do they learn the job is done or failed?
  • Using Kafka as a per-task queue for long jobs without addressing head-of-line blocking.
A user clicks "Export all my data" and it takes 10 minutes. How do you design the API?

POST /exports with an idempotency key returns 202 Accepted and {job_id, status_url} immediately. The API stores a job row (QUEUED) and enqueues the job ID. A worker builds the file, uploads it to object storage, and sets SUCCEEDED with a signed download URL. The client polls GET /jobs/{id} with backoff, and we also send an email or push when it is ready, because the user may close the tab.

How to answer

Name the HTTP status (202), the two resources (create job, read job), where the result lives (blob + signed URL), and the two ways to notify. Mention the idempotency key for double-clicks.

Likely follow-ups
  • How long do you keep the export file? (TTL, e.g. 7 days, lifecycle rule.)
  • What if the user requests a second export while one is running? (Return the existing job.)
A worker crashes halfway through a job. What happens to the job?

The worker held a lease (SQS visibility timeout, or lease_until in the job table). It stops heartbeating, the lease expires, and the job becomes visible again; another worker picks it up. Attempt count goes up. Because the first run may have done part of the work, the handler is idempotent and ideally resumes from a checkpoint. After N attempts the job goes to the DLQ and the status becomes FAILED so the user is told.

How to answer

Lease, expiry, redelivery, idempotency, max attempts, DLQ, user-visible status. In that order.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the worker was only slow, not dead? (Two runs; fencing with lease_owner on the final update; Dropbox-style self-kill after missed heartbeats.)
  • How do you pick the lease length?
How do you choose the visibility timeout for jobs that take 5 seconds to 2 hours?

Do not pick one big number. Start with a short lease (say 1-2 minutes) and have the worker extend it with a heartbeat every 30-60 seconds while it is still making progress. A crash is then detected within about one lease period. SQS documents this approach, with a 30 s default and a 12-hour ceiling from first receive. Better still, split very different job sizes into different queues so each has sane defaults, and chunk 2-hour jobs into smaller checkpointed pieces.

How to answer

Explain the trade-off: too short gives duplicate work, too long gives slow recovery. Heartbeats remove the trade-off.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the heartbeat call itself fails?
  • What if a job needs more than 12 hours on SQS? (Step Functions / split it.)
How do you guarantee a job's side effect (send email, charge card) happens only once?

You cannot get exactly-once delivery; you get at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects. Give each job a stable ID. Before the side effect, check a dedupe table (INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING on job_id); if the insert loses, skip. For external APIs, pass the job ID as the provider's idempotency key (Stripe-style) so the provider dedupes too. Make the final status update conditional on still owning the lease.

How to answer

Say "at-least-once plus idempotency" in the first sentence, then show one concrete mechanism.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where do you store the dedupe records and for how long?
  • What if the email provider has no idempotency key?
Polling, WebSocket or webhook - how should the client learn the job finished?

It depends on who the client is and how long the job takes. A browser waiting 30 seconds: poll with backoff, or SSE for a live progress bar. A mobile user and a 20-minute job: push notification. A partner's server: webhook with a signature and retries, and the receiver dedupes on event ID. In every case keep GET /jobs/{id} as the fallback, so a lost push never loses the result.

How to answer

Give a small decision table, then say the job table stays the source of truth.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you secure webhooks? (HMAC signature, timestamp, replay window.)
  • What if the partner's endpoint is down for a day?
The queue has 2 million jobs and the oldest is 3 hours old. What do you do?

First stop the bleeding: find why consumption slowed (a dependency? a bad deploy? poison messages looping?) - throughput falling is more common than arrivals spiking. Scale workers only if the downstream can take it, otherwise you DDoS your own database. Shed or sideline: move stale or low-priority jobs to a backlog queue and process fresh jobs first; drop jobs a later full sync will replace. Throttle intake per tenant. Afterwards: alarm on age of oldest message, autoscale on it, and separate queues per job class. AWS's Builders' Library notes that recovering from an hour-long outage needs roughly double capacity for another hour.

How to answer

Diagnose, protect downstream, prioritise fresh work, then prevent. Mention Little's Law for sizing.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you process newest-first without losing the old jobs?
  • How would you size the worker pool?
Thumbnails and 40-minute video transcodes share a worker pool, and thumbnails are now slow. Fix it.

Head-of-line blocking across job classes. Give each class its own queue and worker pool with its own autoscaling and maybe its own hardware. Within transcoding, split each video into short segments processed in parallel so no single task is 40 minutes long. Add priority queues if paying users should go first. Dropbox ATF keeps one queue per (task type, priority) with dedicated worker clusters for this reason.

How to answer

Name the problem (head-of-line blocking / noisy neighbour), then isolate, then chunk.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stitch the segments back together? (A final "merge" job when all parts are done: a small DAG.)
  • How do you avoid starving low priority forever?
When would you use Temporal / Step Functions instead of a queue and workers?

When the work is several dependent steps, runs long, and must resume where it stopped: video pipeline, order fulfilment, onboarding with a human approval. A workflow engine records each completed step in an event history; on a crash it replays the code and skips finished steps. It also gives durable timers ("wait 3 days then remind"), per-step retries and compensation for sagas. For a single retry-safe step, a queue is simpler and cheaper.

How to answer

Contrast "one step" vs "many steps with state". Mention the cost (determinism rules, one more system).

Likely follow-ups
  • How does replay work, and why must workflow code be deterministic?
  • How would you build the same with only queues? (State column per step, outbox events.)
Why not just use Kafka as the job queue?

You can for short, uniform jobs at huge volume. But Kafka tracks progress with one offset per partition, so a slow or failing message blocks the rest of its partition, parallelism is limited by partition count, and per-message retry/visibility does not exist - you build retry topics and a DLQ topic yourself. SQS/RabbitMQ give per-message acks, redelivery and DLQs out of the box. A common blend is Slack's: Kafka as the durable intake buffer, a work queue behind it.

How to answer

Log vs queue semantics, then a concrete fix (retry topics) or a hybrid.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do Kafka share groups (queues for Kafka) change this?
  • How do retry topics with delays work?
How do you make "insert job row" and "enqueue job" atomic?

Two writes to two systems cannot be one transaction. Options: (1) transactional outbox - insert the job row and an outbox row in one DB transaction; a relay (or CDC) publishes outbox rows to the queue; (2) make the job table the queue (SKIP LOCKED) at smaller scale; (3) record the job first, enqueue second, and have a sweeper re-enqueue jobs stuck in QUEUED longer than a minute. The queue may then hold duplicates, which idempotent workers already handle.

How to answer

Point out the dual-write problem by name, then give the outbox and the sweeper.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does the relay do if the broker is down?
  • See Distributed Transactions & Event-Driven Data.

Scenario: Black Friday report storm

Sellers on your marketplace can download a sales report. On Black Friday 50,000 sellers click "Generate report" within ten minutes. Each report takes about 2 minutes of one CPU and reads from the orders database, which is also serving checkout.

Weak answer: "Autoscale the workers to 50,000 so everyone gets their report in two minutes."

Strong answer: Little's Law first - 50,000 jobs x 2 min over 10 min means about 10,000 concurrent workers to keep up, and each one hammers the orders DB that checkout needs. So protect checkout: reports read from a read replica or the warehouse, never the primary. Cap worker concurrency to what the replica can take (say 200), and accept that the queue drains over about 8 hours; tell sellers "ready in about N minutes" and email them when done. Dedupe identical requests (same seller, same date range returns the existing job). Pre-compute daily aggregates overnight so most reports are cheap. Alarm on age of oldest message.

Likely follow-ups
  • A big seller's report takes 40 minutes - what changes? (Separate queue, chunked by date.)
  • How do you show an honest ETA? (Position in queue x average duration / workers.)

Scenario: the same email was sent three times

A "send welcome email" job runs on workers reading from SQS. Customers complain they got the email three times. Logs show the job took 50 seconds because the email provider was slow.

Weak answer: "SQS has a bug, switch to FIFO."

Strong answer: the visibility timeout was 30 s (the default) and the job took 50 s, so the message reappeared and a second worker sent it again; that happened twice. Fix both layers: extend the lease with a heartbeat (or set the timeout above the worst case with a call timeout on the provider), and make the send idempotent - record (job_id, 'welcome') before sending and pass the job ID as the provider's idempotency key. Add a metric for receive count above 1.

Likely follow-ups
  • Would FIFO with deduplication fix it? (Only dedupes on send; it does not stop redelivery after a timeout.)

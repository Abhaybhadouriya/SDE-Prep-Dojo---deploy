Authentication vs authorization

Authentication (authN)Authorization (authZ)
QuestionWho are you?What may you do?
EvidencePassword, passkey, OTP, certificate, signed tokenRoles, permissions, ownership, policies
When it failsHTTP 401 Unauthorized (really means "unauthenticated")HTTP 403 Forbidden
HappensOnce per login, then carried by a session or tokenOn every request, for every object touched
Typical placeIdentity provider, login service, API gatewayGateway for coarse rules, each service for object-level rules

You might confuse this with... authN vs authZ: 401 means "I do not know who you are" (log in again); 403 means "I know who you are, and the answer is no" (logging in again will not help). And OAuth 2.0 vs OIDC: OAuth is about access (a valet key for an API: "this app may read your photos"); OIDC is about identity (an ID card: "this is user 42, who logged in with MFA at 10:02"). "Log in with Google" is OIDC on top of OAuth; using a bare OAuth access token as proof of login is a classic bug.

Authorization models

  • RBAC (role-based): users get roles (admin, seller, viewer); roles get permissions. Simple and auditable. Kubernetes RBAC is a good example (K8s Security & RBAC).
  • ABAC (attribute-based): a policy evaluates attributes of user, resource and context: "a doctor may read records of patients in their own hospital during their shift". Flexible, harder to reason about.
  • ReBAC (relationship-based): permission follows a graph: "you can edit this doc because you are in a group that owns its folder". Google Docs-style sharing works like this.
  • Ownership checks: the most common and most forgotten rule: "is this order yours?" Missing it is the number one API risk in the OWASP list below.

Storing passwords: never, ever plain

You store a slow, salted hash, never the password and never a fast hash like SHA-256 alone. A salt is a random value unique to each user, stored next to the hash, so identical passwords hash differently and precomputed tables are useless. A pepper is one extra secret kept outside the database (in a vault or HSM). The OWASP Password Storage Cheat Sheet recommends:

"Slow" is the point: a login that costs 50 ms of CPU is invisible to a user, but makes an attacker with a stolen database billions of times slower. Add MFA (a second factor such as an authenticator app or a passkey), rate-limit login attempts per account and per IP, and alert on credential-stuffing patterns.

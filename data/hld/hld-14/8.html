DDoS protection: surviving a flood

A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack uses many machines (often a botnet of hacked IoT devices) to exhaust your bandwidth, packet processing, connection tables or application capacity. The scale is now enormous. Cloudflare reported mitigating about 21.3 million DDoS attacks in 2024, 53% more than 2023, including a 5.6 Tbps UDP flood from a Mirai-variant botnet of over 13,000 devices that lasted 80 seconds. In mid-May 2025 it reported a 7.3 Tbps attack that delivered 37.4 TB in 45 seconds from over 122,000 source IPs in 161 countries. No single data center can absorb that; the defence has to be distributed too.

From your world

This is your home turf. Cloudflare's write-up of the 7.3 Tbps attack says packets are sampled in the Linux kernel via XDP/eBPF, a heuristic engine builds fingerprints, and matching traffic is dropped by eBPF rules on every server, with anycast spreading the flood across 477 data centers. That is a control-plane / data-plane split you know from routers: ACLs and policers in the fast path, classification in the slow path, and ECMP/anycast to spread load. SYN cookies are stateless connection setup so the SYN queue cannot be exhausted.

  • Hide the origin: only accept traffic from the CDN/edge ranges, so attackers cannot bypass it by hitting your IP directly.
  • Make expensive paths cheap or gated: cache aggressively, require auth before costly searches, put a queue in front of heavy work.
  • Autoscaling is not a DDoS plan: it turns an attack into a bill. Pair it with limits and shedding (see hld-11).

The story

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a giant coat-check room with thousands of hooks. You hand over your coat and get a ticket with a name on it, like "red-umbrella". Later you show the ticket and get your coat back. That's a key-value store: the ticket is the key, the coat is the value.

Now the room is so busy that one helper cannot do it. So there are many helpers standing in a big circle. A magic rule turns each ticket name into a spot on the circle, and the next helper clockwise keeps that coat. To be safe, that helper also gives copies to the next two helpers. If one helper goes home sick, the others still have your coat. If two helpers wrote different notes about your coat while they could not hear each other, they keep both notes and ask you which one is right when you come back.

A key-value store (KV store) has two main operations: put(key, value) and get(key). No joins, no SQL. That simplicity is what lets it scale to millions of requests per second. Amazon built Dynamo in the mid-2000s because the shopping cart had to accept "add to cart" even while servers and network links failed. Their 2007 paper (SOSP) became the blueprint for Cassandra, Riak and Voldemort.

In interviews, "design a key-value store" checks whether you know the core tools of distributed systems: partitioning, replication, quorum, conflict handling, failure detection and repair. Microsoft SDE-2 reports in our gap analysis include an in-memory KV store (a simplified Redis) and a distributed LRU cache; Amazon interviewers expect you to know the Dynamo ideas because they wrote them.

Not the same as a cache. The Distributed Cache page covers Redis-like caches where losing data is acceptable. Here the store is the source of truth: data must survive failures.

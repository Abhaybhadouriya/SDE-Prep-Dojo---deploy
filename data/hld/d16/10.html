How real crawlers do it

  • Mercator (Compaq / DEC research, 1999). The design most interview answers are based on: front and back queues, per-host politeness, a custom DNS resolver, URL-seen tests, and pluggable protocol and processing modules. It is the design taught in the Stanford IR book chapter on crawling.
  • Googlebot. Google's documentation explains how Googlebot fetches, how robots.txt is read, and how "crawl budget" combines a site's capacity with Google's demand for its pages. Google led the effort that turned robots.txt into RFC 9309 (2022).
  • Common Crawl. A non-profit that publishes an open, petabyte-scale archive of crawled pages in WARC format, used widely for research and for training language models.
  • Internet Archive (Heritrix). Heritrix is the Internet Archive's open-source, archival-quality crawler, written in Java, and the origin of much WARC tooling.
  • Apache Nutch is a scalable open-source crawler built around Hadoop-style batch rounds (generate, fetch, parse, update the crawl DB). Scrapy is the popular Python framework for focused, single-site crawls.

Related on this site: Distributed Building Blocks (consistent hashing, queues), Big Data & Probabilistic Structures (Bloom filters), Post Search (what happens to pages after the crawl), Top-K / Heavy Hitters, S3-like Object Storage (where pages land), Design a Message Queue and Distributed Cache (DNS and robots caching).

What went wrong in the real world. In 2024 Read the Docs wrote about AI crawlers abusing their site. One crawler pulled 73 TB of zipped HTML in a month, about 10 TB in a single day, fetching the same files again and again because of a bug and no support for ETag or Last-Modified. That cost them over $5,000 in bandwidth. They noticed it as a spike on their bandwidth charts. Blocking by IP did not work because the crawlers used many addresses, so they blocked whole classes of bots. The lessons for your crawler are on this page: a URL-seen test and recrawl scheduling so you never refetch unchanged files, conditional GETs (If-None-Match, If-Modified-Since), per-host rate limits enforced in the frontier, an honest user agent with a working contact address, and a dashboard of bytes per host per day that alerts long before the site owner does.

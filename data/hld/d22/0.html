The story

Explain like I'm 5

Your class plays a game every day. On the wall there is a magic scoreboard. The moment anyone wins a point, their name slides up to the right place by itself - nobody has to rub everything out and rewrite the whole list. Anyone can walk up and ask three questions: "Who are the top 10?", "What place am I?" and "Who is just above and just below me?" The scoreboard answers instantly. At the end of each month the teacher takes a photo of the board, puts it in a drawer, and starts a fresh one.

That is the whole product. The hard part is the word instantly when there are millions of names. If you stored players in a normal database table and asked "what place am I?", the database would have to count everyone with a higher score - every time anyone asks. With millions of rows and thousands of score changes per second, that is far too slow.

The trick is a data structure that keeps itself sorted on every update: a sorted set. In Redis (an in-memory data store - think of it as a giant, very fast hash table living in RAM on a server) a sorted set gives you insert, update, "what is my rank?" and "give me positions 0 to 9" all in O(log N) time. Most of this design is "use a Redis sorted set, then handle the things a sorted set does not handle for you": durability, ties, time windows, cheating, retries, and scale beyond one machine.

Reported in the wild: "design a real-time gaming leaderboard" is a full chapter in Alex Xu's System Design Interview Vol. 2, appears in the interview bank as a common Amazon / Microsoft / Google question, and is the classic warm-up for "Redis sorted sets". It is often used for SDE-1 and SDE-2 because it is small enough to finish in 45 minutes but has real depth (ties, sharding, idempotency).

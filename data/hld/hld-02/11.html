Distributed transactions: 2PC vs saga vs outbox

Explain like I'm 5

You are planning a birthday party. You book a cake, balloons and a magician. If the magician cancels, you phone the cake shop and the balloon shop and cancel those too. You never pretend the party is fine when one part failed. That "cancel the earlier steps" plan is a saga.

Inside one database, a transaction is easy: BEGIN ... COMMIT and either everything happens or nothing does. With microservices (small separate services, each with its own database), placing an order touches the Order service, the Payment service and the Inventory service. There is no single COMMIT across three databases. You have three main tools.

The problem first: the dual write

A service saves an order in its database and also publishes an OrderPlaced event to Kafka. These are two separate systems. If the database commit succeeds and the Kafka publish fails (or the process crashes in between), the order exists but nobody downstream hears about it. Do it the other way round and you announce an order that was never saved. This is the dual-write problem.

Tool 1: Two-phase commit (2PC)

In two-phase commit, a coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?" (phase 1, prepare). Each participant locks its rows, writes a "prepared" record and votes yes or no. If all vote yes, the coordinator says "commit" (phase 2); otherwise "abort".

  1. Prepare. The coordinator asks each database to get ready to commit.
  2. Vote. Each one locks the rows, makes the change durable as "prepared", and votes YES. After voting YES it is not allowed to change its mind on its own.
  3. Commit. All YES, so the coordinator logs "commit" and tells everyone to commit. Everyone releases locks.
  4. The weakness. If the coordinator dies after the votes, participants are stuck: they cannot commit (maybe someone voted no) and cannot abort (maybe the coordinator decided commit). They hold locks until it recovers. That is why 2PC is called a blocking protocol.

2PC is real and useful inside one system - Kafka transactions use a two-phase style commit internally with a transaction coordinator, and databases use XA (the standard interface that lets a database join a 2PC run by an outside coordinator). Across independent microservices owned by different teams it is rare: it couples their availability (one slow service holds locks in all the others) and many modern stores and brokers do not take part in XA at all.

Tool 2: Saga

A saga is a chain of local transactions. Each step commits in its own database and then triggers the next step. Every step has a compensating action that undoes it in business terms (release the stock, cancel the order, refund a payment). If step 3 fails, run the compensations for steps 2 and 1.

Order the steps on purpose. Put the most likely failures and the easiest-to-undo steps first, and the hard-to-undo step as late as possible. Holding stock is cheap and reversible (the hold simply expires); taking money is not. So the charge is the pivot step: once it succeeds, the saga should only move forward, and a refund is needed only if a later step (such as shipping) fails.

There are two styles:

Orchestration
  • A central orchestrator (for example Temporal or AWS Step Functions: workflow engines that save the state of every step durably and retry it) tells each service what to do and tracks the state.
  • Easy to see the whole flow and to debug.
  • Pick this when there are more than 3-4 steps.
Choreography
  • No boss: each service listens to events (on Kafka) and reacts, emitting its own events.
  • Loosely coupled, nothing central to scale.
  • Hard to see the flow; cycles and "who undoes what" get messy as steps grow.

Careful: a saga is not isolated. Between the stock hold and its release, another shopper can see that item as "out of stock" for an order that will never be paid. Design for that: use clear states such as PENDING, make compensations idempotent, and remember some steps cannot be undone (an email already sent) - put those last.

Tool 3: Transactional outbox (the fix for dual writes)

Instead of writing to the database and Kafka, write only to the database (the outbox pattern). In the same local transaction, insert the business row and an event row into an outbox table. A separate relay reads new outbox rows and publishes them to Kafka. Either both rows commit or neither does, so the event can never be lost. The relay is usually CDC (change data capture: reading the database's own change log, for example with Debezium) or a simple poller.

BEGIN;
INSERT INTO orders (id, user_id, total_paise, status)
VALUES ('ord_81f2', 42, 99900, 'PENDING');

INSERT INTO outbox (id, aggregate_type, aggregate_id, type, payload)
VALUES (gen_random_uuid(), 'order', 'ord_81f2', 'OrderPlaced',
        '{"orderId":"ord_81f2","userId":42,"totalPaise":99900}');
COMMIT;   -- both rows or neither

The relay can crash after publishing but before recording progress, so the same event may be published twice. That is why every consumer keeps an inbox (a table of processed event IDs) or makes its writes idempotent - see Idempotency keys earlier on this page. See Distributed Transactions & Event-Driven Data and Pattern: Multi-step Processes for the full treatment, and CDC pipeline for Debezium in a design.

From your world

Saga compensation is a config rollback. A "commit confirmed" on a router applies the change and automatically rolls back if you do not confirm in time; you do not lock every box in the network while deciding. 2PC is the opposite: every box holds a candidate config locked until the coordinator says go. Where this breaks: a config rollback restores the exact previous state; a business compensation (a refund) is a new action that leaves a trace, and some actions (an SMS) cannot be undone at all.

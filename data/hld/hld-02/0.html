Explain like I'm 5

Five friends live in five different towns. They keep one shared notebook of "who owes whom a chocolate". Each friend has a copy. The post is slow and sometimes a letter is lost.

Soon they hit problems. Two friends write different things on the same line (who is right?). One friend is on holiday, so does the notebook stop (how many must agree?). Someone must be the boss of the notebook (how do we choose?). A letter is sent twice by mistake, and nobody should get two chocolates (do it once). The notebook gets huge, so they want quick tricks to ask "have I seen this name before?" or "which name appears most?" (cheap counting). And when one friend sends letters too fast, the others need a polite way to say "slow down" (rate limits and backpressure).

Every big website has exactly these problems. The tools on this page are the answers people found.

A distributed system is many computers that work together and look like one system to the user. They talk over a network that can be slow, can drop messages and can split into islands. A building block is a small, reusable idea that solves one of these problems. You do not invent new ones in an interview. You pick the right block, say why, and say what it costs.

From your world

You already build distributed systems: a chassis with a route processor and twenty line cards is one. Hellos and dead timers (BFD, OSPF) are failure detection. VRRP is leader election. A token-bucket policer is a rate limiter. Pause frames and the TCP window are backpressure. This page uses those same ideas, but across many servers in many data centres. Where this breaks: inside a chassis the backplane rarely partitions and the clocks are close. Between data centres both of those assumptions fail, and that is what makes the web-scale versions harder.

Back-of-envelope estimation

The numbers below are our own round assumptions for a large single-region store - say them out loud as assumptions. The hard-disk figure is real: a hard drive still does about 120 random I/O operations per second (Werner Vogels, Amazon CTO, 2023), and that one fact drives half the design. (Method: Back-of-Envelope Estimation.)

  1. Logical data. Assume 100 PB of customer data.
  2. Object count. Assume an average object of 1 MB (many tiny thumbnails and logs, a few huge videos). 100 PB / 1 MB = 100 billion objects.
  3. Metadata size. About 300 bytes per object (bucket, key, version, size, checksum, placement, timestamps, ACL ref). 100 billion x 300 B = 30 TB of metadata. Too big for one machine, small for a cluster: shard it over about 100 nodes at 300 GB each, each shard replicated 3 times.
  4. Raw capacity - replication. 3 full copies = 100 PB x 3 = 300 PB raw.
  5. Raw capacity - erasure coding 8+4. Erasure coding k+m: cut the object into k data pieces plus m parity pieces computed from them; any k of the k+m pieces rebuild the object (like RAID-6, but across machines). Here k = 8, m = 4: 12 shards for 8 shards of data = 1.5x, and any 4 can be lost. 100 PB x 1.5 = 150 PB raw. That saves 150 PB.
  6. Drives. With 20 TB drives: 300 PB / 20 TB = 15,000 drives for replication vs 150 PB / 20 TB = 7,500 drives for 8+4.
  7. Request rate. Assume 100,000 GET/s and 20,000 PUT/s on average.
  8. Disk IOPS budget. 7,500 drives x 120 IOPS = 900,000 random IOPS for the whole fleet. If every 1 MB GET read all 8 data shards (8 disk seeks), 100,000 GET/s would need 800,000 IOPS - nearly the whole fleet. So small objects must be readable with one seek (deep dive 3).
  9. Bandwidth. 100,000 GET/s x 1 MB = 100 GB/s, about 800 Gbit/s egress; PUTs add 20 GB/s in, times 1.5 inside the cluster for parity.

From your world

You already know parity from RAID. RAID-5 stores one parity block per stripe (survives 1 disk), RAID-6 stores two (survives 2). Erasure coding is the same idea with m parity pieces instead of 1 or 2, and the pieces live on different servers and zones instead of disks in one box. The price is the same too: rebuilding a lost piece means reading k surviving pieces over the network.

Deep dives

1. Recurring events: store the rule, materialise a window

There are three ways to store "every Tuesday".

ApproachHowGoodBreaks when
Store every occurrenceOne row per TuesdaySimple range queries"Forever" series have no end; editing the series rewrites thousands of rows.
Store only the rule, expand on every readOne row; compute instances at query timeTiny storage, edits are cheapEvery week view runs the rule engine for every series; free/busy for 50 people becomes slow.
Hybrid (pick this)Rule is the truth; a background job materialises the next 6-12 months into an indexFast reads, cheap edits, bounded rowsQueries beyond the window must expand on demand (rare: few people view 2028).

Educative's design uses this hybrid, materialising a rolling window of about 6 to 12 months and expanding later dates on demand. A nightly job extends the window by one day for every series. Use a proven library for the rule engine (python-dateutil, rrule.js, iCal4j); RRULE has corner cases such as "the 31st of every month" that skip short months.

Editing a series. Three cases, and interviewers love to ask them:

  • Only this occurrence: write an event_exceptions row keyed by its original start. The rule is untouched.
  • This and following: split the series. Set UNTIL on the old rule to just before the split point, and create a new series starting there with the new values. This keeps history correct.
  • All occurrences: update the master. Do not silently wipe exceptions; re-apply them where they still make sense (an exception that only moved the location can survive a title change).

2. Time zones and daylight saving time

The rule that saves you: store recurring events in wall-clock time plus an IANA zone name (for example 09:00 America/New_York), and compute UTC per occurrence. For a one-off event, storing UTC plus the original zone is fine. Google's API even requires a single time zone for a recurring event, "to expand the recurrences of the event" (Events and calendars guide).

Why it matters, with real arithmetic. A New York manager runs a weekly 09:00 meeting with a Bengaluru engineer. India has no daylight saving time; New York does.

  • Keep the tz database (tzdata) updated on every server; governments change DST rules with little notice, and each change means re-expanding affected series and rescheduling reminders.
  • Handle the gaps: 02:30 does not exist on a spring-forward day, and 01:30 happens twice on a fall-back day. Pick a documented rule (shift forward; take the first occurrence).
  • All-day events are dates, not instants. "Diwali, 1 Nov" must stay 1 Nov for everyone, whatever their zone. Google notes the timezone field has no meaning for all-day events.

Stop here for SDE-1. "Store the RRULE once and materialise a window of occurrences" and "store wall-clock time plus an IANA zone, compute UTC per occurrence" are the interview-sufficient core, together with the reminder spike from the estimate. 3. Reminders on the exact minute: a timer wheel at scale

The naive design is a cron job that runs SELECT * FROM reminders WHERE fire_at <= now() every minute. It works for a thousand users and falls over at ~140,000 reminders per second. The scalable shape is a time-bucketed delay queue:

  1. Bucket by minute, spread by shard. Key each reminder by (minute_bucket, shard) with, say, 1,024 shards. The 09:50 spike becomes 1,024 small partitions instead of one hot row range.
  2. Workers own shards. Each ticker worker leases a set of shards (via a lease in ZooKeeper/etcd, or Kubernetes-based partition assignment as Dynein supports). Every few seconds it reads "due now or overdue" rows for its shards.
  3. Pre-load slightly early. Workers read the next minute's bucket ~30-60 s ahead into memory and fire at the exact second. That absorbs DB latency during the spike.
  4. Hand off, do not deliver. The worker publishes to a reminders-due topic; the notification service does the slow work (APNs/FCM push, email) with retries. The ticker stays fast.
  5. Claim safely. Use a conditional update ("mark sent if still pending") so two workers never both send. Dynein uses exactly this optimistic, conditional-update approach to avoid duplicates (InfoQ on Dynein).
  6. Only the next occurrence. Store one pending reminder per series and attendee. When it fires, compute and insert the next one. A "forever" series never creates more than one row.

From your world

This is the Linux kernel timer wheel scaled out. The kernel hashes timers into slots by expiry jiffy so that adding or expiring a timer is O(1) instead of keeping one giant sorted list. Our minute buckets are the slots, the shards are per-CPU timer bases, and "only keep the next occurrence" is like a periodic hrtimer that re-arms itself in its own callback.

4. Invites, fan-out and free/busy

The organizer's calendar holds the master copy. Google's docs say each event has exactly one organizer, "the calendar that holds the main copy". Attendees get references (a row in their instance index pointing at the master) plus their own RSVP and personal reminders. This keeps a single truth for time and title while letting each person hide, colour or set reminders privately.

  • Small invites (most meetings, under ~50 people): fan out on write. When the event changes, update every attendee's view index asynchronously through Kafka.
  • Huge invites (a 20,000-person all-hands): fan-out-on-write means 20,000 index writes per edit. Switch to fan-out on read: store the event once against a group, and when a member opens their week, merge in events of groups they belong to. This is the same push vs pull trade-off as a news feed; see Fan-out (Push vs Pull).
  • External guests get an iCalendar invite by email (iTIP methods REQUEST and REPLY, defined in RFC 5546). Their reply email updates the RSVP.

Free/busy reads only start, end and a busy flag from the instance index, never titles, so it can be served from a small, separate, cache-friendly projection. Merging N people's busy intervals is a classic interval merge: sort all intervals by start and sweep, O(K log K) for K intervals. Microsoft Graph's findMeetingTimes goes one step further and scores each candidate slot: free counts as a 100% chance of attendance, unknown 49%, busy 0%, and the slot's confidence is the average across attendees (Graph docs).

5. Multi-device sync

Each calendar has an append-only change_log with a per-calendar sequence number. A sync token is just an encoded "last seq I saw". The device asks "changes after seq 1,042", applies them (including deletions, so it can remove local copies), and stores the new token. If the log has been compacted past the device's token, the server answers 410 and the device does a full resync, the same contract Google documents. For push, the server sends a tiny "something changed" signal over a WebSocket or mobile push; the device then pulls with its token. Pushing whole events to every device would be wasteful and racy.

From your world

Sync tokens behave like OSPF link-state sequence numbers or a BGP route refresh. A neighbour that is only slightly behind gets incremental updates; one that is too far behind (or lost state) gets a full table dump. The 410 is your "full resync" trigger.

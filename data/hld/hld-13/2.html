SLI, SLO, SLA and error budgets

Google's SRE book gave the industry three precise words. Learn them exactly, because interviewers notice when you mix them up.

TermWhat it isExampleWho owns it
SLI (service level indicator)A measured number about the serviceShare of HTTP requests that return non-5xx in under 300 msEngineering (it is a metric)
SLO (service level objective)A target for an SLI over a window99.9% of requests good, measured over 30 daysEngineering + product
SLA (service level agreement)A contract with a consequence if the SLO is missedBelow 99.9% monthly, customer gets service creditsBusiness and legal

The SRE book's test: if missing the target has no explicit consequence, it is an SLO, not an SLA. Keep your internal SLO tighter than the public SLA, so you get warned before you owe anyone money.

You might confuse this with... SLI vs SLO vs SLA: the SLI is the thermometer reading (99.95% good requests this month), the SLO is the number you promised yourselves (99.9%), and the SLA is the number written in a contract with a penalty (for example 99.5%, or service credits). Measure the SLI, aim for the SLO, pay on the SLA. A second pair people mix up is high availability vs fault tolerance: HA means a failure causes a short blip while a standby takes over (seconds of errors are allowed); fault tolerance means the failure is invisible to users (no blip, usually because redundant parts already do the work in parallel, as in a voting flight computer). Most web systems are HA, not fault tolerant.

Choosing good SLIs

  • Measure what users feel. For a request API: availability (good requests / all requests) and latency. For a pipeline: freshness (how old is the newest processed data) and correctness. For storage: durability.
  • Use percentiles, not averages. The SRE book's example: a typical request took about 50 ms but 5% of requests were 20 times slower. The average hides that. Set targets like "p99 under 200 ms". (p99 = the latency that 99% of requests beat; 1 request in 100 is slower. p50 is the median, p99.9 is 1 in 1,000. See the glossary.)
  • Few and simple. The book advises as few SLOs as possible, not copied from current performance, and starting loose then tightening.
  • Do not aim for 100%. It is impossible, it freezes change, and users cannot tell 99.999% from 100% when their own Wi-Fi is worse.

The error budget

An error budget is 1 minus the SLO. With a 99.9% SLO you are allowed 0.1% bad requests. Over 30 days (43,200 minutes) that equals about 43.2 minutes of full outage. The budget turns a fight ("ship faster" vs "be careful") into a shared number: while budget remains, ship features; when it is gone, the error budget policy says freeze risky launches and spend time on reliability.

Alerting on SLOs: burn rates

Paging on "CPU above 80%" wakes people for things users never notice. The SRE Workbook recommends paging on burn rate: how fast you are spending the error budget. It suggests a "multiwindow, multi-burn-rate" setup, firing only when both a long and a short window agree (the short one, about 1/12 of the long, makes the alert stop quickly once errors stop):

ActionLong windowShort windowBurn rateBudget spent
Page1 hour5 minutes14.42%
Page6 hours30 minutes65%
Ticket3 days6 hours110%
 
# Prometheus-style rule: page when the 1h AND 5m error ratios both exceed 14.4x the budget
# (99.9% SLO -> allowed error ratio 0.001 -> threshold 0.0144)
- alert: CheckoutErrorBudgetFastBurn
  expr: |
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[1h]))
       / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
    and
    (sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout",code=~"5.."}[5m]))
       / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[5m]))) > (14.4 * 0.001)
  labels:
    severity: page

Say this in an interview: "I would set an SLO first, for example 99.9% of checkout requests succeed under 500 ms over 28 days. That gives us about 40 minutes of error budget. I page on fast burn rate, not on CPU, and the budget decides whether we keep shipping." It shows you think like an owner, not just a builder.

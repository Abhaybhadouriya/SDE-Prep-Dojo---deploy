Disaster recovery: RPO, RTO, backups

Disaster recovery (DR) is getting the service and its data back after a big event: a region outage, a cyber attack, or someone running DROP TABLE in production. Two numbers define every DR plan:

  • RPO (recovery point objective): how much data you can afford to lose, measured as time. RPO of 5 minutes means you may lose the last 5 minutes of writes.
  • RTO (recovery time objective): how long the service may be down before it is restored.

You might confuse this with... RPO and RTO are both written in minutes, so people swap them. Memory trick: RPO is about the Point you roll back to (data, looking backward); RTO is about the Time until you are back (downtime, looking forward). Asynchronous replication with 2 s of lag gives an RPO of about 2 s no matter how fast you fail over; a faster failover improves RTO, never RPO. Also do not confuse RTO with MTTR (mean time to repair): RTO is a target you design for; MTTR is what you actually measured across past incidents.

The four DR tiers

The AWS DR whitepaper describes four strategies, from cheap and slow to expensive and fast:

StrategyWhat runs in the DR regionTypical RPO / RTOCost
Backup and restoreOnly backups (snapshots copied cross-region) plus infrastructure as code to rebuildHours / hours (tens of hours for big data)Lowest
Pilot lightData stores replicating live; app servers defined but switched offMinutes / tens of minutesLow
Warm standbyA scaled-down but fully working copy that can take traffic now, then scales upSeconds to minutes / minutesMedium
Multi-site active-active (or hot standby)Full capacity serving traffic in every regionNear zero / near zero (for infrastructure failures)Highest

The whitepaper's own distinction between the middle two is useful: pilot light cannot serve requests until you switch things on; warm standby can serve immediately at reduced capacity. Interviewers like hearing that you would pick the tier per component, not for the whole company: the payments ledger may need warm standby while the analytics warehouse is fine with backup and restore.

Backups: replication is not a backup

  • Replication copies mistakes too. A bad migration or a ransomware encryption replicates to every replica in under a second. AWS notes continuous replication may not protect against corruption or malicious deletion; you also need point-in-time recovery (snapshots plus a log of changes) and versioning.
  • Copy backups across accounts and regions, so one stolen credential or one region outage cannot delete both production and the backup.
  • Back up the recipe, not just the data: infrastructure as code, machine images, configuration and secrets. Without them your RTO balloons while people rebuild by hand.
  • Test restores on a schedule. An untested backup is a hope. Measure how long a full restore takes; that number is your real RTO for data disasters.
  • For data corruption, even active-active gives a non-zero RPO: you restore to a point before the bad write, which may be well before anyone noticed.

Durability vs availability: they are different promises. An object store can be extremely durable (your bytes are not lost) yet briefly unavailable (you cannot read them right now). Say which one you mean. See S3-like Object Storage.

Cells, bulkheads and shuffle sharding: shrink the blast radius

Redundancy helps when a machine dies. It does not help when a bad input, a poison request or a bad deploy hits every copy at once. The fix is to stop sharing. A cell is a complete, independent copy of your stack (app, cache, database) that serves only a slice of customers. A thin cell router maps each request's partition key (customer id, tenant id, city) to its cell. If one cell breaks, only its customers notice. That share is the blast radius. AWS says its service teams have used cell-based architecture for more than a decade, and its guidance frames cells as applying the AZ and region isolation idea inside your own workload (the bulkhead pattern).

Shuffle sharding

AWS's Builders' Library article on shuffle sharding gives a clean example. Take 8 workers. Normal sharding into 4 shards of 2 means one bad customer takes down 25% of capacity. Instead, give each customer its own random pair of workers: there are C(8,2) = 28 possible pairs, and here is the subtle part. The poison customer still takes out its own 2 workers, which is still 25% of capacity (the article is explicit that you can still lose a quarter of the whole service). What shrinks is the number of customers who are fully down: only customers assigned the exact same pair, about 1/28 or 3.6% of them, lose all their workers. Everyone else shares at most one worker with the poison customer, keeps at least one healthy worker, and succeeds by retrying to it. Route 53 uses the same idea with 2,048 virtual name servers and a shard of 4 per domain: C(2048,4) is about 730 billion combinations, enough for every domain to get its own.

Real example: Slack turns AZs into cells

On 2021-06-30 a network link between one AZ and the others in us-east-1 had intermittent faults: a gray failure, where different components disagreed about what was reachable. Slack's response was a cellular architecture: each AZ is a cell, services only talk to servers in their own AZ ("siloing"), and operators can drain an AZ. Their stated goals: remove as much traffic as possible from an AZ within 5 minutes, without user-visible errors, in 1% steps, and even when the AZ is offline. They do it by setting weights on edge Envoy load balancers through their xDS control plane; weight changes spread in seconds and in-flight requests finish. The migration took about a year and a half for the most critical services.

Static stability: design so that a failure needs no new action to survive. If you run in 3 AZs and need 2 AZs' worth of capacity, keep 3 x 50% provisioned, so losing one AZ needs no scaling call to a control plane that may itself be struggling. Pair this with load shedding at the edge (Netflix's Zuul capped traffic during failover) and with circuit breakers from hld-11.

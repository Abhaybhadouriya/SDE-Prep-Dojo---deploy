Chaos engineering and game days

A failover path you never use is a failover path that does not work. Chaos engineering is breaking things on purpose, in a controlled way, to find weaknesses before real outages do. The Principles of Chaos Engineering site lays out the method:

  1. Define steady state as a measurable output users care about: throughput, error rate, latency (your SLIs).
  2. Hypothesize that steady state will hold in both a control group and an experiment group.
  3. Inject real-world events: kill servers, cut network links, add latency, return malformed responses, spike traffic.
  4. Try to disprove the hypothesis by looking for a difference between the groups. A difference is a weakness found cheaply.

The advanced principles add: run in production (only real traffic shows real paths), automate experiments to run continuously, and minimize blast radius (start with a small slice and have an abort button). Netflix popularized this with Chaos Monkey, an open-source tool that randomly terminates instances in production so teams must build services that survive it. Google SRE does a version for dependencies: its SLO chapter tells how global Chubby (a lock service) was so reliable that other teams assumed it never failed, so SRE kept it near its SLO and, if a quarter passed without a real outage, staged a controlled one to flush out unsafe dependencies.

A game day is a planned exercise where a team simulates a disaster (evacuate a region, restore a database from backup) and measures real RPO and RTO against the targets.

Lessons from a real outage: AWS us-east-1, 7 December 2021

AWS's public post-event summary describes an automated capacity-scaling activity at 7:30 AM PST that triggered a surge of connections from many clients on its internal network. The surge congested the devices between the internal and main networks, and a latent bug stopped clients from backing off, so retries kept the congestion going. The core network did not fully recover until 2:22 PM, roughly 7 hours, and some dependent services took until the evening. AWS also noted its own monitoring and support tools depended on the impaired network, which slowed diagnosis and communication. Three lessons to quote in interviews:

  • Retries without backoff and jitter turn a blip into an outage. Cap retries, add exponential backoff with jitter, and use retry budgets (see hld-11).
  • Monitoring must not share fate with what it monitors. Host your status page and alerting outside the failure domain.
  • Automation is a change too. Roll it out gradually, cell by cell, and be able to turn it off.

Safe change is most of reliability. Many big outages begin with a deploy or config push. Progressive rollouts (canary one cell or 1% first, watch SLIs, then widen), quick rollback and feature flags are reliability features. See Deployments & Rollouts and CI/CD Platform.

Multi-region data: where do the writes go?

Stateless app servers are easy to copy to a new region. Data is the hard part. Light takes tens of milliseconds to cross a continent and back, so a write that waits for a remote region is slow. The AWS DR whitepaper names three write strategies for active-active, and they are a great interview framework:

How Netflix did active-active

Netflix's 2013 post describes running its user call path in two AWS regions (us-east-1 in Virginia and us-west-2 in Oregon), with users geo-DNS routed to the closest one, roughly 50/50. Their rules are a checklist worth repeating: services are stateless; every resource is accessed in-region; no cross-region calls on the user's path; data replication is asynchronous. User data lived in Cassandra, which replicates across regions natively. To gain confidence they wrote 1 million records in one region and read them from the other region 500 ms later under production load; all were read. For the memcached layer they did not build multi-master replication; their EvCache client instead sends an invalidation message to the other region (via SQS) after a write. Their edge proxy, Zuul, could also cap traffic and shed load during a failover so cold caches did not crush the databases.

Conflicts, consistency and residency

  • Last-writer-wins is simple but silently drops one of two concurrent updates. Fine for "last seen" timestamps, wrong for a bank balance or a seat booking. See conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) and vector clocks in hld-09.
  • Synchronous cross-region writes (for example a consensus group spread across 3 regions) give strong consistency at the cost of a cross-region round trip on every write. Systems like Google Spanner take this path for data that must never diverge. This is CAP/PACELC in practice: during a partition you choose consistency or availability; with no partition you still trade latency for consistency.
  • Sticky routing: keep one user's session in one region (Netflix treated a request outside the user's geo record as "mis-routed"), so they read their own writes.
  • Data residency: laws may say EU or Indian user data must stay in-country. That pushes you toward write partitioned with a home region per user, and away from replicating everything everywhere.

From your world

Async replication lag is just a queue between two boxes, like a transmit ring that the far end has not drained yet. Whatever sits in that ring when the link dies is your data loss. Synchronous replication is like waiting for the ACK before you tell the application "sent": safe, but every write now pays the round-trip time.

Interview questions

What does 99.99% availability mean, and what does it take to get there?

Four nines allows about 52.6 minutes of downtime a year, or about 4.3 minutes a month. That is too short for a human to notice, log in and fix things, so failover must be automatic and no tier can be a single point of failure. Concretely: stateless app servers in at least 3 AZs behind redundant load balancers, a replicated database with automated in-zone failover, timeouts and circuit breakers so one slow dependency does not stall everything, and careful progressive deploys because bad changes cause many outages. Remember the series rule: if a request crosses 5 components at 99.99% each, the end-to-end number is about 99.95%, so each critical component needs better than the overall target.

How to answer

Translate the nines into minutes first, then derive the consequence ("humans are too slow"), then list redundancy per tier, then mention the series math. Ending with "and we prove it with game days" is a strong close.

Likely follow-ups
  • Two copies at 99.9% in parallel: what is the combined availability, and when is that math wrong?
  • Would you promise 99.99% for writes and reads alike?
  • How does a multi-region setup change the number?
Explain SLI, SLO and SLA. Which SLIs would you pick for a URL shortener?

An SLI is a measured number (share of good requests), an SLO is a target for it over a window (99.9% over 28 days), and an SLA is a contract with consequences such as credits if the SLO is missed. For a URL shortener: (1) redirect availability, the share of GET /:code requests that return a 301/302 rather than 5xx; (2) redirect latency, p99 under some budget such as 50 ms at the edge; (3) creation availability for POST /urls, with a looser target because it is less frequent and less urgent; (4) correctness, the share of redirects pointing to the right long URL, checked by black-box probes. I would set redirect SLOs tighter than creation SLOs because reads are what users feel.

How to answer

Define the three words crisply, then pick SLIs per user journey, as ratios of good events to all events, with percentiles for latency. Mention that the internal SLO is tighter than any external SLA.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not use average latency?
  • Where do you measure: server logs, load balancer, or client?
  • What is your error budget per month?
What is an error budget, and how would you alert on it?

The error budget is 1 minus the SLO: a 99.9% SLO over 30 days allows 0.1% bad requests, equal to about 43.2 minutes of full outage. While budget remains, teams ship; when it runs out, an agreed policy pauses risky launches and prioritizes reliability work. For alerting I page on burn rate, the ratio of the current error rate to the allowed one. Following the SRE Workbook: page if the 1-hour and 5-minute windows both burn at 14.4x (2% of the monthly budget in an hour), page if 6-hour and 30-minute windows both burn at 6x, and open a ticket at 1x over 3 days with a 6-hour short window. Requiring two windows avoids flapping and stops the alert quickly once the problem is fixed.

How to answer

Give the minute math, then the policy (it is a social contract between product and SRE), then the multi-window alert. Contrast with threshold alerts on CPU, which page on causes, not symptoms.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if a single dependency keeps burning your budget?
  • Should planned maintenance count against the budget?
  • How do you handle a low-traffic service where one error is 1%?
Active-active or active-passive for a payment system across two regions?

For the money-moving core (ledger, balances, idempotency keys) I would use active-passive with a single write region, because the invariant "never spend the same balance twice" needs one source of truth and last-writer-wins would silently lose updates. Inside the primary region the ledger replicates synchronously across 3 AZs, so an AZ loss loses nothing. The second region is a warm standby with asynchronous replication, which sets a small non-zero RPO for a full regional loss; in-flight payments are reconciled via idempotency keys and the payment provider's records. Read-heavy parts (payment history, receipts, merchant dashboards) can be active-active, read local. If the business demands RPO 0 even for a region loss, I would use a consensus-replicated store across 3 regions and accept a cross-region round trip on every write.

How to answer

Split the system by data invariants rather than picking one answer for everything. Name the RPO and RTO you get, and the cost of the alternative (latency for sync cross-region).

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you avoid double charges during failover?
  • Who decides to fail over, a script or a human?
  • How long does promoting the standby take?
Define RPO and RTO. Choose a DR strategy for an e-commerce site's catalog, cart and orders.

RPO is how much data you may lose (time since the last good copy); RTO is how long you may be down. I would set them per component. Orders: RPO near 0, RTO minutes, so warm standby with continuous replication plus point-in-time backups. Cart: RPO of minutes is acceptable (a customer re-adds an item), so write-local active-active with last-writer-wins or a merge is fine. Catalog: it is rebuildable from the product source of truth, so serve it active-active from read replicas and search indexes, with backup and restore for the source. Analytics: backup and restore, RTO in hours. Spending warm-standby money only where the business needs it is the point.

How to answer

Define both terms with a timeline, list the four AWS tiers in cost order, then map each data store to a tier with a reason.

Likely follow-ups
  • Difference between pilot light and warm standby?
  • How do you verify your RTO is real?
  • What protects you from a bad migration that corrupted data?
We already have 3 replicas across zones. Why do we still need backups?

Replication protects against hardware and zone failure, not against logical failure. A buggy migration, an accidental delete, or ransomware writes to the primary and replicates to every replica within a second. Backups with point-in-time recovery let you restore to the moment before the bad write. They must live in a separate account and region so one compromised credential or one outage cannot take out production and backups together. You also back up infrastructure as code and configuration, and you test restores on a schedule, because the measured restore time is your real RTO for data disasters.

How to answer

Name the failure class replication cannot handle, then the properties a good backup needs (point in time, isolated, tested).

Likely follow-ups
  • How often do you back up, and what does that do to RPO?
  • How do you back up a 100 TB database without hurting production?
In an active-active multi-region design, how do you handle concurrent writes to the same record?

First avoid the conflict: route each entity's writes to a home region (write partitioned), for example by user or city, so two regions rarely write the same row. Where conflicts can still happen, pick a resolution per data type: last-writer-wins for fields where losing one update is harmless (last-seen time, profile photo), CRDTs for counters and sets (likes, cart items) so merges are automatic and commutative, and application-level merge or a single global writer for anything with invariants (inventory, seats, balances). Track causality with version vectors when you need to detect true concurrency rather than trust wall clocks. During a region failover, the surviving region temporarily takes the dead region's home partitions, and you reconcile when it returns.

How to answer

Lead with "avoid, then resolve", give one example per technique, and tie the choice to the data's business invariant. Mention clock skew as the weakness of LWW.

Likely follow-ups
  • What does DynamoDB global tables do by default?
  • How do you give a user read-your-writes after they move regions?
  • What is the cost of making it synchronous instead?
How do you detect a failed primary and fail over without causing split brain or flapping?

Detection: health checks from several independent vantage points, with a failure needing several consecutive misses, and checks that test real work (a write and read) rather than "process is alive", because gray failures look alive. Decision: a single authority, usually a lease held in a consensus store such as etcd or ZooKeeper; the primary must renew its lease to keep accepting writes, so if it is cut off it stops itself when the lease expires. Fencing: each new primary gets a higher epoch or fencing token, and storage rejects writes with an older token, so a zombie primary cannot corrupt data. Anti-flapping: a hold-down timer after a failover, and fail back only manually once the old primary has caught up as a replica.

How to answer

Break it into detect, decide, fence, stabilize. Mention that for whole-region failover many teams keep a human in the loop but automate every step.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why an odd number of consensus nodes across 3 zones?
  • What is a gray failure and why is it hard?
  • What happens to clients holding connections to the old primary?
What is cell-based architecture, and how does shuffle sharding reduce blast radius?

A cell is a full, independent copy of the service stack that serves a slice of customers; a thin router maps a partition key to a cell. A bad deploy, poison request or overload in one cell only affects that cell's customers, and cells give you a natural unit for canary deploys and capacity planning. Shuffle sharding goes further: instead of fixed groups, each customer gets a random combination of workers. With 8 workers and 2 per customer there are 28 combinations, so the poison customer still takes out its 2 workers (25% of capacity), but only customers assigned that exact same pair, about 1/28 or 3.6% of customers, lose all their workers; everyone else keeps at least one healthy worker and retries to it. With fixed shards, every customer in the bad shard (25% of customers) would be fully down. Route 53 uses 2,048 virtual name servers with 4 per domain, giving about 730 billion combinations. The router must stay tiny and stable, since it is the one shared component.

How to answer

Start with the problem (shared fate from bad inputs, not just hardware), then cells, then shuffle sharding with the combinatorics. Close with the router being the remaining SPOF and how you keep it simple.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you move a big customer to another cell?
  • How big should a cell be?
  • How is this different from database sharding?
What observability would you add to your design?

Three signals. Metrics: the four golden signals (latency percentiles, traffic, errors, saturation) per service and per endpoint, plus business metrics such as orders per minute, with SLO burn-rate alerts that page on user-visible symptoms. Logs: structured JSON with a request id and trace id, sampled for high-volume success paths but kept for errors. Traces: OpenTelemetry spans across every hop, sampled, so a slow p99 can be pinned to a specific dependency. I would keep high-cardinality fields such as user_id out of metric labels and in logs or traces instead, and I would host alerting outside the failure domain it watches.

How to answer

Name the three signals, say what each answers, then tie them together with a trace id and an SLO. One sentence on cardinality shows you have run this in production.

Likely follow-ups
  • Head-based vs tail-based sampling?
  • How do you avoid alert fatigue?
  • What is the difference between black-box and white-box monitoring?
During an outage, retries made everything worse. What happened and how do you design against it?

That is a retry storm: each failed call is retried, so load multiplies exactly when capacity is lowest, and with several layers each retrying 3 times the bottom layer sees many times the original load. AWS's December 2021 us-east-1 event is a public example: a latent issue stopped clients from backing off, so congestion persisted for hours. Defences: retry only idempotent operations; exponential backoff with jitter; a retry budget (for example retries may add at most 10% extra load); retry at one layer only; circuit breakers that fail fast; and load shedding at the edge with priorities so critical traffic survives.

How to answer

Name the mechanism (amplification), cite the real incident, then list defences in the order a request meets them.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why does jitter matter?
  • How do you make a POST safe to retry?
  • What is a thundering herd after recovery?
How would you introduce chaos engineering safely?

Start with observability and SLOs, because the experiment's pass/fail is "steady state held". Write a hypothesis ("if one cache node dies, p99 stays under 200 ms"). Begin in staging, then in production on a tiny slice (one cell or 1% of traffic) during working hours with an abort switch and the on-call team aware. Inject one real-world fault at a time: kill an instance, add latency, drop a dependency, fail an AZ. Fix what you find, then automate the experiment so it runs continuously. Grow to game days that evacuate a whole region and restore a database from backup, measuring real RTO and RPO. Google's Chubby planned outages are a good example of doing this for a dependency other teams trusted too much.

How to answer

Follow the four steps from the Principles of Chaos, and stress blast-radius control. Interviewers want to hear "small, observable, reversible".

Likely follow-ups
  • Why run experiments in production at all?
  • What would you never inject?

Scenario: a region degrades on the first night of a big sale

You run a Flipkart-style flash sale in two regions. At 00:05, error rate in Region A climbs to 8% and p99 latency triples. Region B is healthy at 45% CPU. The orders database primary is in Region A.

Weak answer: "Fail everything over to Region B right away" (B cannot absorb double traffic at 45% CPU plus cold caches, and promoting the DB mid-sale risks losing in-flight orders), or "wait and see".

Strong answer: declare an incident and check the error-budget burn (8% errors is a burn rate of 80 on a 99.9% SLO, so page and act now). Find the scope with traces: is it the whole region or one dependency? Shed load first: turn off non-critical features (recommendations, reviews) and rate-limit at the edge to protect checkout. If one AZ is bad, drain that AZ in small steps rather than the region. If the whole region is bad, shift browse traffic to B gradually while B scales up and warms caches, and only then decide on DB failover: promote B's replica, fence A, and reconcile in-flight orders with idempotency keys. Afterwards, a blameless postmortem and a game day to rehearse the exact path.

Likely follow-ups
  • What would you pre-provision before the sale so this is easier?
  • How do you keep inventory counts correct during failover?

Scenario: a bad config push burned 80% of the monthly error budget in one hour

A config change went to every server at once and broke login for 35 minutes. The SLO is 99.9% monthly and the product team wants to launch a big feature tomorrow.

Weak answer: "Roll back and launch tomorrow as planned; it was a one-off."

Strong answer: 35 minutes of a 43-minute budget is gone, so the error budget policy applies: freeze risky launches until the budget recovers or the root cause is fixed. Fix the cause class, not just the instance: config changes go through the same progressive rollout as code (one cell or canary first, automatic rollback on SLI regression), with validation before push. Move the feature launch behind a flag with a 1% ramp once the freeze lifts. The budget makes this a data-driven call rather than an argument.

Likely follow-ups
  • Who has the authority to override the freeze?
  • How would cells have changed the impact?

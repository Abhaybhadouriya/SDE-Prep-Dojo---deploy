Quick quiz

A user with 40 million followers posts a photo. In a hybrid design, what happens?

  1. 40 million ids are written to feed caches
  2. No push; followers' feeds pull and merge the post at read time
  3. The post is emailed to followers
Celebrities are excluded from fan-out on write because one post would cost tens of millions of writes. Their posts are merged in when a follower reads.

Why does the feed cache store post ids instead of full posts?

  1. Ids are encrypted
  2. Redis cannot store text
  3. Ids are tiny and edits/deletes only need fixing in one place
An id is 8 bytes. If full posts were copied into every follower's feed, an edit would require updating millions of copies, and memory would explode.

Why is cursor pagination better than ?page=3 for a feed?

  1. New posts at the top do not shift a cursor, so there are no duplicates
  2. Cursors are shorter strings
  3. Offsets are not allowed in REST
A cursor points at a fixed item. Offsets move when new items are inserted, and deep offsets are also slower to compute.

Fan-out workers get the same Kafka event twice after a rebalance. What happens?

  1. Followers see the post twice
  2. Nothing visible: ZADD with the same member and score is idempotent
  3. Kafka rejects the duplicate
Kafka consumers are at-least-once. Because adding the same id to a sorted set is a no-op, duplicates are harmless here.

Which metric should autoscale the fan-out workers?

  1. CPU usage
  2. Number of HTTP requests
  3. Kafka consumer lag
Lag tells you how far behind the feeds are, which is what users feel. Workers often wait on I/O, so CPU can look fine while feeds fall behind.

The ranking service times out. What should the feed service do?

  1. Return the feed in chronological order
  2. Return an error page
  3. Retry ranking until it works
Graceful degradation: a less relevant feed is far better than none. Retries would add latency and load to a struggling service.

Mia posts and immediately opens her own feed, but fan-out is lagging. How does she still see her post?

  1. She cannot; she must wait
  2. Her client inserts it locally and the feed service merges the reader's own newest posts
  3. Kafka sends it to her phone directly
Read-your-own-writes: the author's own recent posts are always merged in, independent of fan-out.

The story

Explain like I'm 5

Every child in class has a little mailbox on their desk. When Mia draws a picture, the teacher makes a photocopy for every friend who "follows" Mia and drops one copy in each of their mailboxes. In the morning, each child just opens their own mailbox and the newest drawings are right on top. That is fast for the children, but the teacher is busy making copies. Now imagine a famous artist visits, and 10,000 children follow her. The teacher cannot make 10,000 copies every time she draws! So for the famous artist, the teacher does something different: she pins one copy on the big wall. When a child opens their mailbox, they also glance at the wall and mix those drawings in. Mailboxes for friends, the wall for famous people. That is the whole trick of a news feed.

In real words: the teacher making copies is fan-out on write (also called push): when someone posts, we write the post's id into every follower's precomputed feed. The wall is fan-out on read (also called pull): we do not copy anything; we fetch the post when a reader opens the app. The mailbox is a feed cache, usually a list of post ids per user kept in memory (Redis or something like it). Real systems use a hybrid: push for normal people, pull for celebrities, and merge both when you read.

The design is a question about where you pay the cost: at write time (each post costs "number of followers" writes) or at read time (each feed open costs "number of people you follow" reads). Because people read feeds far more often than they post, most designs pay at write time for most users.

From your world

Fan-out on write is ingress replication of multicast: the packet is copied once per receiver at the source, so each receiver's egress queue is ready to drain. Fan-out on read is like receivers polling a shared buffer. You already know the trade-off: replication at ingress burns fabric bandwidth when the receiver list is huge (a celebrity is a multicast group with 50 million members), so you switch strategy for big groups. Where the analogy breaks: a feed copy is a tiny 8-byte id, not the full payload, and copies can be minutes late without anyone noticing.

New words, once each: QPS = requests per second. p99 = the time that 99 out of 100 requests beat. Hydration = turning a list of ids into full objects (text, author name, photo URL, like count). Cursor = a bookmark that says "continue after this item". All are in the glossary.

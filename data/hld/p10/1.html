When you hit this problem

You need this pattern whenever the question contains a where. Listen for these signals in the prompt:

  • "near me", "within 5 km", "closest N": Yelp, Google Places, nearest ATM, nearest Amazon Locker.
  • "match a rider with a driver": the search set is moving every few seconds (Uber, Ola, Swiggy delivery partners).
  • "show people in my area": Tinder's deck, Facebook "nearby friends".
  • "price or count per area": surge pricing per neighbourhood, heat maps, demand forecasting.
  • "which zone is this point in?": delivery zones, geofences, "is this address serviceable?". This is a polygon question, not a point question.

A few terms first. Latitude is how far north or south (-90 to +90 degrees). Longitude is how far east or west (-180 to +180). The haversine formula gives the straight-line distance between two lat/long points on a sphere. A spatial index is any index that finds points by position. A cell is one tile of a grid laid over the map.

Why the ordinary database index fails

Put a B-tree index on lat and ask for points between 12.93 and 13.01. The index gives you a thin horizontal strip around the whole planet. A second index on lng gives a vertical strip. The database can only use one of them well, or intersect two very large result sets. Either way it reads far more rows than the few hundred you want.

From your world

A geohash or S2 cell id is a prefix, just like an IP prefix in a routing table. A longer prefix is a smaller area, like a /28 inside a /24. "Find everything in cell tdr1v" is a longest-prefix-style range lookup, the same shape of problem a FIB trie solves. And the classic gotcha matches too: two hosts that are one address apart can sit on different sides of a /24 boundary, just as two shops 20 metres apart can sit in different cells with no shared prefix.

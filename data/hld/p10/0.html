The story

Explain like I'm 5

You are in a giant toy shop with a million toys spread over a huge floor. You want the ice-cream stand closest to you. The slow way is to walk to every single stall, measure how far it is, and remember the closest one. That takes all day. The smart shop paints the floor into big squares, and writes on a board which stalls sit in which square. Now you only look at the stalls in your square and the squares right next to it. If your square is very crowded, the shop paints it into four smaller squares. If you are walking around, you shout "I moved to square B7!" every few seconds so the board stays right.

That is the whole pattern. The painted squares are a spatial index: a way to turn a 2-D position (latitude and longitude) into a key a computer can look up fast. The "look at your square and its neighbours" rule is the neighbour search. Splitting crowded squares is what a quadtree does. Shouting your new square every few seconds is location ingestion. Every geo design in an interview, from Yelp to Uber to Tinder, is some mix of these four ideas.

The one key insight, as Hello Interview's proximity-search deep dive puts it, is that a normal sorted index orders things along one line, and a single line "can't preserve two-dimensional closeness". Every technique on this page is a trick to squeeze 2-D space onto a 1-D key well enough to get a small candidate set. Then you do exact distance maths on that small set.

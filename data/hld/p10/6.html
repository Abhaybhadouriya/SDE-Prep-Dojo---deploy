In the interview

Phrases that land well

  • "A B-tree on lat or lng gives me a strip, not a square, so I need a spatial index that gives nearby points nearby keys."
  • "I'll split static data from moving data. Places are read-heavy and change rarely; driver locations are a write firehose that only needs the latest value, so they live in memory."
  • "Candidate generation is loose and cheap; then I post-filter with exact distance and rank."
  • "I'll always search the neighbour cells, because nearby points can have no shared prefix."
  • "I'll shard by geography and balance by load, not by area."

Traps

  • Writing every GPS ping to the main SQL database.
  • Forgetting neighbours (the boundary bug), or forgetting the final exact filter.
  • Picking one cell size for every city and every radius.
  • Ranking ride matches by straight-line distance when a river or a one-way street sits between them: use road ETA.
  • Swapping lat and lng. Redis, PostGIS ST_MakePoint and GeoJSON all take longitude first.
  • Saying "quadtree" for moving drivers without discussing the cost of constant re-splitting.
Explain geohash and its boundary problem.

Geohash repeatedly halves the world, alternating a longitude bit and a latitude bit, and encodes every 5 bits as one base-32 character. A longer shared prefix usually means closer points, and each extra character shrinks the cell about 32 times (5 chars about 4.9 km, 6 chars about 1.2 x 0.6 km, 7 chars about 153 m). The trap is that the reverse is not true: two points 10 m apart on either side of a cell edge (worst case at the equator, the prime meridian or the 180 meridian) can share no prefix. The fix is to query the centre cell plus its 8 neighbours and then filter by exact distance.

How to answer

Walk two or three bits by hand, state the prefix property, then volunteer the boundary trap before the interviewer asks.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you choose the geohash length for a 2 km radius?
  • How does Redis store a geohash?
Geohash or quadtree for a Yelp-style "restaurants near me" service with 10M+ businesses?

Both work; the choice depends on density and operations. Geohash: store a prefix column per business, index it, query 9 cells, filter. It is simple, lives in the existing database, and updates are a single column write; its weakness is fixed cell size, so dense areas return big candidate sets. Quadtree: an in-memory tree that splits leaves above about 100 businesses, so every leaf returns a similar small set no matter the density; the whole tree (roughly 240 MB of leaf data for 10M points by my estimate) fits on one server, so I replicate it to every search server and rebuild or patch it from the database. Business data changes rarely, so the quadtree's update cost does not matter here. I'd start with geohash in Postgres or a search index, and mention the quadtree as the next step if dense-city latency becomes the bottleneck.

How to answer

Compare on four axes: density handling, update cost, storage location, operational simplicity. Then commit to one with a reason.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you update the quadtree when a business is added?
  • How long does a new server take to warm up?
  • Where do ratings and categories get filtered?
Drivers send their location every 4-5 seconds. How do you store and query that?

Estimate first: 10 million drivers every 5 s is about 2 million writes per second (Uber's DISCO was designed for a million). Only the latest location matters and losing one ping costs 5 seconds, so it does not belong in a durable SQL table. Ingest via a gateway into Kafka keyed by cell or city, and maintain an in-memory geo index: Redis GEO (geohash in a sorted set, GEOSEARCH by radius) or your own shards keyed by S2/H3 cell. Expire drivers with no update for about 30 s using a last-seen sorted set. Sample a copy to cheap storage for trip history and analytics. Optionally make updates adaptive: stationary drivers send less often.

How to answer

Lead with the write rate number, then "ephemeral, latest-value-only, in memory". That single framing earns most of the credit.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if a Redis node dies?
  • How do you shard it?
  • How do you avoid two riders getting the same driver?
Why does Uber use hexagons (H3) for surge pricing instead of squares?

Uber's H3 blog gives the main reason: in a hexagon grid all six neighbours are the same distance from the centre, while squares have two distances and triangles three. That makes rings of hexagons a good approximation of a circle and makes smoothing a value over neighbours fair in every direction. Hexagons also reduce error as people move across a city. H3 has 16 resolutions, each about 1/7 the area of the one above, so you can count demand at a fine level and roll up. Surge is computed from supply and demand counts per hexagon.

How to answer

State the equal-neighbour-distance property, draw a ring, connect it to smoothing surge so it does not jump at cell borders.

Likely follow-ups
  • What resolution would you pick for surge?
  • What are the downsides of H3 vs S2?
  • How do pentagons affect you?
Find the 10 nearest drivers when you don't know the radius.

Use expanding search. Start with the rider's cell (or a small radius), and if fewer than K candidates come back, add the next ring of cells (or double the radius) and repeat, up to a cap such as 10 km. Once you have at least K, rank the whole candidate set by exact distance, or better by road ETA from a routing service, and return the top K. Rank the whole set because a candidate in an outer ring can beat one in an inner ring's far corner. With Redis this is GEOSEARCH ... BYRADIUS r ASC COUNT K, retried with a larger r.

How to answer

Name "expanding ring", the stopping rule, the cap, and the final exact ranking. Mention ETA over straight-line distance for rides.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the rider is on the edge of two shards?
  • How many ETA calls is that per request, and how do you bound them?
How do you shard a geo index so no shard is hot?

Do not shard by equal area: a downtown cell has thousands of times more points and queries than an ocean cell. Options: by city (simple, but big cities are hot); by cell id through a consistent-hash ring (Uber DISCO with S2 level-12 cells and ringpop), which spreads load but makes queries scatter-gather over the cells covering the circle; or by contiguous ranges of a Hilbert-ordered cell id, sized by measured load (Tinder's geoshards from S2 levels 7-8), which keeps queries to 2-3 shards. Put many small shards on each physical server, randomly, so one hot shard cannot overload a machine, and split shards that grow hot.

How to answer

Show you know the tension: locality (few shards per query) versus balance (no hot shard). Pick load-balanced ranges and explain the rebalancing step.

Likely follow-ups
  • A user moves from one geoshard to another: what happens to their document?
  • How do you rebalance without downtime?
Design "nearby friends": show which of my friends are within 5 miles, live.

This is mostly a fan-out problem, not a spatial index problem, because I only care about my friends (hundreds), not everyone. Each active user keeps a WebSocket to a connection server and sends its location every few tens of seconds. The latest location goes to a cache with a TTL (so inactive users vanish) and is published on a per-user pub/sub channel. Each user's connection server subscribes to the channels of that user's friends, computes distance on every update, and pushes the friend's position to the client only if within the radius. Location history goes to a write-optimised store like Cassandra. The pub/sub layer is sharded by user id with consistent hashing. If the feature were "random people nearby", I'd switch to channels per geohash cell instead of per user.

How to answer

Spot the twist early: friends list makes it fan-out, not geo search. Then cover update rate, TTL, and pub/sub scaling. Link to the real-time updates pattern.

Likely follow-ups
  • How many pub/sub messages per second at 10M active users?
  • How do you handle a user with 5,000 friends?
"Is this address serviceable?" How do you check if a point is inside a delivery zone?

Zones are polygons, so this is point-in-polygon, not nearest-point. Store zones in PostGIS with a GiST (R-tree) index and query ST_Contains(zone.geom, point): the index finds zones whose bounding boxes contain the point, then the exact test runs on only those. Zones change rarely, so cache the answer per small cell (for example an H3 resolution-9 id, edge about 200 m) and invalidate when a zone is edited. A cell on a zone border must fall back to the exact test.

How to answer

Say "shapes means R-tree", describe the two-step bbox-then-exact query, then add the cell cache as the scaling move.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you serve 50k checks per second?
  • What about overlapping zones with priorities?
A Redis geo shard for one city crashes. What happens and how do you recover?

Matching in that city degrades until the index is rebuilt. Because drivers re-send every ~5 s, a fresh empty replica fully repopulates in about one update interval, which is why many designs accept in-memory state here. Better: run a replica (Redis Sentinel or cluster replicas) for fast failover, and keep a compacted Kafka topic of latest locations so a new shard can bulk-load instead of waiting. During the gap, the matcher can widen the search to neighbouring shards or show "finding drivers" for a few seconds. Durable trip state never lived in this index, so nothing is lost permanently.

How to answer

Separate ephemeral state (location) from durable state (trips, payments). Give the recovery time in terms of the update interval.

Likely follow-ups
  • What about the driver locks held in that Redis?
  • How do you detect the failure in under 5 seconds?

Scenario: "nearby restaurants" is slow only in Mumbai

Your Yelp-style search uses 5-character geohash cells and the 9-cell rule. p99 latency (99th percentile: 99 of every 100 searches finish faster than this; it measures the slow tail, not the average) is 80 ms in most cities but 1.4 s in south Mumbai. CPU on search nodes spikes during lunch.

Weak answer: "Add more search servers."

Strong answer: the cells are a fixed 4.9 km, so a dense area returns tens of thousands of candidates that all need distance maths and ranking. Make the cell size adapt: use a 6-character prefix when the 5-character cell is dense (store both columns), or move to a quadtree that splits leaves above ~100 places. Cap candidates per query, and cache popular cell + category results with a short TTL because restaurant data changes rarely. Add per-city latency dashboards so density problems show up by region.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you choose the precision per query automatically?
  • What cache key would you use?

Scenario: riders are matched to drivers across the river

Complaints that a "2-minute away" driver takes 15 minutes. The matcher returns the nearest driver by straight-line distance from a Redis GEOSEARCH.

Weak answer: "Reduce the search radius."

Strong answer: geo search should only generate candidates; ranking must use road ETA. Take the top 20-30 candidates by distance, call the routing/ETA service in a batch (as Uber's DISCO sorts candidates by ETA), and pick the best ETA. Bound the cost with a candidate cap and a cache of ETAs between nearby cells. Track "promised vs actual pickup time" per city as the quality metric.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you keep ETA calls under 100 ms for 30 candidates?
  • What about drivers about to finish a trip nearby?

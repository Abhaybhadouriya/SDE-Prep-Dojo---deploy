Interview questions

Why can't we just put a B-tree index on latitude and longitude for "restaurants near me"?

A B-tree orders rows along one dimension. An index on latitude narrows the search to a horizontal band around the whole planet. The database reads all of it and checks longitude row by row. A composite index (lat, lng) does not help much, because only the first column gives a tight range. Two independent indexes give two big strips that must be intersected. Proximity needs a structure where nearby points are near each other in the index: an encoded cell ID (geohash, S2, H3) or a spatial tree (quadtree, R-tree).

How to answer

Draw the two strips, point at the small overlap, then name the two families of fixes and say which one you will use and why.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which would you pick for 10M static businesses?
  • What does Postgres offer out of the box? (PostGIS GiST)
Explain geohash and its edge problem. How do you choose precision?

A geohash interleaves the bits of longitude and latitude by repeatedly halving each range, then writes every 5 bits as one base-32 character. A longer shared prefix usually means a smaller shared cell, so "nearby" becomes a prefix range scan on a normal index. The edge problem: two points a metre apart across a cell border can have different prefixes. So you always search your cell plus its 8 neighbours and post-filter by exact distance. Precision rule: the 3x3 block is guaranteed to cover your search circle only if the cell's smaller side is at least the radius, because you may stand right at the edge of your cell. A length-6 cell is 1.22 km x 0.61 km, so it only safely covers about 0.6 km. For a 1-2 km driver search use length 5 (4.89 km cells). For a 5 km restaurant search, length 5 is marginal (4.89 < 5), so use length 4, or a 5x5 ring of length-5 cells. The cleanest answer: compute a set of cells that covers the circle, which is what Redis GEOSEARCH does internally.

How to answer

Explain the bit-halving with one example, give two rows of the precision table, then state the 3x3 fix before they ask.

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens at the poles or the 180-degree meridian?
  • How does Redis store a geohash? (52-bit integer score in a sorted set)
  • What if a cell has 10,000 drivers?
Design Yelp: geohash or quadtree?

Both work, so justify the choice from the workload. Businesses are mostly static and reads far outnumber writes. Density is very uneven, with thousands in a city centre and none in farmland. A quadtree adapts to density, so each leaf holds about the same number of businesses (say 100) and "top 20 nearby" touches few leaves. It lives in memory on location servers and is rebuilt from the database periodically. A geohash is simpler: one indexed column in any database, easy to shard and cache. The downside is that fixed cells give either too many results in dense areas or too few in sparse ones, which you fix by widening to a shorter prefix when results are thin. My default: geohash in the database (or PostGIS) for simplicity, plus a cache of popular cells. I would move to a quadtree only if uneven density hurts latency.

How to answer

Name the workload facts (static, read-heavy, skewed density) first, then compare both against them. End with a default and a trigger to switch.

Likely follow-ups
  • How big is the quadtree in memory for 200M businesses?
  • How do you update the quadtree when a business is added?
  • How do you rank by rating and distance together?
10 million drivers send GPS every 5 seconds. Where do you store locations and how do you find the nearest 10?

That is about 2M writes per second of soft state, where only the latest position matters. I would not write it to a disk database row by row. Instead, a stateless location service receives pings and calls GEOADD on a Redis cluster sharded by city or coarse cell, overwriting the driver's position. A TTL or a sweeper removes drivers silent for about 30 seconds. To match, call GEOSEARCH by radius (or look up the rider's H3 cell plus one or two rings), take maybe 50 candidates, filter by availability and vehicle type, then rank by ETA rather than straight-line distance. The same pings also go to Kafka for trip history, ETA training and surge.

How to answer

Do the write maths first (10M / 5 s = 2M/s). It justifies in-memory soft state. Then cover the read path, failure ("rebuilt within one ping cycle") and the hot cell.

Likely follow-ups
  • A Redis shard dies. What do riders see?
  • How do you avoid offering one driver to two riders at once? (a lock or reservation with TTL)
  • How do you reduce ping volume?
Why does Uber use hexagons (H3) instead of square cells?

A hexagon's centre is the same distance from all 6 neighbours. A square has edge and corner neighbours at different distances. For surge pricing and demand forecasting, Uber aggregates supply and demand per cell and smooths over neighbouring rings, and equal distances make that smoothing fair. Hexagons also reduce the error when a moving rider crosses cells. H3 has 16 resolutions where each level is about 1/7 of the parent's area, 64-bit IDs, and 12 pentagons placed in the ocean. One trade-off: children do not nest exactly inside the parent, and close IDs are not close on the map, so you use grid functions (k-rings) instead of prefix scans.

How to answer

Lead with the neighbour-distance argument, connect it to a real use (surge), then mention one honest downside.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which resolution would you use for surge zones?
  • How does S2 differ?
Design the data structure for search autocomplete (Bing / Amazon search box).

A trie where each node stores the top K (for example 10) completions for its prefix, ranked by frequency with time decay. A lookup walks one node per typed character and returns the cached list, so latency does not depend on how many queries are stored. The trie is built offline from query logs (Kafka, then a batch or streaming aggregation) and loaded read-only into replicated servers, sharded by prefix ranges balanced by traffic. The shortest prefixes are cached at the CDN and in the browser, and the client debounces keystrokes. Personalisation and trending are merged at serving time from small separate stores.

How to answer

Start with the plain trie, show why subtree search is slow, add cached top-K, then cover build, shard, cache and freshness in that order.

Likely follow-ups
  • Estimate the trie's memory.
  • How do trending queries appear within minutes?
  • How do you remove an offensive suggestion right now?
How do you keep autocomplete fresh without rebuilding a huge trie every minute?

Split it into layers. A big base trie is rebuilt hourly or daily and swapped in with a pointer flip (blue-green, so readers never see a half-built trie). A small trending layer is updated from a streaming job (for example Flink over Kafka) using windowed counts or a count-min sketch for heavy hitters. At serving time the two top-K lists are merged. A blocklist is checked last and can be updated instantly. This keeps the expensive structure immutable and the fresh part small.

How to answer

Say "immutable big thing plus small mutable thing, merged at read time". It is the same pattern as LSM trees and Lucene segments.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you weight recent queries?
  • See d35 Top-K for the streaming part.
How does an inverted index work, and why is BM25 better than plain TF-IDF?

Each document is analysed into terms (tokenise, lowercase, stem). For each term the index keeps a sorted postings list of document IDs, often with positions and counts. A query looks up each term and intersects or unions the lists, then scores candidates. TF-IDF rewards terms that are frequent in the document and rare in the corpus, but it grows without limit with repeats and favours long documents. BM25 adds saturation (k1, default 1.2) so repeats add less and less, and length normalisation (b, default 0.75) so short, focused fields win. It is the default in Lucene and Elasticsearch.

How to answer

Draw term to postings list, show a two-term intersection, then explain the two BM25 knobs in plain words.

  • Why is Elasticsearch not your source of truth?
  • How do you handle typos? (fuzzy queries by edit distance, n-gram fields)
What is HNSW and when would you use a vector index instead of keyword search?

Use vectors when you match by meaning rather than exact words: semantic search, "more like this" products, image similarity, RAG retrieval, near-duplicate detection. HNSW is a layered proximity graph. Upper layers are sparse long-range links, layer 0 holds every vector, and search descends greedily from top to bottom. It gives high recall at millisecond latency, and the paper reports logarithmic scaling. The costs are memory (all raw vectors must sit in RAM; the links add only a few percent, about M x 2 x 4 bytes per vector), slow builds and pricey inserts. Knobs: M (links per node), ef_construction (build quality) and ef_search (query-time recall vs latency). In practice I combine BM25 and vector results (hybrid) and re-rank the top few.

How to answer

Give the use case first, then the skip-list picture, then one number (6 KB per 1536-dim float vector) and one trade-off (recall vs latency).

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you apply a filter like "in stock"?
  • HNSW vs IVF?
  • What happens when you change the embedding model?
How would you shard a geospatial index across many machines?

Shard by geography, because queries are local. Use a coarse cell as the shard key (geohash length 3-4, an S2 level, H3 resolution 3-5) or simply city or region. Map cells to shards through a lookup table rather than a fixed hash, so you can split a hot cell (a stadium, Mumbai at rush hour) without moving everything. A query near a border touches 2-4 shards: fan out and merge. Replicate read-heavy shards. Avoid hashing driver IDs, because then every proximity query becomes a scatter-gather over all shards.

How to answer

State the principle (shard on the query's locality), the key, how you handle hotspots, and the border case.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you rebalance a hot shard with no downtime?
  • How does consistent hashing compare here?

Scenario: "Nearby restaurants" is slow in Koramangala but fast everywhere else

Your food app uses geohash length 5 (about 5 km cells). After a marketing push, p99 latency (p99 = the latency that 99% of requests beat; 1 in 100 is slower) for "restaurants near me" in one Bengaluru area went from 80 ms to 900 ms. Other cities are fine.

Weak answer: "Add more database replicas."

Strong answer: This is a dense-cell problem. The 3x3 block around Koramangala now returns thousands of candidates, and we compute exact distance and ranking for all of them. Fixes in order: (1) use a finer precision (length 6) where density is high, or switch that region to a quadtree or adaptive cells; (2) cap candidates per cell, pre-sorted by rating, so we score at most a few hundred; (3) cache the ranked result per (cell, filters) for 30-60 seconds, since menus change slowly; (4) if the shard itself is hot, split it via the cell-to-shard map. Verify with per-cell candidate-count metrics.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you pick precision automatically?
  • What would you alert on?

Scenario: product search shows an old price for minutes

During a flash sale, Flipkart-style search results show the old price for 2-3 minutes after the catalog DB is updated.

Weak answer: "Make Elasticsearch the main database so it is always correct."

Strong answer: Search indexes are near real time and fed asynchronously (CDC to Kafka to an indexer), so some lag is expected. Find where the lag is: CDC delay, consumer lag, bulk batch size, or refresh interval. Then fix the user impact. Show search hits from the index, but read price and stock from the pricing service (or a cache) at render time, because those fields change fast. Keep slow-changing fields (title, specs) in the index. Monitor end-to-end indexing lag as an SLO.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you re-index 100M products with zero downtime? (new index, dual write, alias swap)

The 60-second answer. "A normal B-tree keeps one dimension in order, so for 'near me' I map each location to a cell ID: geohash or S2 for range scans, H3 when I need equal-size rings. I query my cell plus its neighbours, with a precision where the cell's smaller side is at least my radius, then compute exact distances on that short candidate list. Static places go in a quadtree or a geohash column; moving drivers go in an in-memory Redis GEO index, sharded by city, refreshed by pings. For text I use an inverted index with BM25, for prefixes a trie with top-K per node, and for meaning an HNSW vector index, often hybrid with BM25 and re-ranked."

Geohash: the map as a string

A geohash turns a latitude/longitude pair into a short string such as tdr1v9q. Gustavo Niemeyer created it in 2008 (per Karan Pratap Singh's free system-design book). Here is how it is built:

  1. Halve the longitude range. The range is -180 to +180. If the point is in the right (east) half, write bit 1. Otherwise write 0. Keep only that half.
  2. Halve the latitude range. The range is -90 to +90. Upper half gives 1 and lower half gives 0.
  3. Alternate. Keep halving longitude, then latitude, then longitude again. This interleaves the bits of the two numbers into one bit string. This is called a Z-order (Morton) curve.
  4. Group every 5 bits into one character using a base-32 alphabet (0-9 and letters without a, i, l, o). Each character picks one of 32 sub-cells.
  1. Longitude 77.59 is east of 0, so the first bit is 1. We throw away the west half of the world.
  2. Latitude 12.97 is north of the equator, so bit 2 is 1. We now hold the north-east quarter.
  3. Keep halving and alternating. Longitude is below 90 (bit 0), so we keep 0..90. Then latitude is below 45 (bit 0), so we keep 0..45. The box is now a quarter of the north-east quarter.
  4. Bit 5 is longitude again: 77.59 is at least 45, so it is in the right part (bit 1). After 5 bits we have 11001 = 25, which is the letter t in the base-32 alphabet. That is the first character: a cell 45 degrees wide and 45 degrees tall.
  5. Repeat for 35 bits and you get tdr1v9q. Koramangala, about 5 km away, is tdr1w6u. They share the prefix tdr1.

Every extra character makes the cell 32 times smaller. These are the cell sizes from Karan Pratap Singh's book, which match the usual geohash tables:

Querying. Store the geohash in a normal indexed column. "Near me" becomes a prefix scan: WHERE geohash LIKE 'tdr1v%'. A prefix scan on a B-tree is a range scan, which databases are very good at. Redis does the same thing in a different way. GEOADD stores a 52-bit integer geohash as the score of a sorted set, and GEOSEARCH ... BYRADIUS turns into range scans over those scores. The Redis docs give its cost as O(N + log M), where N is the number of items in the grid-aligned box around your circle.

# Redis 6.2+ - add two restaurants and search within 3 km of a user
GEOADD restaurants 77.5946 12.9716 "mg-road-dosa" 77.6245 12.9352 "koramangala-biryani"
GEOSEARCH restaurants FROMLONLAT 77.60 12.97 BYRADIUS 3 km ASC COUNT 20 WITHDIST

The edge problem and the 3x3 fix

Geohash has one famous trap. Two points can be one metre apart and still sit on opposite sides of a cell border. Their prefixes can be totally different, for example near the equator or the prime meridian, where even the first character changes. A naive prefix scan of "my cell" would miss the restaurant across the road. The fix is always the same: query your cell plus its 8 neighbours (a 3x3 block), then compute the real distance for each candidate and drop those outside the circle.

Careful: geohash cells are not equal in real size. A degree of longitude is about 111 km at the equator and shrinks to zero at the poles, so cells get thin near the poles. For India, Europe and the US this rarely matters in an interview. For a global aviation or shipping system it does, and that is when you reach for S2.

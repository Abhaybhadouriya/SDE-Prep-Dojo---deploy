The story

Explain like I'm 5

Your class has a big box of 1,000 toy cars, all mixed up. Your friend asks: "Which cars are close to the red fire truck?" You could pick up every car and measure. That takes all day.

So you make a game. You draw a big square on the floor and cut it into 4 smaller squares. Each small square gets a letter. Then you cut each of those into 4 again and add another letter. Now every car has a short name like "B-C-A" that tells you which little square it sits in. Cars with names that start the same way are sitting near each other. To find cars near the fire truck, you only look in its little square and the squares right next to it.

A picture dictionary works the same way for words. All words that start with "ca" live on the same page, so you open that page and you are done. Every index on this page is just a clever way to put things in boxes so you only open a few boxes.

From your world

Most of this page is longest-prefix match in disguise. A geohash is a /n route for the map: the more leading characters two places share, the smaller the area they share, like two IPs inside one /24. A trie for autocomplete is the same data structure as a routing trie. And "filter, then refine" is the TCAM-then-exact-check pattern: a cheap, wide match first, a precise check on the few hits. Where this breaks: a route table answers "which single prefix matches", while "near me" needs your cell and its neighbours, because a close point can sit across a cell border with a very different prefix.

Every special index follows one recipe. You turn a hard question ("near", "starts with", "about", "looks like") into a cheap lookup that returns a small candidate set, meaning a short list of things that might match. Then you run the exact, expensive check on that short list only. Hello Interview's proximity deep dive makes the same point: the index only finds candidates, and exact distance maths finishes the job. Keep this "filter, then refine" idea in your head.

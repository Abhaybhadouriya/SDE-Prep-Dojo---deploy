Quadtree: split only where it is crowded

Geohash cuts the whole world to the same depth, so a cell in Koramangala holds 900 restaurants and a cell in a forest holds zero. A quadtree adapts. Start with one box for the whole map. When a box holds more than a threshold of points (for example 100 businesses, a common interview choice), split it into 4 equal children and move the points down. Dense areas grow deep trees. Empty areas stay as one big leaf. Hello Interview notes that a downtown area can be ten or more levels deep while rural areas are about two.

Searching. Start at the root. Skip any child box that does not touch your search circle. Collect points from the leaves you reach, then do the exact distance check. To get "K nearest" you can keep expanding to neighbouring leaves until you have K.

Where it lives. A quadtree is a pointer-heavy, in-memory structure. That makes it fast in RAM but awkward on disk. The classic "design Yelp" answer keeps the quadtree in memory on a set of location servers and rebuilds it from the database at startup or on a schedule. Businesses change rarely, so a nightly rebuild is fine. For moving drivers it is a poor fit: a driver crossing a leaf border means deleting and re-inserting a point and maybe splitting or merging nodes, millions of times a second.

From your world

A quadtree is close to a multi-level page table. The top level covers a huge range. You only allocate lower levels where memory is actually mapped, so sparse address spaces stay cheap. Quadtrees allocate deeper boxes only where points actually exist. Where this breaks: a page table has a fixed depth (4 or 5 levels), while a quadtree's depth depends on data density, so a crowded cell can make one branch much deeper than the rest.

k-d tree and BKD tree. A k-d tree splits at the median point instead of the middle of the box, alternating x and y at each level, so it stays balanced. Lucene and Elasticsearch use a disk-friendly version called a BKD tree for geo_point fields. It packs points into page-sized blocks and is built in batches, which suits Lucene's write-once segments.

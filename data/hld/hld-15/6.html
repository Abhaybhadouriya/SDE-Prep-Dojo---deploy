Moving points: the driver location pipeline

Static places (restaurants, hotels, lockers) and moving things (drivers, delivery partners, friends) need different designs. Hello Interview's Uber breakdown sizes it like this: about 10 million drivers sending a location every 5 seconds is about 2 million writes per second. Writing that straight into a database is expensive: 2M/s x 86,400 s = about 173 billion writes a day. At DynamoDB on-demand pricing in us-east-1 after AWS's November 2024 price cut ($0.625 per million write request units), that is about $108k per day; Hello Interview's breakdown, using the older price, quotes over $200k. Either way it is on the order of $100k+ per day. The common answer is an in-memory store keyed by cell, where each update simply overwrites the driver's last position.

  • Freshness over durability. If a Redis shard dies, the data is back within one 5-second ping cycle. Remove drivers with no ping for about 30 seconds.
  • Shard by geography. A city or a coarse cell (geohash length 4, or H3 resolution 4-5) is the shard key, because nearly all queries are local. Only queries near a shard border touch two shards.
  • Cut write volume. Send pings less often when the driver is parked and more often when moving fast or turning.
  • Hot cells. A stadium after an IPL match is one cell with thousands of drivers and riders. Split that shard further, use a finer resolution there, or cap the number of candidates you score.

From your world

This is a soft-state table, like an ARP cache or a MAC learning table. Entries are refreshed by traffic, never fsync'ed, and simply age out when the host goes quiet. Losing the table is fine because the next round of packets (pings) rebuilds it. Where this breaks: an ARP cache lives on one box, while the driver table is sharded across many Redis nodes, so a driver crossing a shard border must be moved, not just re-learned.

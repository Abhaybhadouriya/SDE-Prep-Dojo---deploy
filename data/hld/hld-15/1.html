Why a normal index is not enough

A B-tree (the default index in Postgres and MySQL) keeps keys in sorted order. That is perfect for "age between 20 and 30" because the answers sit next to each other in one line. Location has two numbers, latitude (north-south) and longitude (east-west). If you index latitude, a query for "within 2 km of me" returns a thin horizontal strip around the whole planet. Index longitude, and you get a vertical strip. The answer you want is the tiny square where the strips cross. The database still reads every row in one strip and throws most of them away.

There are two families of fixes, and the whole geo part of this page is about them:

  • Encoded cells (geohash, S2, H3). Cut the planet into a grid of cells, give each cell an ID, and store that ID in an ordinary index. Nearby points get similar or related IDs.
  • Spatial trees (quadtree, k-d tree, R-tree). Build a tree of boxes. Each node covers an area. To search, walk down only the branches that touch your search circle.

From your world

You already know this trick from routing. An IPv4 address is a 32-bit number, and a route like 10.1.0.0/16 means "every address that shares these first 16 bits". Longest-prefix match finds the most specific route. A geohash is the same idea for the map: a longer shared prefix means a smaller shared area. A quadtree is like a binary trie of routes, just with four children per node instead of two. Where this breaks: in IP space, adjacent addresses share a prefix, but on the map two points one metre apart can sit on either side of a cell border and share almost no prefix. That is why geo queries also read the neighbour cells.

The story

Explain like I'm 5

You want to buy a toy from a shop across the road. You cannot walk there, so you give your coin to a messenger. The messenger runs across, hands the coin to the shopkeeper, and runs back with a receipt.

One day the messenger does not come back. Did he trip before he reached the shop? Or did the shop get your coin and he lost the receipt on the way home? You do not know. If you send a second coin, you might pay twice. If you do nothing, you might never get the toy.

So you write a number on a sticky note, "Order 7", and tape it to the coin. Now if you send the messenger again with "Order 7", the shopkeeper looks in his notebook, sees "Order 7 already paid", and just gives back a copy of the receipt. Your second trip is safe.

That sticky note is an idempotency key. "Idempotent" is a long word with a simple meaning: doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once. Pressing a lift button five times still calls one lift. The whole of payment-system design is about making every step behave like that lift button, because in a network you can never be sure whether a message arrived.

The messenger is the network. The shop is a PSP (payment service provider, such as Stripe, Razorpay, PayU or Adyen) that talks to the card networks (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay), the banks, or UPI. The notebook is your database. The receipt is the response. If you learn this one story well, you can answer most payment interview questions.

From your world

An idempotency key is the payment version of a TCP sequence number. A receiver that sees a segment it already has does not deliver it again; it just ACKs. Our payment service does the same: it recognises a retried "segment" by its key and re-sends the old ACK (the saved response). Where the analogy breaks: TCP state lives for one connection; an idempotency key must survive crashes, restarts and a different server answering the retry, so it lives in a durable database, not in memory.

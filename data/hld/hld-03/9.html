Interview questions about the round itself

The interviewer says "Design Instagram" and stops talking. What do you do first?

Restate the problem and ask scoping questions. "Instagram does a lot - I'd like to focus on posting photos, following people, and the home feed. Are those the right three? How many daily users should I design for, and is it global?" Then I write the functional list, the non-functional list with numbers (feed loads under a few hundred ms, high availability, eventual consistency is fine for the feed), and what is out of scope (stories, DMs, search).

How to answer

Show the habit: clarify, list, quantify, cut scope. Never name a technology in the first two minutes.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Assume 500M DAU. What's your QPS?" (Do the 10^5 seconds-per-day maths.)
  • "Why is eventual consistency fine for the feed?"
How much time should you spend on estimation?

About three minutes, and only on numbers that change the design. QPS tells me whether one database is enough or I need caching and replicas; storage tells me whether I need sharding or object storage; the read:write ratio tells me whether to optimise reads (caches, precomputation) or writes (append-only logs, queues). If a number changes nothing, I skip it and say so.

How to answer

Give a time box and the rule "do maths when it changes a decision". Name the decision each number drives.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Estimate storage for 5 years of photos." (See the estimation page.)
How do you talk about trade-offs without sounding vague?

I use one sentence pattern: "I'd pick X because of A; the cost is B; if C becomes a problem I'd switch to Y." For example: "I'd fan out the feed on write so reads are instant; the cost is a write storm for celebrities; above a follower threshold I'd switch those accounts to fan-out on read." It shows I know the alternative, I made a choice, and I know when I'd change it.

How to answer

State the pattern and give one concrete example from the design you are in.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What is the threshold, and how would you choose it?" (Measure write cost vs read cost; make it a config.)
You realise mid-design that an earlier choice was wrong. What do you do?

Say it out loud and fix it. "Now that we need per-account ordering, the queue I picked earlier doesn't guarantee order across consumers. I'll switch to Kafka keyed by account ID - here's what changes in the diagram." Interviewers see this as maturity: designs evolve when requirements are understood better. Hiding it, or defending a wrong choice, is what hurts.

How to answer

Show calm self-correction: what you learned, what changes, what stays.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What does the migration look like on a live system?" (Dual-write or outbox to both, backfill, switch readers, remove old.)
The interviewer asks about a technology you have never used. How do you respond?

Be honest and reason from first principles. "I haven't run Cassandra in production. From what I know it's a leaderless, partitioned wide-column store tuned for writes, with tunable consistency through quorums. If that's right, it fits our message log because writes dominate and reads are by partition key. What would you expect to go wrong with it here?" That turns a gap into a conversation and shows I can learn.

How to answer

Admit, state what you do know, reason about fit, ask a good question back.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What would you choose instead, from your own experience?"
The interviewer disagrees with your choice (for example, "Why not just use a SQL database?"). How do you handle it?

Treat it as a question, not an attack. First check what they are probing: "Good question - is the concern operational simplicity, or the joins we'd need?" Then weigh the option honestly against the requirements you agreed: "A single Postgres can take our 5,000 writes per second on average, especially with batched inserts, and it gives us transactions for preferences. The 80,000 per second campaign peaks are the problem - but the Kafka topics already smooth those, so the log writer can drain at a steady rate. If we keep the log for 30 days, Postgres with daily partitions works, and I'd happily switch. If the peak has to be written live, that is when wide-column wins." Either defend with numbers or change your mind with a reason. Both score well; stubbornness and instant surrender both score badly.

How to answer

Clarify the concern, compare against the agreed requirements using your estimates, then decide out loud.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What would make you move off Postgres later?" (Write rate past one primary's limit, or storage past what partitions keep cheap - then shard or move the log.)
  • "How would you migrate without downtime?" (Dual-write, backfill, compare, switch reads, retire.)

Scenario: minute 25, and you only have boxes for one of three requirements

You spent too long on estimates and the data model. The interviewer is looking at the clock.

Weak answer: keep going in depth on the current box and hope there is time.

Strong answer: "I'm conscious of time. Let me sketch the other two flows at a high level now so the design is complete, then come back for depth." Add the missing boxes in two minutes, walk each flow in one sentence, then ask which part to go deep on.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Which deep dive would you pick and why?" (The one tied to the hardest non-functional requirement.)

60-second answer: "Walk me through how you approach a design interview"

"I follow a fixed order so I don't miss anything. First, requirements: three functional ones, a few non-functional ones with numbers, and what's out of scope. Second, quick estimates, only the ones that change the design. Third, the core entities, the API and the data model. Then a simple high-level design that makes every core flow work, and I walk a request through it. Then I pick one or two deep dives tied to the hardest requirement - often consistency, a hot key, or fan-out - and give options with trade-offs. I finish with bottlenecks, failure modes, and what I'd monitor. And I check in with the interviewer at each step."

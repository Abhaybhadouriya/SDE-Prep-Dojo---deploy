The story: the school canteen

Explain like I'm 5

A school canteen starts with one cook who does everything: makes rice, fries snacks, pours juice and takes money. When the school grows, one cook is too slow. So the canteen hires a rice cook, a snack cook, a juice person and a cashier. Each one can work faster, and if the juice machine breaks, kids still get rice.

But now new problems appear. The cashier must know where the snack cook is standing. If the snack cook is very slow, kids pile up in the line and block the rice line too. If every kid keeps shouting "Is it ready? Is it ready?" the snack cook gets even slower. So the canteen makes rules: "Wait at most 2 minutes. If snacks are broken, put up a sign and stop asking. Keep the snack line separate from the rice line. When too many kids come, say 'come back later' at the door." Those rules are this page.

From your world

You already build for failure on the wire: TCP retransmits with exponential backoff, BFD declares a neighbour dead after a few missed hellos, and a router drops packets (tail drop or RED) rather than letting a queue grow forever. Microservices need the same reflexes in user space: every remote call is a lossy, slow link, so it gets a timeout, a bounded retry, a breaker that stops hammering a dead peer, and a queue limit that sheds load.

In software, the one cook is a monolith (one program that does everything). The team of cooks is microservices (many small programs, each owning one job, talking over the network). The canteen rules are resilience patterns: ways to keep working when parts fail. Once you split a system across a network, parts will fail, and they fail in slow, confusing ways. Resilience patterns turn "slow and confusing" into "fast and contained".

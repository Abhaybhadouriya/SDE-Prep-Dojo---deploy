Interview questions

Would you build this new product as microservices or a monolith?

For a new product with one or two teams, start with a modular monolith: one deployable, strict module boundaries, one database with schemas per module. Split a module into a service when there is a concrete reason: a team is blocked by others' release cycle, one part needs very different scaling (for example image processing), or a part needs a different reliability or security boundary (payments). Microservices mostly solve an organisational problem and add network failure, eventual consistency and operational cost. The Prime Video monitoring case (over 90% cost cut by merging a distributed pipeline into one process) shows the cost of splitting where large data flows between steps.

How to answer

Do not pick a side blindly. State the trade-off table (deploy independence and scaling vs network calls, no ACID across services, tracing). Then give your decision rule and a real example.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you split it later without a big-bang rewrite? (strangler fig behind the gateway)
  • What is a distributed monolith?
  • How do you keep data consistent across services? (saga, outbox - hld-10)
How do services find each other? Client-side vs server-side discovery.

A registry maps service names to healthy instances. Instances self-register with heartbeats, or the platform registers them (Kubernetes from readiness). In client-side discovery the caller fetches the list and load-balances itself (Eureka + Ribbon). This means one fewer hop and smarter balancing, but a library is needed in every language. In server-side discovery the caller hits a stable VIP/DNS name and a load balancer or kube-proxy picks the instance. This keeps clients simple, at the cost of one more hop. A service mesh is client-side discovery done by a sidecar, so the app looks server-side.

How to answer

Define registry and registration, compare the two models in one line each, then map to Kubernetes (ClusterIP, DNS, EndpointSlices, readiness).

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens when the registry is down? (clients cache the last list; the registry must be HA, e.g. etcd with Raft)
  • Why can DNS-based discovery be stale? (TTL and client caching)
What is a service mesh? When is it worth the cost?

A mesh puts a proxy (Envoy) beside every service as a sidecar, or per node in ambient/eBPF modes. The proxies form the data plane: they do discovery, load balancing, mTLS, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking and outlier detection, and emit metrics and traces. The control plane (istiod) turns policy into proxy config and issues certificates; it never carries requests. It is worth it with many services in several languages that need uniform mTLS and traffic policy. It is not worth it for a handful of services, because of the extra hops (latency and CPU), the operational complexity, and the risk of retries layered in both app and mesh.

How to answer

Data plane vs control plane, list 4-5 features, then give costs and the decision rule.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does the sidecar intercept traffic? (iptables redirect in the pod network namespace)
  • How do you avoid double retries?
How do you choose a timeout value for a downstream call?

Measure the dependency's latency distribution. Pick an acceptable false-timeout rate (say 0.1%) and set the timeout near that percentile (p99.9), with padding if the tail is very close to the median. Add network latency for internet clients. Make it fit inside the caller's own deadline: propagate an absolute deadline and give each hop the remaining time. Have separate connect and request timeouts, and pre-warm connections so the TLS handshake does not eat the budget (AWS saw exactly that with a 20 ms timeout).

How to answer

Say "from data, not a guess", give the percentile method, then deadline propagation.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the p99.9 is 10 s but users need 1 s? (hedged requests, async design, or cache)
  • What is a hedged request? (send a second copy after the p95 delay, take the first answer, cancel the other)
Why exponential backoff, and why add jitter?

Backoff gives a struggling server time to recover and lowers each client's retry rate. Without jitter, clients that failed together retry together: you get synchronized waves of load at 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms and so on. Full jitter, sleep = random(0, min(cap, base*2^n)), spreads those retries into a near-constant rate. In AWS's simulation with 100 contending clients it cut total calls by more than half and finished sooner than plain backoff. Always cap the backoff and the attempt count.

How to answer

Draw the two timelines (spikes vs flat). Name full, equal and decorrelated jitter. Mention the same trick for scheduled jobs and cache TTLs.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where else does synchronisation hurt? (cron at midnight, cache entries expiring together - jittered TTL)
  • Which errors would you not retry?
A dependency had a 2-minute blip and the whole system stayed down for 40 minutes. What happened and how do you prevent it?

Classic retry storm, which is a metastable failure. The blip caused timeouts, and every layer retried (3 tries at 4 layers is 81x load on the bottom). Clients retried without jitter in waves, queues filled with requests whose callers had already given up, and the system stayed overloaded after the trigger was gone. Prevention: retry at one layer only, with capped jittered backoff; a retry budget (token bucket, or retries at most ~10% of traffic); circuit breakers so callers stop sending; bounded queues and dropping requests past their deadline; and load shedding with priorities at the front. Recovery: shed hard (or block traffic at the edge), let queues drain, then ramp traffic back up.

How to answer

Name the feedback loop, quantify amplification, then list fixes for each link of the loop. Finish with how you would detect it (retry rate metric, queue age).

Likely follow-ups
  • Why does autoscaling not save you? (new pods take minutes to start; the DB may be the bottleneck)
  • What is the difference between a retry budget and a circuit breaker?
Explain the circuit breaker pattern and how you would tune it.

It has three states. Closed: calls pass and results go into a rolling window. Open: once a minimum volume is seen and the failure (or slow-call) rate passes a threshold, calls fail fast with a fallback for a wait period. Half-open: a few trial calls; success closes it, failure re-opens it. Tune from data: the minimum volume (Hystrix 20, resilience4j 100) so a few errors at low traffic do not trip it; the threshold (50% is the common default); a wait time that matches how long the dependency usually takes to recover (Hystrix 5 s, resilience4j 60 s), possibly growing; and the number of half-open probes. Key breakers per host or shard, not globally. Emit an event on every state change.

How to answer

Draw the state machine, give the numbers, then the gotchas: per-shard breakers, retry logic must not retry "circuit open", and the fallback must be cheap.

Likely follow-ups
  • Circuit breaker vs outlier detection?
  • Where do you put it: library or mesh? (mesh for uniform policy; library when you need business fallbacks)
What is a bulkhead? Give a concrete example.

A bulkhead isolates resources so one failing part cannot consume what others need. Example: the product page service calls payments, reviews and recommendations. Give each its own connection pool or semaphore (for example 20/10/10). If recommendations hang, only its 10 slots fill, new calls to it are rejected instantly with a fallback, and payments keep working. At a larger scale: separate pods or clusters per customer tier, separate consumer groups per priority, cells of the whole stack, and Kubernetes resource limits.

How to answer

Ship analogy in one line, the thread-pool example, then the bigger forms (cells, tiers).

Likely follow-ups
  • Thread pool vs semaphore bulkhead?
  • How do you size each pool? (Little's law: rate x p99 latency, plus headroom)
Rate limiting vs load shedding vs backpressure: what is the difference?

Rate limiting enforces a per-client quota against a fixed policy and returns 429. It protects fairness and cost. Load shedding is the server protecting itself from total overload regardless of who sends: it rejects early and cheaply, lowest priority first, based on its own signals such as queue time or in-flight count, and returns 503. It protects goodput. Backpressure is the consumer telling the producer to slow down: bounded queues that block, credit-based flow control, or pull-based consumers like Kafka. You usually need all three.

How to answer

Table format: question, decider, response code. Then give Uber Cinnamon or Netflix adaptive limits as a real shedder.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you decide what to shed first? (priority tiers propagated from the edge)
  • How does Kafka give backpressure for free?
During a flash sale, which features of the product page would you degrade, and how?

Classify every dependency as critical (price, add-to-cart, inventory hold, payment) or optional (recommendations, reviews, Q&A, "people also bought", live stock counter). Optional features sit behind bulkheads and breakers with cached or hidden fallbacks, and can be switched off by feature flags before the sale starts. Critical paths get priority in shedding. Real-time stock shows an approximate value from cache and the exact check happens at the reservation write. Shed browse traffic before checkout traffic. Rehearse with load tests and fault injection before the event.

How to answer

Critical vs optional list, the mechanism for each (flag, breaker, cache), then shedding priority. Link to the flash sale design.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you stop overselling when inventory is degraded? (atomic decrement at the write - d09)
  • What would you never degrade?

Scenario: the recommendations service is slow and checkout is failing

At 8 pm, p99 latency of the recommendations service goes from 80 ms to 8 s. Within two minutes, checkout error rate is 40%, even though checkout does not need recommendations. What happened and what do you do?

Weak answer: "Scale up the checkout service and restart recommendations."

Strong answer: the product and checkout pages share one thread or connection pool in the web tier. Slow recommendation calls hold those threads (Little's law: rate x 8 s), so checkout requests find no free thread. That is resource exhaustion without bulkheads. Right now: turn off recommendations with a feature flag or force its breaker open, so threads free up and checkout recovers. Then find the root cause in recommendations. To prevent a repeat: a 200-300 ms timeout from its p99.9, a separate bulkhead pool, a breaker with a "popular items" fallback, no retries on that call, and an alert on pool saturation.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why would autoscaling the web tier not help? (more pods also block on the same slow dependency)
  • How do you prove the fix works? (fault injection: add a 5 s delay in staging)

Phrases that land well: "Every remote call gets a timeout, from the p99.9." "Retry at one layer, with jitter, within a budget." "Fail fast beats fail slow." "Optional dependencies sit behind a bulkhead and a breaker with a fallback." "Shed early, by priority, and measure goodput, not throughput."

Service mesh and sidecars

Every service needs the same plumbing: retries, timeouts, mTLS, load balancing, metrics, tracing. Writing that in every language is costly. A service mesh moves it out of the app into a proxy. In the classic sidecar model, each pod gets an extra container (Envoy, in Istio) that intercepts all traffic in and out. The app talks plain HTTP to localhost; the sidecar does the hard parts.

Istio splits the mesh into two planes. The data plane is the set of Envoy proxies that touch every request: they do dynamic discovery, load balancing, TLS, HTTP/2 and gRPC proxying, health checks, circuit breakers, retries, fault injection and metrics. The control plane (istiod) never touches requests. It watches Kubernetes for services and endpoints, turns your routing rules into Envoy config, pushes that config to every sidecar, and acts as the certificate authority for mTLS.

Pros of a mesh
  • Same retry/timeout/mTLS policy for every language
  • Change policy with config, not a code deploy
  • Uniform metrics and traces for free
Cons
  • Two extra proxy hops per call (added latency and CPU)
  • One more complex system to run and upgrade
  • Retries in both app and mesh multiply (see below)

Newer sidecar-less modes (Istio ambient, eBPF meshes such as Cilium) move the proxy to a per-node agent; the concepts stay the same.

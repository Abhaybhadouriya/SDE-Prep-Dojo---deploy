The story

Explain like I'm 5

Ten friends want to talk on walkie-talkies from ten different houses. If every friend had to talk into nine walkie-talkies at once, their arms would get tired very fast. So they find a helper who sits in the middle. Each friend talks into one walkie-talkie that goes to the helper. The helper copies each voice and passes it on to everyone else. The helper does not listen or change the words; it just passes them along very quickly. If one friend lives far away, there is a second helper near that friend, and the two helpers pass voices to each other. If a friend's house has a tall wall (a strict front door that does not let strangers in), the friend shouts over the wall to a relay tower that hands the voice in and out.

That is the whole design in kid language. The helper in the middle is a Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU), a media server that forwards packets without decoding them. The second helper is a cascaded SFU in another region. The tall wall is a NAT or firewall, and the relay tower is a TURN server. The friends' rule "talk into one walkie-talkie, not nine" is why nobody serious uses peer-to-peer mesh for group calls.

Why interviewers ask it: it is the hardest of the "real-time" designs. Chat and live comments are about pushing small messages; here the payload is a continuous stream of audio and video packets where late is the same as lost. The awesome-system-design-resources list puts "Design Zoom" in its hard tier, Microsoft builds Teams, and Amazon builds Chime. For you, it is the best design on this site: the deep dives are UDP, NAT, congestion control, jitter buffers and packet forwarding, which is your home ground.

Say this early: "I will split the system into a control plane (scheduling, join, signalling over WebSocket) and a media plane (RTP over UDP through SFUs). They scale differently and fail differently." Every good answer to this question is built on that split.

From your world

This is a router problem wearing an app costume. The control plane (signalling, meeting state, SFU assignment) is like your routing protocol daemons: slow-ish, reliable, stateful. The data plane (the SFU forwarding RTP packets) is like the forwarding ASIC: it must never wait on the control plane for each packet. Simulcast layer selection is like per-port QoS shaping, and RTCP feedback is the flow-control loop.

New words, once each: SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit) forwards media packets without decoding them; an MCU (Multipoint Control Unit) decodes and mixes them into one stream. WebRTC is the browser standard for real-time audio and video; RTP is the packet format that carries the media over UDP. NAT traversal is getting packets through home routers and firewalls, using STUN (a server that tells you your public address) and TURN (a relay when no direct path works). Simulcast means a sender uploads the same video at 2-3 qualities so the SFU can pick one per receiver. Signalling is the control chat (join, leave, mute) that sets up the media path, usually over a WebSocket. Jitter is variation in packet delay; p99 means "99 out of 100 are faster than this".

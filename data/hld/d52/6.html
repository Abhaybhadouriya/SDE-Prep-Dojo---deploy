Deep dives

1. Mesh vs MCU vs SFU: the topology choice

This is the first real decision, and interviewers expect the maths. Take a 10-person meeting where each camera stream is about 1.5 Mbps at 720p (LiveKit's typical figure).

TopologyClient upload (N=10)Client downloadServer CPUGood for
Mesh (peer-to-peer)9 x 1.5 = 13.5 Mbps (most home uplinks cannot)9 streamsnone (only TURN when needed)2 people. Zoom says 2-person meetings can use P2P.
MCU (decode, mix, re-encode)1.5 Mbps1 mixed streamvery high: decode 10, encode 10, per meetingLegacy room systems, phone dial-in, very weak clients
SFU (selective forward)1.5 Mbps (or about 2 Mbps with 3 simulcast layers)up to 9 streams, sized per tilelow: forwarding only (Jitsi: 550 Mbps at about 20% of a quad-core)Almost everything today: Zoom, Teams, Meet, Discord, LiveKit, Jitsi

Zoom's own explanation of its approach is that legacy systems used an MCU to pick or mix streams before sending them, which costs a lot of compute, while Zoom's multimedia routing sends the individual streams to each client and leaves decoding to the clients. Discord rejected peer-to-peer outright because its cost grows with participant count. The answer is: P2P for 1:1 if you want to save server cost, SFU for groups, MCU-like mixing only for recording and phone gateways.

2. Getting through NAT and firewalls: ICE, STUN, TURN

Most laptops sit behind a NAT: the home router owns one public IP and rewrites private addresses and ports. You cannot just send UDP to someone's private 192.168.x.x address. WebRTC solves this with ICE (Interactive Connectivity Establishment, RFC 8445): gather every possible address ("candidates"), exchange them through signalling, and try pairs until one works.

  1. Host candidate The local interface address. Works on the same LAN.
  2. Server-reflexive candidate (STUN) The client sends a packet to a STUN server, which replies "I saw you as 49.37.x.x:61022". That is the public mapping the NAT created. Cheap: STUN servers only answer tiny requests.
  3. Relay candidate (TURN) If the NAT is symmetric (it creates a different mapping per destination and only accepts packets from peers you already contacted, as MDN describes), the public address from STUN is useless to anyone else. Then the client allocates a relay address on a TURN server (RFC 8656) and all media flows through it. Expensive: TURN carries the full media bandwidth.
  4. TCP/TLS 443 fallback Some corporate firewalls block all outbound UDP. The client then reaches TURN or the SFU over TLS on port 443, which looks like HTTPS. Quality drops a bit because TCP retransmits late packets that you would rather drop, but the call works. Zoom documents exactly this order: UDP, then TCP/TLS including 443.

An SFU design gets a big simplification here: the SFU has a public address. The client only needs an outbound UDP flow to it, which every NAT allows, plus periodic keepalives so the NAT mapping does not expire. Discord says it skips ICE entirely for this reason; it also improves reliability behind NATs and hides each participant's IP from the others. You still keep TURN/TLS on 443 for the locked-down networks.

From your world

A NAT binding is a conntrack entry. "Endpoint-independent mapping" means one entry per inside 5-tuple regardless of destination; "symmetric" means a new entry per destination, which is why a STUN-learned port is useless to a third party. Keepalive pings every 15-25 seconds are there because UDP conntrack timeouts are short (often 30 seconds on consumer routers). TURN is just a user-space UDP relay with allocations and permissions, and it costs you the full media bandwidth, so you size the TURN fleet like a transit link, not like a DNS server.

3. Bandwidth adaptation: simulcast, SVC, congestion control

Receivers are wildly different: one is on office fibre watching a full-screen speaker, another is on a train with a 180-pixel tile. Two techniques let one sender serve both.

  • Simulcast: the sender encodes several independent versions of its camera (Chrome allows up to 3 for HD video and 1 for screen share, according to webrtcHacks). The SFU switches each receiver between layers. Cost: more upload and more encoder CPU on the sender.
  • SVC (Scalable Video Coding): one stream with dependent layers: a base layer plus layers that add resolution (spatial), frame rate (temporal) or quality. The SFU drops the upper layers for weak receivers. Zoom has long described using SVC, often quoted as one roughly 1.2 Mbps layered stream that can be trimmed down. VP9 and AV1 support SVC; VP8 gives temporal layers only.

How does the SFU know what fits? Congestion control. Receivers send RTCP feedback (receiver reports with loss and jitter, plus bandwidth estimates such as REMB or transport-wide congestion control), and the SFU estimates each receiver's downlink. It then picks the highest layer under that estimate and also tells senders how much they may send (Discord's SFU processes RTCP receiver reports and tells senders how much video bandwidth is available). Add application rules on top:

  • Audio first. Audio is about 60-80 kbps and drives the conversation. Under pressure, drop video layers, then pause video, and keep audio. Zoom says audio is prioritised over video, which is how it stays usable at very high loss.
  • Active speaker high, others low. The dominant speaker gets the high layer; gallery tiles get the low one.
  • Last-N. In a 300-person meeting only forward video for the N most recent speakers (for example 25 for a gallery page). Everyone else sends nothing or the SFU does not forward it. Clients also pause video for tiles that are off screen.
  • Switch on keyframes. A receiver can only start decoding a new layer at a keyframe, so on a layer switch the SFU asks the sender for one (an RTCP PLI). Too many switches cause a keyframe storm; add hysteresis.
  • Loss repair. NACK-based retransmission for short round trips, forward error correction (FEC, for example Opus in-band FEC for audio) for longer ones, and a jitter buffer at the receiver that trades a few tens of milliseconds of delay for smooth playback.

From your world

This is TCP congestion control re-implemented for media, minus the retransmit-everything rule. The bandwidth estimate is like cwnd; RTCP feedback is the ACK clock; switching to a lower layer is a multiplicative decrease that keeps the "flow" alive instead of filling the bottleneck queue. Delay-based estimators (rising one-way delay means a queue is building) are close cousins of TCP Vegas and BBR. The jitter buffer is a playout ring buffer sized from measured inter-arrival variance.

4. Placement and cascading across regions

Latency is set by geography. Zoom routes participants to the nearest data centre and the least-loaded server. But a meeting has many participants, and they are not all in one city.

  • Single SFU per meeting (simplest, and what LiveKit self-hosted and Discord-per-guild do): everyone connects to one server. Problem: whoever is far from it pays. webrtcHacks estimates that in about a third of three-person meetings with one remote participant, the single server ends up in the "wrong" place if you place it by the first joiner.
  • Cascaded SFUs: each participant connects to the nearest SFU, and SFUs exchange streams over the provider's backbone. Jitsi's Octo protocol does this between bridges in six AWS regions. Retransmissions now happen over short local hops, which repairs loss faster. Cost: one more hop of latency for remote pairs, and inter-region bandwidth.
  • Room size limits: a single server has finite egress. 1,000 participants receiving 25 tiles each is a lot of egress, so very large meetings must cascade even inside one region (a tree of SFUs: one "origin" receives publishers, others fan out to viewers).
  1. One server. The meeting was placed in Mumbai because Asha joined first. Ben and Clara in Europe each send and receive over a long, lossy intercontinental path.
  2. Nearest SFU each. Placement gives the European participants a London SFU for the same meeting. Each person now has a short last-mile hop.
  3. Cascade link. The two SFUs exchange the streams their local receivers subscribed to, over the private backbone, once per stream (not once per receiver).
  4. Result. Lower jitter, faster loss repair on short hops, and inter-region traffic proportional to streams rather than to participants.

5. Failures: SFU crash, region loss, join storms

  • SFU process crash. Keep the SFU's state reconstructible. Discord's signalling component restarts a crashed SFU and rebuilds its stream tables without the clients doing anything, losing only a few packets; the same trick gives zero-downtime SFU upgrades.
  • SFU host dies. It stops sending health pings, service discovery removes it, the clients notice their media and signalling sockets die, ask for a new assignment, and the placement service moves the whole meeting (or that cascade leg) to a new server. Clients re-publish; target a 2-5 second blip. Zoom notes that failover between regions for real-time services requires a reconnect.
  • DDoS on a media server. Media servers have public IPs and are attractive targets. Discord watches the incoming packet rate, removes the attacked server from rotation and moves guilds elsewhere until it calms down.
  • Top-of-the-hour join storm. 25K joins per second hits signalling, auth and placement. Pre-scale before :00 and :30 (scheduled meetings tell you the load in advance), make placement a cheap in-memory decision from etcd watch data, and add jittered retries in clients.
  • Degrade on purpose. When Teams hit pandemic load, Microsoft switched off the typing indicator for two weeks (30% less peak traffic on some services) and suppressed mobile calendar prefetch (80% fewer requests). In meetings the equivalent levers are: cap gallery size, lower the default send resolution, turn off non-essential features like reactions animation.

6. Recording and end-to-end encryption

Recording is the one place you do decode. A recorder is a headless client (a bot) that joins the meeting as a hidden subscriber, receives the streams like any participant, composites a layout, encodes to MP4 and uploads parts to object storage. Because it is just another subscriber, the SFU needs no special logic, and recorders scale as a separate worker pool. After upload, an event goes to Kafka, a transcoding job makes lower renditions (see Video Upload & Transcoding) and the notification service tells the host.

End-to-end encryption (E2EE) means the SFU cannot read the media at all: clients encrypt frames with keys the server never sees (WebRTC insertable streams allow this), and the SFU forwards encrypted frames using only the RTP header. Trade-off: server-side recording, transcription and phone dial-in no longer work, because no server can decode. That is why products make E2EE an opt-in mode.

Interviewers love this: "Why is the SFU cheap?" Because it never touches the payload. It reads RTP headers, picks packets and rewrites sequence numbers and timestamps. That is also exactly why E2EE is compatible with an SFU but not with an MCU.

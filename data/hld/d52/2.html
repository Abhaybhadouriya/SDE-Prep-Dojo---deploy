Back-of-envelope estimation

The method is from Back-of-Envelope Estimation. We anchor on Microsoft Teams' published numbers and on Zoom's published per-user bandwidth, then derive everything else. Say each assumption out loud.

~2.9Maverage concurrent participants
~8.5Mpeak concurrent
~15 Tbpspeak SFU egress
~25K/sjoins at top of the hour
  1. Concurrency from minutes 4.1B meeting minutes per day (Microsoft Teams, April 2020, from the Azure blog in Resources) / 1,440 minutes in a day = about 2.85M participants in a meeting at any average moment. Work days are bursty (everything happens 9 am to 6 pm local), so take peak = 3x average = about 8.5M concurrent participants.
  2. Per-person bandwidth Zoom's own guidance for group video is about 1.0 Mbps up and 0.6 Mbps down at "high quality", 2.6 up / 1.8 down for 720p, and about 2.0 Mbps to receive a 25-tile gallery. Audio alone is 60-80 kbps. Use a blended planning number of 1.0 Mbps up and 1.8 Mbps down per participant.
  3. SFU traffic Ingress = 8.5M x 1.0 Mbps = 8.5 Tbps. Egress = 8.5M x 1.8 Mbps = about 15 Tbps. Egress is bigger than ingress because the SFU copies each stream to many receivers. This is a bandwidth and packets-per-second business, not a storage business.
  4. Packets per second Video RTP packets are close to the MTU, say 1,200 bytes. 15 Tbps / (1,200 x 8 bits) = about 1.6 billion packets per second of egress at peak. Sanity check: Discord reported 220 Gbps and 120 Mpps for 2.6M concurrent (mostly voice) users, which is about 230 bytes per packet, typical of small audio packets.
  5. Number of media servers Jitsi measured one quad-core Xeon forwarding 1,056 streams at 550 Mbps with about 20% CPU, so forwarding is cheap per bit. Plan on a modern server safely doing about 2 Gbps of egress (a planning assumption; you would load-test it). 15 Tbps / 2 Gbps = about 7,500 SFU servers. Zoom says it keeps 50% excess capacity, so plan about 11,000, spread across 15-30 regions. Discord ran its voice with 850+ servers in 13 regions, so this is the right order of magnitude for a much larger, video-heavy load.
  6. Join rate Microsoft reported about 200M daily meeting participants (same April 2020 post). Check it against the minutes: 4.1B / 200M = about 20 minutes per participant per day, which is roughly one meeting each, so assume ~200M joins a day (an assumption; people who join several meetings push it higher). 200M / 86,400 s = about 2,300 joins per second on average. But meetings start at :00 and :30, so a large share of joins land in the same minute. Take 10x = about 25K joins per second for the signalling and meeting service. That spike, not the steady state, sizes the control plane.
  7. Signalling state Per participant: who they are, which SFU, which tracks they publish, which layers they want, mute state: under 2 KB. 8.5M x 2 KB = 17 GB. It fits in a sharded in-memory store (Redis) keyed by meeting id.
  8. Recording storage Assume 5% of minutes are recorded and the composite recording is about 1 Mbps (7.5 MB per minute). 4.1B x 5% = 205M minutes x 7.5 MB = about 1.5 PB per day. That number is why recordings go straight to object storage with lifecycle rules (cheaper tiers after 30 days, delete after the retention period).

What the numbers tell you: the database is small. The control plane is a spiky 25K joins per second problem. The media plane is a 15 Tbps, 1.6 billion packets per second forwarding problem spread across thousands of servers close to users. Recording is a petabyte-a-day blob problem. Three different systems.

From your world

1.6 Gpps is the language of line-rate forwarding. An SFU is a user-space packet switch: it receives SRTP, looks at the SSRC (stream id) in the RTP header, decides which receivers get this packet, rewrites sequence numbers and timestamps, and sends it out. Teams that push this hard use the same tricks you know from kernel networking: recvmmsg/sendmmsg batching, SO_REUSEPORT to spread sockets over cores, large socket buffers, NIC queue pinning, and sometimes kernel bypass.

Interview: drive the 45 minutes

  1. 0-5 min: scope Group video meetings up to about 100 interactive people, screen share, chat, cloud recording. State the latency budget (under about 300 ms mouth-to-ear) and that late equals lost.
  2. 5-10 min: estimate Concurrent participants from meeting minutes, then bandwidth up and down, then server count. Point out that egress, not storage, is the big number.
  3. 10-25 min: high-level design Draw the control plane (API, meeting DB, placement, signalling) and the media plane (SFU, STUN/TURN) separately. Walk through a join.
  4. 25-40 min: deep dives Let the interviewer choose, but have ready: mesh vs SFU vs MCU maths, NAT traversal, simulcast/SVC with congestion control, cascading, SFU failover.
  5. 40-45 min: wrap up Recording pipeline, E2EE trade-off, monitoring (per-participant QoS into Kafka, alert on loss and jitter by region).

60-second answer, said out loud: "I split it into a control plane and a media plane. The control plane is an API, a meeting database and a signalling service over WebSocket that places each meeting on a nearby SFU. The media plane is SFUs that receive one stream from each sender and forward copies to receivers without decoding, over UDP with RTP. A mesh breaks above a handful of people and an MCU costs too much CPU, so SFU is the default. Clients use ICE with STUN and fall back to TURN, even TURN over TLS on 443. Senders simulcast, and the SFU picks a layer per receiver using congestion feedback. Big or global meetings cascade SFUs across regions over the backbone. Recording is a bot that joins and writes to object storage. Egress bandwidth, not storage, is the big number: about 15 Tbps at peak in my estimate."

SDE-1 stops here / SDE-2 adds. SDE-1: control plane vs media plane, why SFU beats mesh for groups, UDP not TCP for media, STUN/TURN in one line each, recording to object storage, the :00 join spike. SDE-2 adds: simulcast vs SVC and per-receiver layer choice from congestion control, cascading SFUs, SFU failover and rejoin, ICE candidate ordering, E2EE and why it limits server-side features, and per-participant QoS telemetry through Kafka.

You might confuse this with an MCU. An SFU forwards packets and never decodes them, so it is cheap per stream but each receiver gets several streams. An MCU decodes, mixes and re-encodes one composite stream per receiver: easy on weak clients, very expensive on servers, and it adds latency. Also do not confuse STUN (tells you your public address, carries no media) with TURN (relays all your media when nothing else works).

Your strongest card: you can talk about UDP, NAT, MTU, congestion control and packet forwarding more fluently than most candidates. Use it, but keep tying it back to the user ("this is why the call does not freeze on a train").

Why not just use peer-to-peer for every meeting? It has no server cost.

Because each sender must upload a separate copy to every other participant. In a 10-person call at 1.5 Mbps per 720p stream, that is 13.5 Mbps of upload per person, more than most home and mobile uplinks can sustain, and each client also encodes many times. Mesh is fine for 2 (Zoom can use P2P for two-person meetings) and maybe 3. For groups an SFU turns N-1 uploads into one, and the server picks a layer per receiver. Discord rejected P2P for exactly this cost-grows-with-participants reason.

How to answer

Do the upload arithmetic out loud, then state the rule: P2P for 1:1, SFU for groups. Mention that even 1:1 often goes through the server when NAT traversal fails or for consistent features.

Likely follow-ups
  • When would you still use an MCU?
  • How does the 1:1 call upgrade to an SFU when a third person joins?
Explain SFU vs MCU, and which one Zoom and Teams use.

An MCU decodes every incoming stream, mixes audio, composites video into one layout and re-encodes one stream per receiver. Low client bandwidth, but huge server CPU and a fixed layout. An SFU forwards encoded packets without decoding, picking which streams and which quality layer each receiver gets. Client download is higher but the server is cheap and the client controls layout and per-person volume. Zoom describes its multimedia router as routing individual streams to clients instead of MCU-style processing, and modern Teams, Meet, Discord and LiveKit are all SFU-based. Mixing survives in recorders and phone gateways.

How to answer

One sentence each, then a table of upload/download/CPU, then the "who uses what" fact. End with where mixing still exists.

Likely follow-ups
  • How many streams can one SFU forward?
  • How does E2EE interact with each?
A participant is in a hotel where UDP is blocked. How do they still join?

ICE tries candidates in order of cost. Direct UDP to the SFU fails, a TURN relay over UDP fails, so the client connects over TCP with TLS on port 443 (to TURN or straight to the SFU's TLS listener). To the firewall it looks like HTTPS. The media is still SRTP inside, but now over TCP, so a lost segment delays everything behind it; the client compensates by lowering its bitrate and the SFU sends that user lower layers. Zoom documents this exact fallback order (UDP, then TCP/TLS including 443).

How to answer

Name ICE, list the candidate ladder (host, STUN reflexive, TURN UDP, TURN TLS 443), then explain why TCP is worse for media (head-of-line blocking).

Likely follow-ups
  • What is a symmetric NAT and why does STUN not help with it?
  • How do you size the TURN fleet?
One participant has a slow connection. How do you keep the call good for everyone else?

Make adaptation per receiver, not per meeting. Senders publish simulcast (or SVC) layers. The SFU estimates each receiver's downlink from RTCP feedback and forwards the highest layer that fits, so the slow receiver gets 180p while others keep 720p. Without simulcast, the sender would have to encode for the weakest receiver and everyone would suffer. Under more pressure: pause video for that user's off-screen tiles, keep audio (60-80 kbps) at all costs, and use FEC for audio. If the slow user is a sender, their client lowers its own send layers based on the SFU's feedback.

How to answer

Say "per-receiver layer selection" first. Then the feedback loop (RTCP, bandwidth estimation). Then the priority order: audio, active speaker, gallery.

Likely follow-ups
  • Simulcast vs SVC: which would you pick?
  • What happens at the moment of a layer switch? (keyframe request)
A meeting has people in India, Europe and the US. Where do you put the media server?

Not in one place. Give each participant the nearest SFU in their region and cascade the SFUs over the backbone, so each stream crosses an ocean once, not once per viewer. Placement keeps a per-meeting map of which regional SFUs are in the cascade (in etcd or Redis). Retransmissions happen on short local hops, which repairs loss faster. Cost: one extra hop for cross-region pairs and inter-region bandwidth. For small local meetings, one SFU is simpler and better. webrtcHacks shows that placing a single SFU by "first joiner" picks the wrong place surprisingly often (about a third of three-person calls with one remote participant).

How to answer

Start with the naive single-server choice and its failure, then cascading, then when not to cascade. Mention Jitsi Octo or Zoom meeting zones as real examples.

Likely follow-ups
  • Full mesh between SFUs or a tree?
  • Which SFU runs dominant-speaker detection?
The SFU hosting a 50-person meeting crashes. What happens?

Two cases. If the SFU process crashes, a supervisor on the same host restarts it and the signalling layer replays the meeting's stream table (who publishes which SSRCs, who subscribes to what). Clients keep the same address and lose a few packets; Discord describes exactly this. If the host dies, it misses health pings, discovery removes it, clients see their sockets drop and call placement, placement assigns a new SFU for the meeting, and clients re-publish and re-subscribe. The meeting roster lives in Redis, not in the SFU, so nobody is "kicked out". Target a 2-5 second interruption with audio coming back first.

How to answer

Separate process crash from host crash. Stress that the SFU holds only rebuildable state, and that meeting state lives in the control plane.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you avoid all 50 clients reconnecting to different SFUs?
  • How would you do a zero-downtime SFU upgrade?
Why does media use UDP when almost everything else on the web uses TCP?

For a live call, a packet that arrives 500 ms late is worthless: the moment has passed. TCP guarantees in-order delivery, so one lost segment blocks every later packet until it is retransmitted (head-of-line blocking), causing freezes. UDP lets the receiver skip the lost packet, conceal it (audio codecs interpolate) and keep playing. Reliability is then added selectively: NACK retransmission only if there is time, FEC for audio, keyframe requests for video.

How to answer

"Late equals lost" in the first line, then head-of-line blocking, then how WebRTC adds just enough reliability on top of UDP.

Likely follow-ups
  • Would QUIC/WebTransport change this?
  • What is a jitter buffer and how big should it be?
How do you support a 1,000-person all-hands where only a few people talk?

Use the fact that attention is limited. Only forward video for the last-N active speakers (for example the current speaker plus a gallery page of 25), and audio for the top few loudest speakers, chosen by audio level indicators in the RTP header extension. Viewers who are not on stage publish nothing. Fan out through a tree of SFUs (one origin receives speakers, edge SFUs serve viewers) so no single server's egress is exceeded. Past a few thousand, switch the audience to a one-way streaming mode (HLS/DASH via CDN, seconds of delay) and keep only the panel on WebRTC.

How to answer

Identify that it is now mostly one-to-many. Name last-N, SFU tree fan-out, and the cut-over to CDN streaming.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does an audience member raise a hand and get promoted to speaker?
  • What latency would the CDN audience see?
How does cloud recording work without slowing the live meeting?

Treat the recorder as just another participant. A recorder service joins as a hidden subscriber, receives the streams through the normal SFU, decodes and composites a layout, encodes MP4, and uploads parts to object storage with multipart upload. It runs in its own autoscaled worker pool, so recording load never competes with forwarding. On finish it emits recording-ready to Kafka; a transcoder makes renditions and the notification service emails the host. About 1.5 PB a day at our assumed 5% recording rate means lifecycle rules (cool tier after 30 days, delete after retention).

How to answer

"Recorder is a bot subscriber" is the key idea. Then storage maths and the async pipeline.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the recorder crashes halfway?
  • Can you record an E2EE meeting?
The top of every hour, joins spike and some users wait 20 seconds to get in. How do you fix it?

First measure which step is slow: auth, the meeting DB lookup, placement, signalling connect or ICE. Typical fixes: pre-scale signalling and placement a few minutes before :00 and :30 using the schedule (you literally know tomorrow's load); make placement an in-memory decision from a watched copy of SFU load instead of a synchronous query; cache meeting settings; add jitter to client retries so a failure does not create a synchronised retry wave; warm TURN allocations; and pre-assign an SFU when a scheduled meeting is 5 minutes away. Microsoft's Teams team used capacity forecasting and time-of-day balancing for the same kind of spikes.

How to answer

Measure, then attack the synchronised nature of the load: predictability lets you pre-scale, jitter breaks waves.

Likely follow-ups
  • What metrics would you alert on?
  • How would you load-test this?

Scenario: "Video freezes for everyone when one person joins from a train"

In a 12-person meeting, every time one participant on a train joins, everyone else's view of the shared slides becomes blurry and stutters.

Weak answer: "Add more SFU servers and increase their bandwidth."

Strong answer: this smells like the sender is encoding for the weakest receiver, which means simulcast or SVC is off for screen share, or the SFU is reacting to one receiver's congestion by asking the sender to slow down for all. Fix: per-receiver layer selection; screen share uses temporal scalability (lower frame rate, full resolution) so text stays sharp; the SFU only lowers what that receiver gets. Verify by looking at per-participant QoS in the media-qos stream.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why is screen share usually one layer in Chrome?
  • What would you put on the on-call dashboard?

Scenario: a whole region degrades

A network provider in one region has a routing problem. Packet loss to the SFUs there jumps to 20% for an hour.

Weak answer: "Failover the region's database."

Strong answer: media is the problem, not data. Detect it from QoS telemetry (loss by region and ASN). Stop placing new meetings in that region (mark its SFUs draining in discovery), place them in the next-nearest region, and for live meetings offer an automatic migrate (clients reconnect to a new SFU). Meanwhile clients survive on adaptation: audio is prioritised, FEC increases, video drops layers. Zoom documents that real-time cross-region failover needs a reconnect, so make that reconnect fast and quiet.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you avoid overloading the neighbour region?
  • Would anycast help here?

Common mistakes

Common mistakes: one box labelled "video server" with no SFU/MCU choice; sending media over WebSocket or Kafka; forgetting NAT traversal; peer-to-peer for big groups; no adaptation per receiver; storing video in a database; ignoring the top-of-the-hour join spike; placing a global meeting on one server.

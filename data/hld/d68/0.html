The story

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a tiny shop hidden behind your apartment building. Nobody walks in; it has no door for customers. Inside, shelves are packed with the 5,000 things people in your area buy most. When your mum taps "milk" on her phone, a helper inside grabs the milk, puts it in a bag, and a bike rider who is already waiting outside zooms it to your door. Because the shop is only 2 km away, it takes 10 minutes.

The tricky part: the phone must only show things that are really on that shop's shelf. If the app says "milk: yes" but the last packet was sold a minute ago, your mum is sad, the helper wastes time looking, and the rider waits.

The hidden shop is a dark store: a small warehouse (a micro-fulfilment centre) that serves only online orders for a few kilometres around it. The helper is a picker (and packer). The rider is the last-mile delivery partner. Behind the dark stores is a bigger mother warehouse (distribution centre) that refills them, often daily.

Unlike Amazon, where one product page is the same for everyone, here everything is local: what you can see, its price, whether it is in stock and how fast it arrives all depend on which dark store serves your address.

New words, once each:

  • SKU (stock keeping unit) = one sellable item variant: "Amul Taaza 500 ml" is one SKU. WMS (warehouse management system) = the software that tracks every unit inside a warehouse or dark store: receiving, shelving (bin locations), picking, damages, counts.
  • On hand = units physically in the store. Reserved = units promised to orders not yet picked. ATP (available to promise) = on hand minus reserved minus a safety buffer. The app sells ATP, not on hand.
  • Serviceability = "which dark store (if any) serves this address right now, and how fast?"
  • GRN (goods received note) = the record that a truck's stock arrived and was counted in. Cycle count = counting a few shelves every day to find drift between system and reality.
  • Optimistic vs pessimistic locking: check-at-write vs lock-first. Outbox = write the event in the same DB transaction as the change, publish later.

From your world

ATP = on hand - reserved - buffer is credit-based flow control. A sender may only transmit while it holds credits; each frame spends one, and the receiver returns credits as it frees buffers. Here each order spends units of ATP at checkout (a reservation), and picking or a cancel gives them back. The safety buffer is the headroom you keep so a miscount does not overflow the "receiver" (the shelf). You can also read a store's ATP for one SKU as a counting semaphore: down() on reserve, up() on cancel or reservation expiry, and the value never goes below zero. Per-store sharding is per-CPU data: each store's stock lives on one shard, the way per-CPU counters avoid cross-core locking; only a rare cross-store query has to sum across shards. Where the analogy breaks: credits in a NIC are exact, but shelf counts drift (damage, misplacement), so the system needs cycle counts to re-sync the "credit" number with reality.

Back-of-envelope estimation

These are round assumptions for one large Indian player, not any company's real figures. Press reports in 2025-26 put the big players at roughly one to two thousand dark stores each; we take 1,000 to keep the arithmetic easy.

  1. Orders. 1,000 stores x 1,500 orders per store per day = 1.5 million orders a day. Divide by ~100,000 s (a day is 86,400 s, rounded up for easy maths, see Estimation): about 15 orders/s average. Evening peak at 4x: about 60 orders/s nationally, which is only about 1 order every 15 seconds per store on average, maybe 1 every 5 seconds in a busy store. Conclusion: total throughput is small; the challenge is latency and correctness, not raw scale.
  2. Inventory rows. 1,000 stores x 6,000 SKUs = 6 million (store, SKU) rows x 100 bytes = 600 MB. Conclusion: the whole inventory fits in memory many times over.
  3. Inventory writes. Each order has about 8 lines; each line causes a reserve, a pick and maybe a release: about 3 writes per line. 60 orders/s x 8 x 3 = about 1,500 writes/s at peak, plus inbound GRNs and counts. Spread over 1,000 stores, it is tiny per store, but hot SKUs (milk, bread, eggs at 7 am) concentrate on a few rows.
  4. Search and browse. Assume 20 searches and category views per order, and 3x more sessions than orders (people browse and leave): 1.5M x 20 x 3 = 90 million reads a day, about 1,000/s average, 5,000-10,000/s at peak. Every result must be filtered by one store's stock. Conclusion: we need a store-aware search index or a fast stock filter in memory.
  5. Riders and location. Assume 20 riders per store at peak = 20,000 riders, each sending GPS every 5 s: 4,000 location writes/s. Conclusion: a location stream in Redis/Kafka, not a SQL table.
  6. The clock. 10 minutes = about 1 min order to picker, 2-3 min pick and pack, 5-6 min ride. Software decisions (store lookup, reservation, assignment) must take milliseconds to a few seconds; the rest is physical.

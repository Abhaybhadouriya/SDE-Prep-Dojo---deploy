Deep dives

1. Search scoped to one dark store

A normal e-commerce search ranks the whole catalogue. Here a result is useless unless it is in stock at this store, now. Three designs, with different trade-offs:

DesignHowProsCons
A. One doc per (store, SKU)Index 6M documents with store_id and in_stock; query filters store_id = X AND in_stockOne query, exact filtering and paging, ranking can use store salesEvery stock flip is an index update (refresh about 1 s); 1,000x duplicated product text; heavy reindex
B. Catalogue index + stock filterES returns top 200 product ids (one doc per SKU); a filter removes ids not in the store's in-stock set in Redis; return top 20Small index; stock changes touch only Redis; very freshDeep pages may run out of in-stock hits (fetch more); two hops
C. Per-store in-memory indexEach store's ~6K SKUs fit in a few MB; a search service holds them in memory, sharded by storeFastest; per-store ranking easyYou build and run a custom search tier

My pick and why: B for text search (the store's 6,000 SKUs are a small subset of the catalogue, so 200 candidates almost always contain 20 in stock), and precomputed per-store category pages (a store's "Dairy" shelf is a short list rebuilt on every stock event and cached). Flag in-stock flips with a few seconds of delay at most; the reservation step catches the rest.

Short TTL plus invalidation, both. The Swiggy SDE-3 report mentions exactly this: a short-TTL inventory cache with invalidation on dark-store updates (post). Invalidation alone is fragile (a lost event means a wrong answer forever); TTL alone is either stale or expensive. Events give freshness; the TTL bounds the damage when an event is lost.

2. Reserving stock: the last packet of milk

Two customers check out the last two packets of milk at once, with carts of 8 lines each. Options:

  • Conditional update per line (my default): in one transaction on the store's shard, for each line UPDATE store_inventory SET reserved = reserved + :q WHERE store_id = :s AND sku_id = :k AND on_hand - reserved - safety_buf >= :q. Lock lines in sku_id order to avoid deadlocks. If any line returns 0 rows, roll back and return the short lines. Each row lock is held for microseconds.
  • Pessimistic (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on all lines, check, update): same correctness, longer locks, more deadlock risk; only worth it if the check needs logic SQL cannot express (bundles, substitutions).
  • Optimistic with versions: read all rows, compute, write WHERE version = :v, retry on conflict. Fine for slow-moving SKUs; bad for milk at 7 am, where most attempts conflict and retry.
  • Redis-first reservation (Lua script decrementing a per-store counter): fastest, but Redis becomes a second copy of the truth that must be reconciled after every failover. Use it only as a gate if one SKU is extremely hot (a viral launch), with the database still the record.

When to reserve? At add-to-cart (hold early) or at checkout (hold late)? Holding at add-to-cart locks stock in thousands of abandoned carts. Holding at checkout risks "sorry, gone" at the last step. Most quick-commerce apps reserve at checkout with a short timer and show live "only 2 left" hints earlier. Say this trade-off out loud.

Release: unpaid reservations expire after a few minutes (a sweeper with SKIP LOCKED), cancellations release at once, and every reserve/release is an inventory_movement row with a unique id so a retried message is applied once.

3. Keeping inventory consistent across WMS, store and app

This was the focus of a Swiggy SDE-2 Instamart round: design the warehouse system and keep inventory consistent across systems (post). The trap is having three systems that each think they own the number.

  1. One writer. The inventory service owns on_hand and reserved. The WMS and picker apps send movements (GRN +48, PICK -1, DAMAGE -2, COUNT_ADJUST -3), never absolute numbers. Absolute "set stock to 17" messages from two systems overwrite each other; deltas with ids compose.
  2. A ledger. Every movement is a row with a unique id, applied in the same transaction as the counter change, plus an outbox row. Replaying the ledger rebuilds the number; a duplicate message hits the unique id and is ignored.
  3. Per-store ordering. Movements and stock.changed events are keyed by store_id on Kafka, so consumers see a store's changes in order; each event carries the row's version, and consumers drop anything older than what they have.
  4. Reality wins. The physical shelf is the final truth. Cycle counts and picker "bin empty" reports create COUNT_ADJUST movements. Track the shrinkage rate (system says more than the shelf) per store as a key metric.
  5. Safety buffer. For SKUs with frequent count drift, hide 1-2 units from ATP. It trades a little lost sales for far fewer "ordered but missing" refunds. Tune per SKU from the miss rate.
  6. Inbound. A GRN adds stock only after the truck is counted in and shelved, not when it leaves the mother warehouse; in-transit stock is a separate number used for forecasting, not for selling.

4. Pickers, riders and the peak-time queue

At 8 pm on a match day, orders arrive faster than pickers can pick. The Zepto interviewer added "prioritise orders for fast delivery at peak" (post). Treat each store as a little scheduling problem:

  • Priority = slack. slack = promised_by - now - (pick_estimate + ride_eta). The order with the least slack goes first (earliest-deadline-first). A plain FIFO lets a far-away order miss its promise while a nearby one waits needlessly.
  • Admission control at the front door. When a store's queue makes the ETA exceed, say, 20 minutes, serviceability raises the shown ETA, adds a surge fee, or shrinks the service radius so far addresses fall to a neighbour store or "not serviceable now". Better than accepting orders you will deliver late.
  • Picker assignment: a store-local scheduler (one instance per store, chosen by consistent hashing on store_id) pulls from the priority queue when a picker becomes free; pick lists are sorted by bin walking order. Batch picking (one picker, two small orders on the same aisle) helps at peak.
  • Rider assignment: assign when the order is about 1 minute from packed, not at order time, so riders are not idle at the counter. Choose by arrival time back at the store and current load; batch two orders only if both still meet their promises. Rider GPS lives in Redis GEO (latest point only).
  • Payments at peak: COD orders skip the payment wait; prepaid orders enter the pick queue only after payment success, but the reservation is already made, so a slow PSP never sells their stock to someone else. Each method is a strategy behind one PaymentProcessor interface (the LLD part the Zepto round also asked for).

5. Variant: medicine delivery with prescriptions

An October 2026 startup SDE-1 round asked for this, and the report says the focus was the state machine, stock concurrency and edge cases rather than boxes (post).

  • Upload: the app gets a presigned URL and uploads the image directly to object storage (encrypted; it is health data with access logs and a retention policy).
  • Extract: an OCR + LLM job (async, on a queue) returns medicine names, strengths and quantities with a confidence score; names are matched to the drug catalogue by brand and salt (generic) name.
  • Verify: a licensed pharmacist reviews the extraction before checkout. The AI speeds the work up; it does not make the medical decision. Low confidence or a controlled drug always goes to a human.
  • Find pharmacies: Elasticsearch geo_distance over partner pharmacies within a radius, filtered by those reporting stock for all lines (or allow a split order across two), ranked by distance and acceptance rate.
  • Stock concurrency: partner stock feeds are stale, so the order is offered to the best pharmacy with an accept timeout (for example 2 minutes); on reject or timeout, offer it to the next. Payment is authorised up front and captured only on acceptance, or refunded.
  • Scale to many cities: shard by city; each city's pharmacies and orders live together.

Quick quiz

Why must almost every piece of state be keyed by store_id?

  1. Because Elasticsearch requires one index per store
  2. Stock, price, ETA and capacity all differ by store
  3. Because payments are settled per dark store
Everything the user sees depends on the serving store, and a cart touches only one store, so store-sharding keeps each checkout on one shard. Elasticsearch does not require per-store indexes (design B uses one catalogue index). Payment settlement is a finance detail, not why the hot path is sharded.

The WMS sends "stock = 17" and the order system sends "stock = 15" for the same SKU. What is the fix?

  1. Take whichever message arrives last
  2. Take the larger value to avoid lost sales
  3. Let the app read both and show the average
  4. Send deltas with ids to a single writer
Absolute values from two writers overwrite each other; deltas with unique ids, applied by one service with a ledger, compose and are idempotent. Last-writer-wins silently drops a change. Taking the larger value sells phantom stock. Averaging two wrong numbers is still wrong.

What does ATP (available to promise) subtract from on hand?

  1. Reserved units and a safety buffer
  2. Units in transit from the warehouse
  3. Units sold yesterday at that store
ATP = on hand - reserved - safety buffer: what can still be promised to a new order. In-transit stock is not on the shelf, so it is not part of on hand at all. Yesterday's sales are forecasting input, not a subtraction.

Why reserve at checkout rather than at add-to-cart, usually?

  1. Checkout reservations need no database transaction
  2. Add-to-cart reservations are impossible in SQL
  3. Abandoned carts would lock stock for nobody
  4. Customers prefer not to see stock counts
Most carts are abandoned; reserving at add-to-cart hides stock from real buyers. Checkout reservations still need a transaction. Reserving at add-to-cart is technically easy, just a bad trade-off. Customer preferences about counts are unrelated to when you reserve.

At peak, which picking order best protects delivery promises?

  1. First come, first served (FIFO)
  2. Least slack first (earliest deadline)
  3. Largest orders first, to clear volume
Ordering by slack (promise minus now minus pick and ride time) serves the orders closest to missing their promise first. FIFO ignores distance, so far orders miss. Largest-first starves small, quick orders and makes the average worse.

Why combine event-driven invalidation with a short TTL for the stock cache?

  1. TTLs make invalidation events unnecessary
  2. Events are too slow to be useful
  3. The TTL makes the cache strongly consistent
  4. A lost event would otherwise stay wrong forever
Events give freshness; the TTL bounds the damage when an event is lost. A TTL alone is either stale or expensive. Events usually arrive within a second. Neither makes a cache strongly consistent; the reservation step is the real check.

In medicine delivery, partner pharmacy stock feeds are stale. How do you avoid selling what the pharmacy lacks?

  1. Offer the order and wait for an accept, with a timeout
  2. Trust the feed and refund later if needed
  3. Hold a database lock on the pharmacy's stock
Acceptance by the pharmacy is the real reservation; on reject or timeout, offer to the next pharmacy, and capture payment only on acceptance. Trusting the feed creates refunds and angry users. You cannot lock a partner's stock in your database.

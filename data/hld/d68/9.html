Interview: driving the 45 minutes

What interviewers actually asked (real reports):

  • Swiggy SDE-3, Nov 2025: design an Instamart-like service focused on inventory search and discovery plus order management; a standard public breakdown still got negative feedback (post).
  • Flipkart SDE-3, Oct 2025: design a quick-commerce system (Flipkart Minutes) in a 90-minute round; the same loop's HM round asked about caching, CDNs and networks (post).
  • Swiggy SDE-2, Feb 2025: a warehouse management system for Instamart: APIs, schemas, a high-level diagram, and keeping inventory consistent across systems (post).
  • Zepto SDE-2 backend, Sep 2024: order and delivery management; set your own requirements, then the interviewer added peak-time prioritisation and several payment methods; class diagram, APIs, DB design, patterns, SQL vs NoSQL per component (post).
  • Startup SDE-1, Oct 2026: a medicine delivery platform: prescription upload, AI extraction, cart, nearby pharmacies with stock, pay, route to pharmacy; state machine, stock concurrency, edge cases, multi-city (post).
  1. 0-5 min: requirements. Set them yourself (the Zepto style): serviceability, store-scoped search, reserve, pay, pick, ride, track. NFRs with numbers: 10 minutes, search p99 200 ms, no selling phantom stock.
  2. 5-10 min: numbers. Small total throughput, millions of store x SKU rows, thousands of store-scoped searches per second. Conclusion: shard by store, the problem is accuracy and latency.
  3. 10-15 min: data + APIs. store_inventory with on_hand/reserved/buffer, the movement ledger, orders. SQL vs NoSQL table.
  4. 15-25 min: diagram. The stepper flow.
  5. 25-40 min: deep dives. Pick two: inventory consistency across systems (single writer + ledger) and reservation; then peak prioritisation if asked.
  6. 40-45 min: trade-offs in your own voice and metrics: order-to-door p50/p90, promise-met rate, in-stock accuracy (orders with a short pick), reservation failures at checkout, search staleness (consumer lag), picker utilisation.

An SDE-1 answer sounds like

"Find the nearest dark store from the location, search with Elasticsearch but only show items in that store's stock from a Redis cache, reserve stock at checkout with an atomic update, pay, then send the order to the store's picker app and assign a nearby rider. Track with WebSockets."

An SDE-2/SDE-3 answer adds

"Everything shards by store. The inventory service is the single writer, fed by movements with ids from the WMS, pickers and orders, with a ledger and outbox; caches use events plus a short TTL. A safety buffer hides drift-prone units. Picking is earliest-deadline-first by slack, riders are assigned at predicted pack time, and at peak I raise ETAs or shrink the radius instead of breaking promises. Here are the costs I accept for each."

Common mistakes: designing national search without store scoping; letting the WMS and the app each write absolute stock numbers; reserving at add-to-cart without discussing abandoned carts; FIFO picking at peak; assigning riders at order time and letting them idle; no answer for "the shelf is empty but the system says 3"; reciting the public template with no numbers or personal trade-offs.

How do you make search show only what is in stock at the user's dark store?

Resolve the store first (lat/lon to H3 cell to store_id, carried in the session). Search Elasticsearch over the product catalogue for the top ~200 candidates, then filter by the store's in-stock set in Redis (one set or bitmap per store, updated from stock.changed events with a short TTL as a safety net), and return the top 20. Category pages per store are precomputed lists rebuilt on stock events. The alternative, one ES document per store x SKU, filters exactly but turns every stock flip into an index write and duplicates product text 1,000 times.

How to answer

Compare two designs with their costs, pick one with a number ("a store carries ~6K SKUs, so 200 candidates contain 20 in stock almost always").

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the user is on the border of two stores? (Serviceability picks one by ETA; you can fall back to the neighbour for out-of-stock items only if the product allows split orders.)
  • How stale can search be? (Seconds; the reservation is the real check.)
How do you keep inventory consistent across the warehouse system, the store and the app?

Make one service the single writer of each store's stock. The WMS, picker scans and order reservations send movements (deltas with unique ids: GRN +48, PICK -1, DAMAGE -2), never absolute values. Each movement is applied in one transaction with the counter change and an outbox row, so it is idempotent (unique id) and published reliably on a per-store Kafka partition. Caches and search consume those events with versions and a short TTL. The physical shelf is the final truth: cycle counts and "bin empty" reports post COUNT_ADJUST movements, and a safety buffer absorbs known drift.

How to answer

Start with the failure ("two systems each set stock to their own number"), then single writer + deltas + ledger + outbox, then reconciliation with the real shelf.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the WMS is a vendor product you cannot change? (Wrap it: an adapter turns its updates into movements, and periodic snapshots are reconciled as COUNT_ADJUST.)
  • How do you measure accuracy? (Short-pick rate and count variance per store.)
Pessimistic or optimistic locking for reserving stock?

For a cart, a conditional UPDATE per line (reserved = reserved + q WHERE on_hand - reserved - buffer >= q) in one transaction, lines in sku order. It is optimistic in spirit (no read-then-lock round trip, no retry loop) and pessimistic in effect (the row lock lasts one statement). Pure optimistic versioning suits slow SKUs but retries badly on hot ones (milk at 7 am). SELECT FOR UPDATE is correct but holds locks longer and risks deadlocks; use it only when the check needs logic SQL cannot express. Never hold a lock across the payment.

How to answer

Tie the choice to contention level, give the hot-SKU example, and mention lock ordering for multi-line carts.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if one line is short? (Roll back the transaction, return available quantities, let the user adjust.)
At peak, how do you prioritise orders so they still arrive fast?

Each store runs a priority queue ordered by slack: promised time minus now minus estimated pick and ride time. Least slack goes first (earliest-deadline-first), so a far-away order is not starved by FIFO. Batch small orders on the same aisle for one picker and two orders on the same route for one rider when both still meet their promise. And control admission: when the queue pushes ETAs past a threshold, serviceability shows a higher ETA, adds a surge fee or shrinks the radius, so the store does not accept orders it will deliver late.

How to answer

Name the policy (EDF by slack), the inputs (pick estimate, rider ETA), and the admission-control lever. Mention priority customers only if the interviewer wants a business rule.

Likely follow-ups
  • Does a premium membership jump the queue? (Only by reducing its slack target; never by breaking another order's promise silently.)
How do you support several payment methods (card, UPI, wallet, cash)?

One PaymentProcessor interface with initiate, handleCallback, refund, and a strategy per method chosen by a factory. Prepaid methods return a payment action (redirect or UPI intent) and confirm by signed, idempotent webhook; the stock reservation has a timer so a slow PSP does not sell the stock. Wallet debits are a ledger entry in our own system. COD confirms immediately with risk limits (new user, high value) and is settled when the rider collects cash. Split tender (wallet + UPI) is a list of payment legs with a compensating refund if one fails.

How to answer

This round mixed HLD with a class diagram: show the interface, then the state each method needs.

Likely follow-ups
  • Refund for a missing item on a COD order? (Credit to wallet, since there is no card to refund.)
SQL or NoSQL for each component?

Inventory and reservations: SQL, sharded by store, for conditional multi-row transactions and constraints. Orders and payments: SQL, or a key-value store with conditional writes if write volume demands it (Zepto has written about using DynamoDB for order data). Catalogue: a document store for flexible attributes. Search: Elasticsearch. Stock cache and rider locations: Redis. Events: Kafka. Analytics: a lakehouse. Give one reason per row: the access pattern and the consistency need.

How to answer

Draw the table. The interviewer is checking that you choose per access pattern, not one database for everything.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not keep inventory in Redis only? (Durability and constraints; Redis is the cache, not the record.)
The picker finds an empty bin, but the system said 3 in stock. What happens, and how do you stop it recurring?

Now: the picker marks "short"; inventory posts a COUNT_ADJUST setting on_hand to what is actually there; the customer is offered a substitute or an automatic partial refund before the rider leaves. Later: count drift is a metric (short-pick rate per SKU and store); high-drift SKUs get a safety buffer and more frequent cycle counts; misplaced stock (put in the wrong bin) is found by bin-level counts. Root causes are usually unrecorded damage, theft, mis-shelving or a GRN counted wrong.

How to answer

Immediate customer handling, then the data fix, then the process fix. Interviewers like that the "truth" is the shelf, not the database.

Likely follow-ups
  • Should the safety buffer be global? (No; per SKU, tuned by its miss rate.)
Medicine delivery: prescription upload, AI extracts medicines, then find pharmacies with stock. Walk through the state machine and the edge cases.

States: RX_UPLOADED, EXTRACTED, VERIFIED (by a pharmacist), CART_CONFIRMED, PAYMENT_AUTHORISED, OFFERED_TO_PHARMACY, ACCEPTED, PACKED, OUT_FOR_DELIVERY, DELIVERED; plus REJECTED, REUPLOAD_NEEDED, CANCELLED, REFUNDED. Edge cases: unreadable image (ask to re-upload), low AI confidence or a controlled drug (always human review), a medicine nobody nearby has (offer generic by salt, or split across two pharmacies), the pharmacy rejects or times out (offer to the next; auth is captured only on acceptance), expired prescription (reject). Stock concurrency: partner stock is stale, so treat it as a hint and make acceptance the real reservation.

How to answer

The report says the interviewer wanted states and edge cases, not boxes. Draw the state machine first, then the failure arrows.

Likely follow-ups
  • Where does the prescription image live and who can see it? (Encrypted object storage, presigned short-lived URLs, access logs, retention policy.)
  • How do you find pharmacies? (Elasticsearch geo_distance filtered by reported stock, ranked by distance and acceptance rate.)
Where do caching and CDNs help in quick commerce, and where are they dangerous?

Help: product images and static assets on a CDN; catalogue details and per-store category pages in Redis; serviceability (cell to store) in memory, since it changes only when a store changes its area. Dangerous: anything that decides money or stock. The reservation always goes to the inventory database; prices shown from cache are re-checked at checkout. Per-store pages must use the store in the cache key, or one store's stock leaks into another's page.

How to answer

Two lists: safe to cache (with TTL) and never decided from cache.

Likely follow-ups
  • CDN for API responses? (Only for anonymous, store-keyed category pages, with a short TTL.)

Scenario: refunds for missing items doubled on Saturday evening

Customer care reports twice the usual "item missing, refunded" tickets in ten stores during a cricket match.

Weak answer: "Pickers were rushed; hire more pickers."

Strong answer: check whether those orders were picked short (stock drift) or packed wrong. If short: was a GRN in those stores applied twice or not at all, or did stock.changed consumer lag let search sell items already gone? Compare ledger vs counts for the affected SKUs. Fix: idempotent GRN movements, raise the safety buffer on those SKUs during events, alert on short-pick rate per store in real time.

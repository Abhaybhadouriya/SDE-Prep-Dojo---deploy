Back-of-envelope estimation

These are round assumptions for an India-scale movie platform, not BookMyShow's real numbers. Then we add one hot concert. Write the assumptions on the board.

A normal day

  1. Inventory. Assume 10,000 screens x 5 shows a day x 200 seats = 10 million seat-shows per day. Shows open about 7 days ahead, so about 70 million show_seats rows are live. At about 60 bytes per row that is about 4 GB. Small: it fits on one database server, with room to spare.
  2. Bookings. Assume 30% occupancy sold online: 3 million seats per day. At 2.5 seats per booking: about 1.2 million bookings per day. Divide by 100,000 seconds: about 12 bookings per second on average. Evenings and weekends are peaky; take 10x: about 120/s.
  3. Browsing. Assume 20 page views (lists, show pages, seat maps) per booking: 24 million reads per day = about 240/s average, a few thousand per second at peak. Mostly cacheable.
  4. Hold writes. Many people hold and give up. Assume 3 holds per booking: about 40/s average, a few hundred at peak. Any SQL database handles this.

A hot on-sale

  1. Supply is tiny. A stadium concert has, say, 50,000 seats.
  2. Demand is huge. Assume 2 million fans arrive in the first 10 minutes, and each browser refreshes or retries every 2 seconds while waiting. 2,000,000 / 2 s = 1 million requests per second aimed at one event.
  3. Conflicts. If 100,000 people are let into the seat map at once and all try the front rows, thousands of hold attempts hit the same few hundred rows. That is the real contention problem.
  4. Reality check. In its public statement about the 2022 Taylor Swift Eras Tour presale, Ticketmaster said its systems saw about 3.5 billion total requests, four times its previous peak, much of it from bots and from fans without presale codes. Numbers like this are why the waiting room exists.

What the numbers tell you: normal traffic is boring, so a single well-indexed Postgres primary per region (with replicas for reads) is fine. The design is driven by the ratio in a hot sale: 1 million requests per second for 50,000 seats. You cannot scale the database to that. You must shape the demand before it reaches the database.

From your world

This is a classic oversubscribed link. Forty 10G ports feeding one 10G uplink will drop packets no matter how fast the switch is. You do not fix it with a bigger buffer; you fix it with admission control and policing at the edge. The waiting room is a token-bucket policer for humans. Where it breaks: dropped packets get retransmitted by TCP silently; dropped fans get angry, so the "drop" must be a polite, ordered queue with a position number.

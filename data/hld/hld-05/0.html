The story: sending a letter to a big company

Explain like I'm 5

You want to send a letter to "Toy Company". You do not know where they live, so you look in the phone book. It gives you an address. That is DNS.

Toy Company is famous, so it has little shops in every town that keep copies of its most popular toys. If you only want a popular toy, the shop near your house gives it to you right away. That is the CDN.

If you need something special, your letter goes to the big office. At the door sits a receptionist. She opens the letter, checks you are allowed in, and decides which department should read it: toys, money, or complaints. That is the API gateway (a kind of reverse proxy).

The toy department has ten workers who all do the same job. A helper hands each new letter to whoever is least busy. That is the load balancer.

Every system design interview answer starts with a box on the left called "Client" and a box called "Load Balancer" or "API Gateway". Most candidates draw those boxes and move on. You already know more about the wires underneath than most interviewers. This page connects your packet-level knowledge to the boxes system designers draw, so you can explain why each box is there and what it costs.

From your world

Everything on this page is stuff you have seen in routers and kernels, just placed at a different layer. DNS is a distributed, cached lookup table (think ARP cache with TTLs, but global). Anycast is just BGP announcing the same prefix from many places. An L4 load balancer is NAT plus a connection table (conntrack). An L7 load balancer is a user-space proxy that terminates TCP and opens a new connection. HTTP/2 head-of-line blocking is the same problem as one shared FIFO ring for many flows.

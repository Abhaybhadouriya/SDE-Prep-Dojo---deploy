The story

Explain like I'm 5

The whole school writes their names in one sign-up book for the picnic. Only one child can write in the book at a time, so a long line forms. Making photocopies does not help: you cannot write in a copy, because then the copies disagree.

So the teacher makes several books: A-F, G-M, N-Z. Now three children write at once. But if every child is called "Aarav", the A-F book gets the whole line again. And when the bell rings and everyone runs in together, the teacher gives out slips of paper first and copies them into the books later, a whole stack at a time.

That is the pattern. Splitting the book is sharding (also called partitioning): each machine owns part of the data and takes the writes for that part. The rule that decides which book a name goes in is the shard key. A book that gets far more than its share is a hot partition. The slips are a queue or buffer that absorbs a burst, and copying a stack at once is batching.

Scaling reads (see Scaling Reads) is mostly about making copies. Scaling writes is harder because every write must land in exactly one authoritative place, in the right order, without being lost. You can only make each write cheaper, spread writes over more owners, or delay and combine them.

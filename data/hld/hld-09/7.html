Leases, distributed locks and fencing tokens

A lease is a lock with a timer: "you own this for 10 seconds unless you renew it." If the owner crashes, the lease simply expires and someone else can take over, so nobody waits forever. Leases are how ZooKeeper sessions, etcd leases and Chubby's sessions work: clients send keep-alive messages to renew. The owner must use a monotonic clock to measure its remaining time - a wall clock can be stepped by NTP.

Martin Kleppmann's famous essay splits locks into two kinds. An efficiency lock stops two workers doing the same job twice; if it fails now and then, you pay for a duplicate email. A correctness lock stops two writers corrupting data; if it fails once, you have a real bug. Most interview problems (seat booking, wallet debit, a single cron runner) need the second kind.

  1. Client 1 gets the lock. The lock service grants a lease and a fencing token - a number that grows every time the lock is granted. Client 1 gets 33.
  2. Client 1 freezes. A stop-the-world GC, a VM migration or a page fault pauses it longer than the lease. It does not know time has passed. Checking "is my lease still valid?" right before writing does not help, because the pause can land just after the check.
  3. Client 2 gets the lock. The lease expired, so the service grants it to client 2 with token 34.
  4. Client 2 writes. Storage records 34 as the highest token it has seen.
  5. Zombie write blocked. Client 1 wakes up and writes with token 33. Storage sees 33 < 34 and rejects it. Safety now lives in the storage check, not in timing.

Say this: "A lock alone cannot be safe because the holder can pause past its lease. I'd use a consensus-backed lock (etcd or ZooKeeper) that hands out a monotonically increasing fencing token, and make the storage reject writes with an older token. If the protected data lives in one database, I'd skip the distributed lock and use a conditional write on a version column."

Google's Chubby is the classic lock service: a 5-replica Paxos cell offering coarse-grained, advisory locks through a file-system-like API, with client caching and server-driven invalidation. Google used it to elect GFS and Bigtable primaries, and it ended up serving mostly as a name service. Building one is design d55 Distributed Lock Service; the wider family of techniques is in Pattern: Dealing with Contention.

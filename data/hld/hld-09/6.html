Heartbeats, gossip and failure detection

In a network you can never be sure a node is dead. A missing reply could be a crash, a long GC pause (garbage collection: a Java or Go runtime freezing all threads for milliseconds to seconds to reclaim memory, much like a long softirq stall that starves your process), a full queue or a lost packet. So every failure detector is really a suspicion detector with a timeout. Pick the timeout too short and you fail over healthy nodes (flapping); too long and users wait on a dead node.

  • Heartbeats. Each node sends "I am alive" every interval to a leader or monitor. Simple, but central (the monitor itself must be replicated) and it costs O(N) messages per interval at the monitor.
  • Gossip. Every interval, each node picks a random peer and swaps what it knows (membership list, heartbeat counters, versions). Dynamo and Cassandra gossip once per second. News spreads like a rumour: the number of informed nodes roughly doubles each round, so it reaches everyone in about log2(N) rounds with no central server.
  • Phi accrual detector. Instead of a yes/no timeout, Cassandra computes a suspicion level (phi) from the recent distribution of heartbeat arrival times. A node on a jittery link gets more slack automatically.

From your world

Hello/dead timers in OSPF, keepalive/hold timers in BGP (hold = 3 x keepalive by default) and BFD's detect multiplier are all heartbeat failure detectors with the same trade-off: a lower multiplier means faster convergence and more false positives. Gossip is close to how link-state flooding spreads an LSA, except gossip is randomised and periodic instead of reliable flooding.

Split brain

Split brain is when a network partition makes two nodes both believe they are the leader, and both accept writes. You prevent it with two ideas used together:

  1. Majority quorum. Only the side with more than half the voters can elect or keep a leader. The minority side stops accepting writes.
  2. Fencing with an epoch. Every leadership change bumps a number (Raft term, Kafka controller epoch, ZooKeeper zxid: its 64-bit transaction id, made of the leader epoch plus a counter). Storage and followers reject any request carrying an older number, so a zombie ex-leader cannot do damage even if it has not noticed it was replaced.

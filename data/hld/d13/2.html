Back-of-envelope estimation

The number most often used in interviews is 500 hours of video uploaded per minute. It comes from older YouTube press material, so call it "the commonly quoted figure" and use it as an assumption, not a fact you are sure about today. Everything below is a round-number assumption. Write each assumption on the board. The method matters more than the digits. (Refresher: Back-of-Envelope Estimation.)

  1. Video per day. 500 hours/min x 60 min x 24 h = 720,000 hours of video per day. That is about 43 million minutes.
  2. Uploads per day and per second. Assume the average video is 10 minutes. 43M / 10 = about 4.3 million uploads per day. Divide by 86,400 seconds (round to 100,000): about 43-50 uploads start every second. Peak at 3x: about 150/s. That is small. The upload count is never the problem; the bytes are.
  3. Raw bytes. Assume a 1080p source averages 8 Mbit/s. 8 Mbit/s = 1 MB/s = 60 MB per minute = 3.6 GB per hour. 720,000 h x 3.6 GB = about 2.6 PB of originals per day.
  4. Upload bandwidth. 2.6 PB / 86,400 s = about 30 GB/s, which is about 240 Gbit/s of ingress on average, maybe 700 Gbit/s at peak. Spread across many regions and edge entry points, this is fine, but it tells you the bytes must not flow through our API servers.
  5. Chunk requests. A 10-minute video is about 600 MB. With 16 MB parts that is about 38 parts. 4.3M x 38 = about 160M part uploads per day = about 1,900 part PUTs per second. Blob storage handles this easily.
  6. Transcoded copies. Add up a typical ladder: 240p (0.4 Mbit/s) + 360p (0.8) + 480p (1.4) + 720p (2.8) + 1080p (5) = about 10.4 Mbit/s. That is about 1.3x the source for one codec. With a second codec for popular videos, call all renditions about 2x the source. Original + renditions = about 3x, so about 8 PB per day before replication.
  7. Durable storage. With 3 full copies: about 24 PB per day. With erasure coding (about 1.5x overhead) for older data: about 12 PB per day. Per year: several exabytes. Say it calmly; then say "this is why we tier storage and do not encode every video in every codec".
  8. Encoding compute. Assume, only for estimation, that turning 1 minute of video into the full ladder costs 10 CPU-minutes (the real number depends heavily on codec and preset; AV1 is much slower than H.264). 30,000 minutes of video arrive per minute, so 30,000 x 10 = 300,000 CPU cores busy all the time. This is the number that justifies autoscaling, spot capacity and hardware encoders.
  9. Metadata. 4.3M new rows per day x about 2 KB = under 10 GB per day. Tiny. The metadata DB is about read QPS (every watch page reads it), not size.

Say what each number changes. 240 Gbit/s ingress means "direct-to-storage uploads, never through app servers". 300k cores means "a queue plus autoscaled stateless workers, and cheap spot machines". 10 GB/day of metadata means "a normal sharded SQL database is enough". Numbers that change nothing are wasted minutes.

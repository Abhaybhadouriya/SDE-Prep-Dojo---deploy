  2. 5-10 min: Estimate search QPS, supplier fan-out, look-to-book, booking rate. Conclude: cache-first search, correctness-first booking.
  3. 10-17 min: APIs (async search, re-price with price token, idempotent booking) and data model (booking + segments + payments).

Common mistakes: locking seats in your own Redis and calling it solved; holding seats during search; one synchronous call to all suppliers; caching per user; charging before ticketing; no idempotency key; trusting a client-sent price; graph search per request instead of precomputed connections; forgetting that a timed-out ticketing call may have succeeded.

Other platforms sell the same seats. How do you prevent double booking?

We cannot, by ourselves: we do not own the inventory. The airline's reservation system (or the GDS acting for it) is the single owner of seat inventory and serialises all sellers. Our booking flow asks it for a time-limited hold (a PNR with a ticketing time limit) on the exact seats; it either grants the hold or says unavailable. We record the hold and its expiry, run our payment window shorter than that expiry, and ticket before it ends. Any lock in our own database only prevents two of our users from racing, which is still worth doing to save supplier calls, but it is not the guarantee.

How to answer

Lead with "single owner of the truth", then the lease idea. That was the accepted answer in the CRED round.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the hold call times out? (Retrieve the PNR by our reference before retrying.)
  • What if we are the airline? (Conditional update on the seat row with hold expiry.)
Every seat has its own price. How do seat locking and pricing work together under concurrency?

The seat map comes from the supplier with availability and a fee per seat. When the user picks seats, the pricing service builds an offer (fare + seat fees + taxes + markup) and signs it into a price token. Booking sends the token and the seats; the orchestrator asks the supplier to hold exactly those seats at that price. Two users picking 12C at the same time: the supplier grants one hold; the other gets SEAT_UNAVAILABLE and a refreshed seat map. If we own inventory, the hold is a conditional update on the (flight, seat) row (WHERE status='FREE' OR hold_expires_at < now()), so exactly one wins.

How to answer

Separate fare-bucket inventory from seat assignment, then show the single winner.

Likely follow-ups
  • Price of the seat changes after selection? (The token fixes it until expiry; after expiry, re-price.)
Support connecting flights with at most one stop. How do you keep search fast?

Connections depend on schedules, not prices, and schedules change slowly. So precompute, nightly and on schedule-change events, all valid one-stop pairs per (origin, destination, day): second leg departs after the first arrives plus the airport's minimum connection time and within a maximum layover. At search time, look up nonstop and precomputed one-stop candidates, then fetch prices for the legs from the cache or suppliers in parallel, and stream results. Round trips are two one-way searches combined, plus native round-trip fares where suppliers offer them.

How to answer

Name the split: schedule graph (precompute) vs prices (cache with short TTL).

Likely follow-ups
  • Two stops? (Precompute is still possible but grows fast; cap candidates by total duration.)
A round trip uses two different airlines. How do you keep the booking consistent?

A saga, because airlines do not take part in our transactions. Hold both segments, authorise payment, ticket segment 1, ticket segment 2, capture. Each step is persisted before the next; each has a compensation: cancel holds, void a ticket within the void window, void the authorisation, or refund. Holding first and ticketing last keeps most failures cheap to undo. A ticketing timeout is "unknown" until we retrieve the PNR. A daily reconciliation with supplier and payment records catches anything left.

How to answer

Draw the step table with compensations. Interviewers want to see the order of steps justified.

Likely follow-ups
  • Void window passed? (Refund per fare rules; this is why ticketing both segments close together matters.)
Here is an existing flight search and booking design. Review it: what would you improve?

Go through it in a fixed order. Correctness: who owns seat locks, is booking idempotent, is the price trusted from the client, is payment captured before ticketing. Reliability: does search wait for every supplier, are there timeouts, circuit breakers and bulkheads per supplier, can a crashed booking resume. Scalability and cost: cache strategy and TTLs, supplier rate limits, look-to-book. Contracts: versioned internal supplier contract, error mapping, pagination and streaming for search. Then say how you would validate each NFR: SLOs, load tests replaying real traffic, chaos with a fake supplier, canaries.

How to answer

Rank by impact, propose minimal fixes, and give a test for each. Reviewers lose points by redesigning from zero.

Likely follow-ups
  • What would you fix first with one sprint? (Idempotency and charge-after-ticket: they cost money today.)
How do you integrate 20-200 suppliers without one slow or broken supplier hurting everyone?

One adapter per supplier behind a versioned internal contract. The aggregator calls them in parallel with a per-supplier deadline, connection pool, rate limiter and circuit breaker, streams answers as they come, and stops waiting at a global deadline of about 2 s. Open breakers fall back to cached quotes. Results are normalised and deduplicated by flight and fare family, keeping alternates for booking fallback. Per-supplier dashboards (latency, errors, timeouts, quote accuracy, look-to-book) decide which suppliers to call for which routes.

How to answer

Name scatter-gather plus three isolation tools (deadline, bulkhead, breaker). This is also the core of a Skyscanner metasearch answer.

Likely follow-ups
  • Skyscanner sends users to partners to book: what changes? (No booking saga; deep links with tracking; quote freshness and partner load become the main problems.)
Where does CAP show up, and how do you handle network failures?

Search picks availability: during a partition or supplier outage, serve cached results marked as unconfirmed. Booking picks consistency: if we cannot reach the supplier or our database primary, fail the step and tell the user, never guess. Network failures: timeouts everywhere, retries only for idempotent calls with backoff, retrieve-before-retry for side effects (holds, tickets, payments), breakers, and a booking state machine that can resume. Observability ties it together: traces across search fan-out and booking steps, and alerts on stuck saga states.

How to answer

Give CAP per flow, not for the whole system.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you test these failures? (Fake supplier with injected latency, errors, and "succeeded but timed out" responses.)
Premium members get special prices. How does that fit the cache, and how is the price guaranteed at booking?

The cache stores neutral supplier prices only. The pricing service applies per-request rules (member tier, route, airline, coupons) from an in-memory, versioned rules engine, so the cache is shared by everyone. The price shown is signed into a price token with the rule version and expiry; the booking service accepts that token, re-checks the supplier's live price, and if it moved, shows the new total for confirmation. Every booking stores the rule version for audit.

How to answer

"Cache neutral, personalise late, sign what you show."

Likely follow-ups
  • Load balancing for supplier-heavy search pods? (Least outstanding requests, since call durations vary a lot.)
  • How do you shard bookings? (By booking_id, with a user_id index for "my trips".)
You are the airline: give entities, schema and APIs for the ticket management system.

Entities: Airport, Aircraft (seat layout), Flight (number, route), FlightInstance (date, times, status), FareClass inventory per instance (bucket, price, seats left), Seat per instance (status, price, hold), Booking (PNR), Passenger, Segment, Ticket, Payment. Hold = conditional update on Seat plus decrement of FareClass seats_left in one transaction; a sweeper or lazy check releases expired holds. APIs: search instances, get seat map, create hold, confirm with payment, cancel, issue ticket. Keep the booking state machine explicit.

How to answer

Flipkart's "design round" can be LLD-leaning: write the tables and the hold SQL, not only boxes.

Likely follow-ups
  • Overbooking? (Airlines sell slightly more than capacity in fare buckets on purpose; seat assignment is separate.)
How do you test this system and prove it meets its NFRs?

Write SLOs first: p95 time to first results under 2 s, booking success rate, zero charges without tickets. Then layers: unit and contract tests for every supplier adapter using recorded responses; integration tests in supplier sandboxes; a fake supplier with configurable latency, errors and partial success for chaos and saga tests; load tests that replay real search logs at 2x peak; synthetic bookings in production against a sandbox supplier; canary releases with automatic rollback on SLO burn; and reconciliation reports as the final proof that no money and ticket mismatch slipped through.

How to answer

Tie each test to a specific NFR. Agoda asked this in both rounds.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you test the "ticket succeeded but response lost" case? (Fake supplier that commits then drops the reply; assert the saga retrieves instead of re-ticketing.)

Scenario: sale day, searches up 10x, two suppliers throttling

A fare sale drives 12,000 searches/s. Two big suppliers return HTTP 429 and their breakers keep flapping.

Weak answer: "Scale the search pods to 10x."

Strong answer: more pods mean more supplier calls and more 429s. Instead: extend TTLs for the sale routes, pre-warm the cache, serve stale-while-revalidate, enforce a per-supplier token bucket below their limit, collapse identical concurrent searches into one supplier call (single-flight), and show "prices confirmed at checkout". Booking keeps its own reserved supplier quota so searches cannot starve it.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which metric tells you the cache is now too stale? (Quote accuracy at re-price.)

Back-of-envelope estimation

Assumptions for an Indian OTA (state them out loud): 5M daily users, 20M searches/day.

  1. Search QPS: 20M / 86,400 = about 230/s average. Travel has evening and sale peaks, say 5x: about 1,200 searches/s.
  2. Supplier fan-out without a cache: if each search asks 50 suppliers on average, 1,200 x 50 = 60,000 supplier calls/s. Suppliers will rate-limit or bill us heavily. A metasearch site that queries dozens or hundreds of partners per search pays this cost on every uncached search.
  3. With caching: at an 80% hit rate, live calls drop to 12,000/s. Assume most repeated quotes for the same flight are unchanged within minutes (an assumption to confirm with the interviewer), which is why a short cache works.
  4. Bookings: at 1,000:1 look-to-book, 20M searches give about 20,000 bookings/day, about 0.25/s on average, a few per second at peak. Booking is low volume and high value: optimise for correctness, not throughput.
  5. Cache size: key = (origin, destination, date, cabin, pax-type). Say 20,000 active city pairs x 330 days = 6.6M possible keys; results about 20 KB each = 130 GB if all were cached. Only hot keys are, so a Redis cluster of tens of GB suffices.
  6. Route graph: assume 3,000 flights/day in the market. Precomputed one-stop connections per city pair are a small table (thousands of rows per busy pair, recomputed when schedules change).
  7. Booking storage: 20,000 x 10 KB (passengers, segments, payments, events) = 200 MB/day. Trivial; any SQL database works.

The estimate tells you the shape: search is a read-heavy, cache-first, fan-out problem; booking is a tiny-volume, multi-party transaction. Two different designs in one product.

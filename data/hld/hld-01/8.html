Replication: copies of the data Must know

Replication means keeping the same data on several machines (replicas). It gives you three things: survival (lose a machine, keep the data), read scale (spread reads across copies), and locality (a copy near users in another region). The price is keeping the copies in agreement.

Leader-follower (the default)

One replica, the leader (or primary), accepts all writes. It records each change in a log (in PostgreSQL, the write-ahead log or WAL) and ships the log to followers (replicas, secondaries), which replay it. Reads can go to any replica.

Replication lag is how far a follower is behind. You can see it in PostgreSQL on the leader:

postgres=# SELECT client_addr, state, sync_state, replay_lag FROM pg_stat_replication;
 client_addr |   state   | sync_state |   replay_lag
-------------+-----------+------------+-----------------
 10.0.2.21   | streaming | async      | 00:00:00.001842
 10.0.2.22   | streaming | async      | 00:00:00.002310
(2 rows)
-- example output: two async followers about 2 ms behind

The classic lag bug: "I just saved my profile and it reverted!"

A user updates their name (goes to the leader), the page reloads and reads from a follower that has not replayed the change yet, and they see the old name. The fix is called read-your-writes consistency: for a short time after a user writes (say 10-30 seconds, or until a follower has caught up to that write's log position), send that user's reads to the leader. Other users can keep reading followers.

So how do two conflicting writes get merged? Multi-leader and leaderless stores need a merge rule, and there are three common ones. Last-write-wins (LWW): keep the write with the newest timestamp. It is simple and Cassandra does it by default, but the "losing" write silently disappears, and clocks on different machines disagree. Application merge with version vectors: a version vector is a small per-replica counter list that tells you whether one version is newer or whether the two were made at the same time. When they were made at the same time, the store keeps both and your code merges them; Amazon's Dynamo paper merges two shopping carts by taking the union of their items. CRDTs (conflict-free replicated data types): counters, sets and text structures designed so that any two copies can always be merged automatically, in any order. Collaborative editors use them.

From your world

Two routers in an HA pair that were split and both changed their running config have the same problem when the link heals: you need a rule for whose config wins (highest priority, newest commit, or a manual merge). Where this breaks: routers can usually afford to let one side win; a shopping cart or a document cannot silently drop a user's change. See quorums and read repair and the Collaborative Editor design for CRDTs in practice.

From your world

Leader-follower is an active/standby redundancy pair where the standby is kept warm by a state-sync stream. Replication lag is the standby being "not ready to take over" yet. Leader election (who becomes leader when it dies) is VRRP/HSRP with one big upgrade: it requires a majority vote, so a network split cannot create two leaders (split-brain). Async replicas converging is like routing protocol convergence: during it, some nodes have stale routes. Where this breaks: VRRP will happily elect two masters if the link between them dies; consensus protocols like Raft refuse to. Full details in Replication, Consensus & Coordination and Kafka's version in Kafka Replication & ISR.

Interview questions

What does "scale to a million users" actually mean in requests per second?

It depends on how active they are, so I'd state it: if a million daily users each make about 10 requests, that is 10 million a day. A day is about 10^5 seconds, so roughly 100 requests per second on average, maybe 300 at peak. That is small - one or two app servers and one database. "A million users" only gets hard when each user does a lot (a chat app, a game) or when traffic arrives all at once (a sale).

How to answer

Convert with 10^5 seconds per day, add a peak factor, and say what that means for the design.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if all 1M users open the app in the same minute and each makes 10 requests? (10M requests / 60 s = about 170,000/s - a completely different system: pre-scale, queue the work, or put a waiting room in front.)
Why do interviewers ask for estimates at all?

Because the numbers decide the architecture. QPS tells you whether one database is enough or you need caches, replicas and shards. Storage tells you whether data fits in memory, on one disk, or in object storage. The read:write ratio tells you what to optimise. Bandwidth tells you whether you need a CDN. A candidate who estimates and then says "so we need X" shows they design from evidence, not habit.

How to answer

Connect each kind of number to the decision it drives.

Likely follow-ups
  • Which estimate would you skip for this design, and why?
How would you design for 99.99% availability, and what does it allow?

99.99% allows about 52 minutes of downtime a year, or about 4 minutes a month. That means no single point of failure: several app servers across availability zones, a replicated database with automatic failover, health checks that remove bad nodes, and safe deploys (canaries and quick rollback), because most outages come from changes. I would also keep the critical path short - every dependency in series multiplies in its own downtime.

How to answer

Translate the nines into minutes first, then list redundancy per tier and deploy safety.

Likely follow-ups
  • Your service depends on three others at 99.9% each. What is your best possible availability? (About 99.7%.)
A request makes 5 sequential calls to other services in the same data centre and each does one SSD-backed DB read. Roughly what is the minimum latency?

Each network round trip inside the data centre is about 0.5 ms, and a database read that hits SSD is very roughly a millisecond once you include query work. So each call is about 1-2 ms, and five in sequence is about 5-10 ms before any real processing. If the calls are independent, making them in parallel cuts that to about one call's time. If p99 matters, remember that sequential calls also add up their tail latencies.

How to answer

Use the latency table, multiply by hops, then suggest parallelism and caching.

Likely follow-ups
  • One of the five services is in another region. Now? (Add 50-150 ms - move it or cache its data locally.)

Scenario: the interviewer pushes back on your numbers

You said 4,000 reads/s. The interviewer says, "Actually, assume this goes viral - 400,000 reads/s."

Weak answer: "Then we add more servers." (Nothing about what breaks first.)

Strong answer: "That's 100x. The app tier still scales horizontally. The cache becomes critical - a single hot link would be one hot key, so I'd add an in-process cache on each app server and put a CDN in front of redirects. The database should see almost none of this if the hit rate stays high, but I'd protect it with request coalescing so a cache miss storm doesn't reach it."

Likely follow-ups
  • What hit rate do you need so the database sees under 2,000 reads/s? (Above 99.5%.)

60-second answer: "How do you estimate quickly?"

"I start from daily active users and actions per user, and divide by about 10^5 seconds in a day - so a million a day is about 12 per second. I multiply by a peak factor, usually 2-3x, more for sales. For storage I do items per day times size times retention times three replicas. For bandwidth, QPS times payload, and I convert bits to bytes. I round hard, write units on everything, and after each number I say what it changes - fits in memory, needs sharding, needs a CDN."

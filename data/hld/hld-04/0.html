The story: how many sandwiches for the picnic?

Explain like I'm 5

Your school is having a picnic. You need to know how many sandwiches to make. You do not count every child one by one. You think: "About 30 kids in a class, 10 classes, so about 300 kids. Each eats 2 sandwiches. So about 600 sandwiches. Let's make 700 in case some are very hungry." That is an estimate. It does not need to be exact. It needs to be close enough to decide: one kitchen or two?

Back-of-envelope estimation means quick, rough maths with round numbers - the kind you could do on the back of an envelope. In a design interview it answers questions like: Will one database handle the writes? Do I need a cache? How much disk over five years? Is the network the bottleneck?

Being off by 2x is fine. Being off by 1,000x is not - that is the difference between "one server" and "a thousand servers". So the goal is the right order of magnitude (the right power of ten).

From your world

You already do this. A 100 Gbps port at minimum-size 64-byte frames (84 bytes on the wire with preamble and inter-frame gap) is about 148.8 million packets per second - you know that number. Web systems run at much lower rates (thousands to hundreds of thousands of requests per second), but each request does far more work: a database query takes about a millisecond, not nanoseconds. So "50,000 requests per second" sounds tiny to you, and it is not - it can mean dozens of servers. Where this breaks: a packet is processed in a fixed pipeline; a web request fans out to caches, databases and other services, so its cost depends on how many hops it makes.

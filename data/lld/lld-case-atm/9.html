Concurrency and thread safety

One ATM serves one person at a time, so many candidates say "no concurrency here". That is wrong in three places.

1. Inside one ATM: user actions versus the operator

The keypad thread and a maintenance thread (cash refill, remote shutdown) can both touch the ATM. Every public method of Atm is synchronized, so a state transition is never seen half done. The dispenser has its own lock so that reserve (plan and take the notes in one step) cannot interleave with load.

2. Many ATMs, one account

  1. Two ATMs (a joint account, or two cards on one account) ask at the same time. If the ATM reads the balance and then decides, both see 1,000.
  2. Both debit and the account goes negative. This is the classic check-then-act race, just spread across two machines.
  3. The fix: the ATM never decides from a balance it read earlier. It sends debit, and the bank checks funds and subtracts inside one critical section (a synchronized method here, a row lock or a conditional UPDATE ... WHERE balance >= amount in a database). The demo's 20-ATM test proves exactly 10 of 20 succeed.

3. Retries and the network

The debit request can reach the bank and the answer can be lost. The ATM must retry, and the retry must not debit twice. That is why debit carries a transaction id and the bank keeps a durable outcome for every id it has seen: who, how much, the response code, and whether it was later reversed. A retry of a known id gets the stored code back, declines (51, 61, 62) included, so "the second call returns the first answer" holds for every answer, not just approvals. A reversal does not delete that record: it marks it REVERSED (a tombstone), so a delayed retransmit of the original debit is declined instead of posting a second debit. A reversal that arrives before its debit also leaves a tombstone, so the late debit is declined. And if debit throws (timeout, network error), DispensingState releases the reserved notes, sends reverse(txnId) (safe because of the tombstone) and reports "bank unavailable", so the claim in the flow diagram holds on that path too. Response 14 maps to UNKNOWN_CARD and anything unexpected to BANK_ERROR, never to CARD_BLOCKED. Test 6 in the demo covers retry, reverse-then-retry and reversal-before-debit; LostAnswerBank covers the exception path.

From your world

A transaction id is a TCP sequence number. The receiver drops a retransmitted segment whose sequence number it has already acknowledged, so the sender may retransmit freely. Reserve-then-commit in the dispenser is ring_buffer_lock_reserve() followed by ring_buffer_unlock_commit() in the kernel tracing ring buffer: claim the space first, fill it, then publish, and discard the reservation if you change your mind.

Where this breaks: TCP sequence numbers wrap and are per-connection; transaction ids must be unique across every ATM forever (or for the bank's retention window). That is the bug in the warning box above.

Trade-offs to say out loud

  • Do not hold the ATM lock across slow I/O in production. Our ATM holds its lock during the bank call. With one customer per ATM that is fine, but an operator "shutdown" would wait for a slow network. A real design would hold the lock only for state transitions and let DISPENSING itself block other actions.
  • Reserve, or just check? Reserving notes before the debit costs a little complexity but means a refill or a second session cannot spend the same notes. With one customer per ATM, "check" would usually work; reserving makes it always correct.
  • Reverse immediately, or let the bank reconcile? We reverse at once. Real networks also send a reversal advice later and reconcile against the ATM's electronic journal, because the ATM may lose power before it can send anything.

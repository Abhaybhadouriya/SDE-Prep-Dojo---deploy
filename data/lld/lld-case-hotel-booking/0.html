The story: the receptionist's big calendar

Explain like I'm 5

A small hotel has a receptionist with a big paper calendar. Each row is a kind of room (double, suite), each column is one night. When a family asks "do you have a double room from Friday to Monday?", she looks at the Friday, Saturday and Sunday columns. If every one of those three columns still has a free double, she says yes and writes the family's name in pencil in all three columns.

"Pencil" matters. She tells the family: "I will keep it for 15 minutes while you pay." If they pay, she goes over the pencil in ink. If they walk away, she rubs the pencil out and the room is for sale again. If a family with an ink booking cancels, the hotel's rule decides how much money they get back.

Notice three things the receptionist does without thinking. She checks every night of the stay, not just the first one. She counts Monday morning (check-out day) as free, because the family leaves before the next guests arrive. And the price is not one number: Friday and Saturday nights cost more, and a long stay may get a discount. Those three habits are the heart of this problem, and most bugs in interview solutions come from forgetting one of them.

From your world

Room inventory by date is a per-slot resource counter, like a token bucket per time slot, or a bitmap of free pages where a request needs a run of consecutive pages. A booking for nights 18..20 is a multi-slot reservation: it must succeed on all slots or on none, exactly like grabbing several resources under one lock so you never hold half of them. The 15-minute hold is a lease with a timeout, the same idea as a DHCP lease or a TCP connection in SYN_RECV that the kernel drops when the handshake never completes.

Where this breaks: kernel resources are freed by the holder or by a timer you control. Here the holder is a human on a phone who may pay one second after the timer fires, so "expired" and "paid" can arrive in either order, and the design must decide who wins (we do: the state machine).

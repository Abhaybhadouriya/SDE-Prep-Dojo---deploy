The story: checking in and checking out

Explain like I'm 5

When you arrive at a hotel, the front desk does a few jobs before you get your room: they check your name, give you a key card and make sure the room is clean. That is a constructor: the work that makes a new thing ready to use.

When you leave, someone must take the key back, check the minibar and clean the room for the next guest. That is a destructor: the work that tidies up when a thing is finished.

Now imagine your friend wants "a copy of your room". You could hand them a copy of your key card (you now share one room, and if one of you messes it up, both of you see the mess). Or the hotel could give them their own room set up the same way. The first is a shallow copy. The second is a deep copy.

Every object you design in an LLD round goes through the same three moments: it is created, it is used (maybe copied or moved), and it is destroyed. Bugs love the edges: a half-built object, a resource that is never released, two objects that both think they own the same memory. This page teaches each edge, first in plain words, then in Java (our main interview language), C++ and Python.

From your world

You already write constructors and destructors in C, you just call them foo_init() / foo_alloc() and foo_destroy() / foo_free(). Think of alloc_netdev() followed by register_netdev(), and the mirror pair unregister_netdev() then free_netdev(). The classic kernel goto err_free; unwind ladder is a hand-written destructor chain: undo what you did, in reverse order. And kref_put() calling a release function when the count hits zero is the kernel's version of "destroy when the last owner lets go". OOP languages make the compiler run these for you, at the right time, every time.

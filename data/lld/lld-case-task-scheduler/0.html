The story: the kitchen with one order board

Explain like I'm 5

A busy kitchen has an order board and a few cooks. Some orders say "now", some say "at 7 o'clock", and some say "every 10 minutes, check the soup". A few orders must wait for others: you cannot put icing on a cake that is not baked yet. The head cook looks at the board, picks the most important order that is ready, and gives it to a cook who has free hands. If a cook burns the toast, the head cook writes "try again in 2 minutes" on the board.

Two rules keep the kitchen safe: a cook never gets two orders at once, and an order is never cooked twice by two cooks.

That is the whole problem. The board is a priority queue ordered by time, the cooks are a worker pool, "wait for the cake" is a dependency graph, and "every 10 minutes" is a periodic schedule. The interview question is how to write the head cook so that it never sleeps through an order, never wakes up for nothing, and never gives the same order to two cooks.

From your world

You have used this machine for years. A kernel timer (timer_list on the timer wheel, or an hrtimer in a red-black tree ordered by expiry) is our time queue. A delayed_work on a workqueue is exactly "run this function on a worker thread after N jiffies"; queue_delayed_work() is our submit(...delay(n)) and cancel_delayed_work_sync() is our cancel(). The CFS runqueue picking the next task by priority is our ready queue. systemd unit ordering with After= and Requires=, or make building targets, is our DAG. And the job is a C struct with a function pointer, struct work_struct { work_func_t func; }: that is the Command pattern before it had a name.

Where this breaks: the kernel can preempt a running task and runs timers in interrupt context. Our workers cannot stop a running job: cancellation is cooperative, and a slow job blocks its worker until it returns. Say this out loud in the interview.

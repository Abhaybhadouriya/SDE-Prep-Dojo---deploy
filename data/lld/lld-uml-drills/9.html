Notice three things that repeat in every row: an "offered thing vs taken thing" split, at least one strategy for the rule that will change, and exactly one check-then-act race that needs a lock. Once you look for these on purpose, new problems stop being new.

Use this in the room: after your diagram, say "the classic risks here are X (hidden split), Y (what will change) and Z (the race); here is how my design handles each." Interviewers hear that you have done this before, and they often skip their own follow-ups because you already answered them.

How much of the class diagram should you draw before coding in a 90-minute machine-coding round?

About 10-15 minutes of pre-coding: a list of entities with key fields, relationships, the orchestrator's method list and the interfaces for what will vary. Not a full UML diagram with every getter. The design then lives in the code's package layout (entities, services, strategies, repositories, driver). In a 45-60 minute OOD whiteboard round the diagram is the main deliverable, so spend more care on relationships and multiplicities there.

Scenario: the interviewer adds a requirement at minute 25

You are in drill 2's shape for real. Midway, the interviewer says: "premium members can book 5 classes a day and jump to the front of the waitlist."

Adds if (user.isPremium()) inside book and inside the waitlist code, and changes MAX_PER_DAY to a ternary.

"That is a membership policy. I will add a MembershipTier on User, a BookingPolicy interface with dailyLimit(user) and waitlistPriority(user), and make the waitlist a priority queue ordered by (priority, join time). book asks the policy; the existing standard-member behaviour becomes the default policy." Then shows the two-line change in book.

The story: the trip notebook

Explain like I'm 5

Four friends go on a trip. Asha pays for dinner. Ben pays for the taxi. Chitra buys movie tickets for only two people. Nobody wants to pay money back after every single thing, so they keep a notebook. Every time someone pays, they write who paid, how much, and who shared it. At the end of the trip they look at the notebook and say "Ben, you owe Asha 620. Asha, you owe Dev 230."

A clever friend then says: "Wait. Instead of five small payments, if Chitra pays Asha and Ben pays Dev, everyone is square in fewer steps." That notebook, plus the clever friend, is Splitwise.

In the interview you build that notebook as code. Three things matter. First, how one bill turns into shares (equally, by exact amounts, or by percentages). Second, a balance sheet that always says correctly who owes whom after many bills. Third, money must never be lost or invented: if 100 rupees is split three ways, the shares must add up to exactly 100.00, not 99.99.

From your world

The split rules are a classic ops table. In C you would write struct split_ops { int (*split)(long total, int n, const long *vals, long *out); }; and keep a static array split_ops[SPLIT_EQUAL], split_ops[SPLIT_EXACT], ... indexed by the enum, exactly like the kernel picks file_operations per device. In Java that table becomes a SplitStrategy interface and an EnumMap. The balance sheet is a small adjacency matrix (a sparse one: a map of maps), and "simplify debts" is a flow problem on that graph. Money in paise as a 64-bit integer is the same habit as keeping byte counters in u64 rather than floating point.

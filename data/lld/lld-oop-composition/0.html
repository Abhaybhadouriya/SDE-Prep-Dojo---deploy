The story: a carved toy and a Lego car

Explain like I'm 5

Grandpa carves you a wooden toy car. It is lovely, but its shape is fixed. When you want a car that can also fly, he has to carve a whole new toy, copying the old one and adding wings. Want a flying car with big wheels? Another new toy. Soon the shelf is full of almost-the-same cars.

Your friend builds a car from Lego. It has wheels, it has an engine block, it has a seat. Want it to fly? Pull the wheels off and click on propellers. Want big wheels? Swap them. One car, many shapes, and you can change it while you play.

The carved toy is inheritance: a new class is made by extending an old one, and its shape is fixed when you write the code. The Lego car is composition: an object is built from smaller objects it holds, and it asks them to do the work. "Favor object composition over class inheritance" is one of the two core rules in the Gang of Four's Design Patterns book (the other is "program to an interface"). It does not mean "never inherit". It means: reach for has-a first, and use is-a when it truly fits.

From your world

The kernel is built this way. A struct sock does not inherit a congestion-control algorithm; it holds a pointer to a struct tcp_congestion_ops, and setsockopt(TCP_CONGESTION, "bbr") swaps it for one socket at run time. A network device does not subclass a queueing discipline; tc qdisc replace dev eth0 root fq_codel attaches a different one. Each part is small, has one job, and can be changed without touching the object that holds it. That is composition, and the swappable part is a Strategy.

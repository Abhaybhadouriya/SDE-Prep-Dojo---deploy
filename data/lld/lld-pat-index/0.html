The story: recipes, not dishes

Explain like I'm 5

Your grandmother does not invent a new way to make dough every time she cooks. She knows a few tricks that always work: "knead, then let it rest", "add salt before, sugar after". She uses the same tricks for bread, pizza and parathas. Each trick has a name in the family, so when she says "do the resting trick", everyone knows what she means.

Design patterns are those named tricks for code. They are not a finished dish (not a library you download). They are a recipe idea you cook again in your own kitchen, with your own ingredients, every time you meet the same kind of problem.

In 1994 four authors (Gamma, Helm, Johnson and Vlissides, the "Gang of Four" or GoF) wrote down 23 such tricks in the book Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software. Every name you hear in an LLD interview - Factory, Strategy, Observer, Singleton - comes from that list or from a few later additions.

From your world

You already use patterns daily; the kernel just does not call them that. struct file_operations is a table of function pointers that each driver fills in: that is Strategy (and Bridge). Notifier chains (register_netdevice_notifier) are Observer. Netfilter hooks that return NF_ACCEPT or NF_DROP are Chain of Responsibility. The TCP state machine is State. kref with a release function is reference-counted ownership. Learning patterns is mostly learning the names interviewers use for shapes you have already written in C.

Where this breaks: in Java, Python and C++ the language builds the function-pointer tables for you (classes, interfaces, virtual methods), so the patterns look like classes and arrows instead of structs and registrations. Same ideas, different notation.

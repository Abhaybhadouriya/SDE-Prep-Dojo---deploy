8 and 9. Indirection and Protected Variations

Indirection: to avoid a direct link between two things, put an intermediate object between them. Protected Variations: find the points where you expect change (or instability), and put a stable interface around them so the change cannot leak into the rest of the system.

They work as a pair. Protected Variations says where to protect; Indirection (often an interface plus an adapter) is how. In our lot two things will clearly change:

  • Pricing rules - weekend flat rates, surge pricing, monthly passes. Protected by the PricingPolicy interface.
  • Payment providers - a UPI vendor today, cards and wallets tomorrow, each with its own SDK. Protected by PaymentGateway, with one adapter per vendor.

The demo code proves it: we build a second controller with new FlatPricing(3000) and no other class is touched. That is also what the Open/Closed Principle asks for. Protected Variations is the more general idea; OCP, information hiding, Dependency Inversion and even the Law of Demeter are ways to achieve it.

Even Clock is a small protected variation: real time is "unstable" in a test. Behind an interface, the demo moves time forward by setting a variable, so the fee logic is tested without waiting two hours.

Protect only predicted change. Ask "what does the interviewer or the problem say will vary?" Wrapping every class in an interface "just in case" breaks YAGNI and adds indirection that costs reading time. Two good walls beat ten speculative ones.

From your world

struct tcp_congestion_ops is Protected Variations done well: congestion control was the predicted point of change, so TCP talks to an ops table and CUBIC, BBR or Reno register behind it. The kernel deliberately does not protect its internal driver API (see stable-api-nonsense): it accepts the cost of changing callers to keep the code simple. Choosing where not to put a wall is a design decision too.

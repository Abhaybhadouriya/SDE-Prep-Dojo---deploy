The smells, in interview-priority order

Fowler and Beck named the classic smells in Refactoring (1999). Researchers later grouped them into families; the best-known grouping is by Mika Mäntylä, Jari Vanhanen and Casper Lassenius, "A Taxonomy and an Initial Empirical Study of Bad Smells in Code" (ICSM 2003), which uses seven groups: bloaters, object-orientation abusers, change preventers, dispensables, encapsulators, couplers and others. Several websites, refactoring.guru among them, present a five-group version of that idea and are good further reading.

For interview prep we sort the same smells differently: by how early a grader is likely to flag them in a machine-coding review. The "family" column keeps the useful question from the taxonomy - is it too big, misused OO, costly to change, unneeded, or too much coupling? - so you can still name the category when asked.

Smell to cure table

Our own one-line description of each smell, and the refactorings that usually cure it. Bold cures are shown in code below.

Tier 3 - know the name and the cure
18Lazy ClassunneededA class that does almost nothingInline Class, Collapse Hierarchy

20Parallel Inheritance Hierarchieschange costAdding a subclass here forces a subclass thereMove Method/Field so one hierarchy refers to the other

Hide Delegate (cure for Message Chains) and Remove Middle Man (cure for Middle Man) are opposites. Too many chains: add delegating methods. Too much delegation: remove them. Refactorings often come in such pairs, and the right direction depends on the smell you actually have.

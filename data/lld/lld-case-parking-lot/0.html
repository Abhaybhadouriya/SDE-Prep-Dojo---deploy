The story: the car park guard

Explain like I'm 5

A car park has a guard at the gate. A car comes in. The guard looks at the size of the car, finds an empty space that is big enough, writes the space number on a small paper ticket and gives it to the driver. When the car leaves, the driver gives the ticket back. The guard looks at how long the car stayed, asks for money, and marks the space as empty again.

The guard has three simple rules: a bike can park anywhere, a car needs a car-size space or bigger, and a truck needs the big space. Two cars must never get the same space, and one ticket can only be used once.

That is the whole problem. Everything an interviewer adds later (more floors, peak-hour prices, many gates at once, an electric-car charger) is a twist on that guard's notebook. Our job is to write the notebook as classes so that each twist changes one small piece and nothing else.

From your world

A parking lot is a memory allocator with size classes. Spot sizes are like the slab caches behind kmalloc (kmalloc-64, kmalloc-128, ...): a request goes to the smallest class that fits, and you keep a free list per class. "Nearest first" versus "best fit" is the same first-fit versus best-fit argument you know from allocators. The ticket is an opaque handle, like a file descriptor: the caller holds a number, the kernel holds the object, and using a handle twice must fail (think close() on an already closed fd returning EBADF).

Where this breaks: an allocator frees memory when asked; a car park must also charge on free, so time and money enter the picture, and the "free" call comes from an untrusted user who may lose or copy the ticket.

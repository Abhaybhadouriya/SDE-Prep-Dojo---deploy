Writer starvation and fairness

The simplest RW lock lets a reader in whenever no writer holds the lock. Under steady read traffic there is always some reader inside, so a waiting writer never gets in. Step through it:

  1. R1 and R2 hold the read lock together. Fine.
  2. Writer W arrives. It must wait until the readers inside leave.
  3. A reader-preferring lock lets new reader R3 in, because no writer holds the lock yet. With a steady stream of R3, R4, R5..., W never runs. Our naive lock made the writer wait 1.2 s, the whole time readers kept arriving.
  4. A writer-preferring lock makes R3 queue behind the waiting W. When R1 and R2 leave, W writes.
  5. Then R3 and the other queued readers go in together and see the new data. Java's lock made the writer wait about 4-6 ms in the same test.

Java's ReentrantReadWriteLock has two modes:

  • Nonfair (the default): no strict order, but a heuristic: a new reader blocks if the thread at the head of the wait queue is a writer. That prevents most writer starvation and keeps throughput high.
  • Fair (new ReentrantReadWriteLock(true)): roughly arrival order. When the lock frees up, either the longest-waiting writer gets it, or a group of readers that have all waited longer than every writer. More predictable, lower throughput.

From your world

The kernel's queued rwlock_t mostly queues fairly. In interrupt context, though, a reader may take the lock even while a writer waits, because an interrupt handler on the same CPU might otherwise deadlock against that writer. rw_semaphore queues new readers behind a waiting writer. This kind of starvation, plus the shared reader count bouncing between CPUs, is why kernel developers usually reach for RCU or seqlocks for hot read paths.

The story: the small desk

Explain like I'm 5

You have a huge bookshelf in the hall and a tiny desk that holds only three books. Walking to the shelf is slow, so you keep the books you are using on the desk. When you need a fourth book, one book must go back to the shelf. Which one?

A sensible rule: put back the book you have not touched for the longest time. That is Least Recently Used. Another rule: put back the book you have opened the fewest times. That is Least Frequently Used. A lazy rule: put back the book that came first. That is FIFO. The desk is the cache, the shelf is the database, and the rule is the eviction policy.

An interviewer wants two things from this problem. The data-structure core: get and put in O(1) time, which needs a hash map for lookup plus a doubly linked list for order. And the design: the rule for choosing a victim must be pluggable, because the Groww candidate below was told exactly that FIFO alone "isn't scalable".

From your world

You already know this cache: it is the Linux page cache. Pages sit on LRU lists made of struct list_head, an intrusive doubly linked list, and are found through a hash (the page-cache xarray). Touching a page moves it towards the active list; reclaim takes victims from the inactive tail. Newer kernels use multi-generational LRU (MGLRU). Dirty pages written later by flusher threads are write-back; O_SYNC makes writes write-through. ARP and neighbour entries ageing out are TTL. Our Node with prev/next is list_head, and EvictionPolicy is the reclaim policy behind an ops table.

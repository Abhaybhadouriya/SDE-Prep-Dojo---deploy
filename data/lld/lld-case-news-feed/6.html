Core flows

Pull vs push at a glance

Flow 1: post() with push fan-out

  1. The console parses post~hi for the logged-in user and calls the facade.
  2. The facade validates, then takes the next sequence number from an AtomicLong. The sequence breaks ties when two posts share a timestamp, so ordering is total.
  3. The post is stored: by id (for votes and comments) and appended to the author's own timeline (for pull feeds).
  4. Observer: the facade calls every registered listener. It does not know the push generator exists; it only knows the FeedListener interface.
  5. The push generator copies a reference into the author's and each follower's inbox, newest at the front, trimming to the cap. Cost: one deque push per follower.
  6. The id goes back to the console. With the pull generator, steps 4 and 5 do not happen; the work moves to read time.

Flow 2: reading a pull feed (k-way merge)

  1. Put the newest post of each source (ana follows bo and cy, plus herself) into a heap: one entry per timeline, so the heap has k entries.
  2. Pop the newest (012). Push the next older post from the same timeline (008).
  3. Pop 010, push 006. Each pop costs O(log k), and we never look at posts older than the page needs.
  4. After four pops the page is full. A deleted post would simply be skipped at the pop.
  5. The page comes back with an opaque cursor. LeetCode 355 is exactly this merge with a page size of 10.

From your world

The cursor is the same trick as a netlink dump. A dump that does not fit in one recvmsg() buffer stores where it stopped in cb->args[] of struct netlink_callback, and the next call resumes from there. The client never sees the format; it just calls again. Our cursor is an offset today, and because it is opaque we can change it to a keyset ("posts older than time T and seq S") later without breaking clients.

Interview follow-ups

Why the State pattern? Each light just cycles red, green, yellow.

A single light does just cycle, and an enum with a next() is right for it (our TrafficSignal). The controller is different: it has modes (normal green, yellow, clearance, emergency hold, fault) and six inputs whose meaning depends on the mode. An emergency during green ends the phase; during clearance it is queued; during a fault it is handled manually. One class per mode keeps those answers together and makes a new mode a new class.

How to answer

Separate the two levels of state out loud: signal colours (enum) and controller modes (State pattern).

How do you adjust green times with real-time traffic data?

Make timing a Strategy. Sensors report queue counts; at the start of each green, AdaptiveTiming computes enough time to clear the longest queue in the phase, clamped between a minimum (pedestrian and driver expectation) and a maximum (so the other road does not starve). Refinements: gap-out (end early when no car crossed the detector for a few seconds), max-out (force the end), and weighting by arrival rate. Swapping strategies at run time lets a city switch plans.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if a sensor fails? (Fall back to fixed timing: a strategy that wraps another and checks sensor health.)
  • How is it tested? (Feed counts with arrive, run ticks, assert durations.)
Why an all-red interval? Is yellow not enough?

Yellow tells drivers to stop, but a car that entered on the last moment of yellow is still crossing. The all-red clearance interval gives it time to leave before the crossing road gets green. It costs a couple of seconds per phase change, and it is why our emergency path also goes through it: an ambulance should not meet a car that is still in the junction.

An ambulance arrives. Walk me through what happens.

If its road is green, the controller enters EMERGENCY and holds green until it is cleared. Otherwise the current phase goes yellow, then all-red, then the ambulance's phase goes green and holds. Worst case response is yellow + all-red. On clear, the held phase goes yellow, all-red, and the cycle continues with the next phase or the next queued emergency. The safety rule is never bypassed.

Two emergency vehicles from crossing roads at the same time?

They cannot both have green. Queue them (FIFO, or a priority queue by vehicle type) and serve one at a time, each with full clearance. An emergency on the road already being served is merged. Real preemption systems also give a "clear" signal or a timeout so a stuck request does not hold a road forever; add a maximum hold time in EmergencyState.

How do you guarantee two roads are never green at once?

Three layers. Structure: the state machine has no transition from one phase's green to another's; it always passes YELLOW and ALL_RED. Local guard: TrafficSignal.set throws on GREEN to RED or YELLOW to GREEN. Independent check: a ConflictMonitor listener counts any moment with two live phases, and a random test runs 24,000 seconds of random inputs asserting zero. Real cabinets have the hardware version of the monitor, which forces flashing on a conflict.

Should the timer be a Singleton?

No. Time is an input: the controller has a tick() method (or takes a Clock interface). In production a scheduler calls it every second; in tests you call it 600 times in a loop. A Singleton timer makes tests slow and order-dependent and hides who controls time. If several junctions must share a cycle, pass the same clock to each, which is dependency injection, not a Singleton.

Add protected left-turn arrows.

Phases are data. Add phases NS-left and EW-left with their own directions or movements, and give the controller a phase sequence (NS-left, NS, EW-left, EW). Movements that may run together are described by a conflict matrix, which the conflict monitor uses instead of "phases are exclusive". The states do not change; skipping a left phase when no car waits is a timing decision (green time 0 means skip).

How does it scale to a whole city?

Each junction keeps its own controller and stays safe on its own if the network fails (it falls back to its local plan). A central system computes plans and offsets for green waves from aggregated sensor data and pushes them as strategy configurations; controllers stream events to it through their listeners. That central part is HLD: queues, time-series storage, and optimisation, not part of the LLD answer.

Threads: the clock, sensors and the emergency receiver all call in. How do you stay correct?

Every public method takes the controller's lock, so one transition (several light changes) is atomic and an emergency cannot interleave with a tick. States run only under that lock. Listeners must be quick or hand off to a queue. Alternatively, all inputs go into one event queue drained by one thread, which removes locking from the domain and gives a replayable log. Safety does not depend on timing; a late tick only slows the cycle.

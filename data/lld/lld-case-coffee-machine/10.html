Concurrency and thread safety

This is where the Dunzo round is won or lost. There are two kinds of threads: callers placing orders (and an operator refilling), and N outlet threads pouring.

  1. The oversell race Two outlets both read "milk = 150", both decide a latte fits, both subtract 150: milk is now -150. This is check-then-act. Fix: check and subtract inside one critical section (synchronized block in Java, with self._lock in Python). The 200-order test in the demo exists to catch exactly this race.
  2. One lock for the whole recipe, not one per ingredient Per-ingredient locks (or a ConcurrentHashMap with atomic decrements) make each subtraction safe but not the recipe: thread A takes the coffee, thread B takes the milk, and both then fail on the other, half-taking two recipes. If you do want finer locks, take them in a fixed global order (sorted by name) to avoid deadlock, and roll back on failure. At vending-machine scale one inventory lock held for microseconds is simpler and fast enough.
  3. N in parallel A fixed pool of N threads guarantees at most N pour calls at once; the demo measures the peak with an atomic counter. An alternative is a Semaphore(N) acquired around brewing, with the caller's own thread doing the work. The pool is better here: callers do not block for the brew time.
  4. No slow work under the lock Brewing (seconds on real hardware) happens outside the inventory lock. Listeners are called after the lock is released, so a slow SMS sender cannot stall every order, and a listener that calls back into the inventory cannot deadlock.
  5. Immutable shared objects Menu beverages and recipes are read by every thread; they never change, so they need no lock. Decorators create new objects per order.
  6. Refill races Refill takes the same lock, so it is atomic with respect to reservations. Revenue and the busy counter are atomics (Java) or protected by the machine's small lock (Python).
  7. Shutdown close() follows a small protocol so stock never leaks. (1) It sets a volatile closed flag first; order() checks the flag before reserving, so new orders are refused without touching the inventory. (2) An order that passed the check just before close() can still have its submit rejected by the shutting-down pool (RejectedExecutionException); that path releases the reservation and returns a "machine closed" result instead of throwing. (3) A pending counter tracks reserved-but-not-poured orders, and close() loops on awaitTermination until the pool has drained, logging how many are still pouring. So an accepted (already reserved) order is always poured, and a refused one has taken nothing; check 8 in the demo ("closed machine does not leak stock") tests it.

From your world

The per-ingredient-lock trap is the classic "lock two inodes for rename" problem: the VFS takes both locks in a fixed order (lock_rename) to avoid ABBA deadlock. And "call listeners after dropping the lock" is why kernel code collects work under a spinlock and runs callbacks (or call_rcu) after unlocking, and why notifier chains are not called with a spinlock held when the callbacks may sleep.

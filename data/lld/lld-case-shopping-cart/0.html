The story: the shop counter and the coupon box

Explain like I'm 5

You put a toy car and two T-shirts in a basket and walk to the counter. The shopkeeper adds up the prices. Then you hand over two coupons. The first one says "20% off clothes". The second says "10% off everything, but never more than 500". The shopkeeper reads each coupon, checks its small print ("is there a piece of clothing in the basket?", "is the coupon still valid today?"), and if the small print is fine, takes the money off. A third coupon says "200 off, cannot be used with any other coupon", so the shopkeeper hands it back.

Last, the shopkeeper checks the shelf: is there really one toy car left? If yes, you pay and the car leaves the shelf. If someone else took the last one a second earlier, nothing is charged and nothing leaves the shelf.

In the interview you write that shopkeeper as code. Three things decide the grade. First, coupon rules must be small pieces you can combine ("minimum cart 1000 and valid until 30 Nov"), not one giant if. Second, discounts must stack in a defined order and never push the total below zero; money is integer paise, never double. Third, checkout must be all-or-nothing: stock for every line is checked and decremented together, so two buyers can never both get the last laptop.

From your world

You have seen every piece of this before. A coupon rule is a predicate you compose, exactly like a BPF filter expression: tcp and port 443 and not host 10.0.0.1 is three small predicates joined by and / not. The discount types are an ops table: struct discount_ops { long (*apply)(const struct cart *, long running); }, one instance per type, the way each filesystem fills in its own file_operations. And the pricing pipeline is a priority-ordered hook chain: netfilter runs hooks sorted by priority (conntrack before NAT before filter), and every hook sees the packet as the previous hook left it. Our coupons run sorted by priority too, each seeing the running total the previous one left.

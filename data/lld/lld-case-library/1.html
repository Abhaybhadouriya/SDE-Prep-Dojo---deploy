How it is asked

Library management is short enough for a 45-minute machine-coding slot and rich enough for a 60-minute design discussion, which is why it shows up in both. Interviewers use it to check modelling basics (type versus instance, ownership, state) more than algorithms. Round styles are covered in How LLD rounds work.

Open-source LLD collections and course playlists also tag Google and Flipkart for this problem; we found no recent first-hand report for those two, so treat that as a signal, not a fact. The requirement list most people practise comes from the classic object-oriented design books and is repeated in the public low-level-design-primer question list and in awesome-low-level-design: search by title, author, subject or date; a unique id and a rack number per book; several copies per book ("book items"), each of which can be checked out or reserved; a limit on books per member and on days per loan; fines for late returns; notifications when a reserved book is available.

The Zepto report is a useful warning: the candidate "solved the problem and handled the edge cases" by the interviewer's own words and was still rejected. The candidate used Singleton and Abstract Factory, solved the edge cases and was still rejected without feedback: extra patterns are not a substitute for clean modelling. What earns points is the Book / BookCopy split, a clean state model for copies, the reservation hand-off, and being able to explain each choice.

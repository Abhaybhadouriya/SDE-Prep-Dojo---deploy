In interviews

DI is rarely asked as "implement DI". It is graded in every machine-coding round: interviewers look for services that receive repositories and strategies through constructors, a clean main or driver that wires everything, and code they can extend without rewriting. Java-heavy companies (in the interview reports we collected, Walmart's LLD rounds are almost all tagged Java + LLD) also ask Spring-flavoured theory: constructor vs field injection, bean scopes, circular dependencies, and why @Transactional on a self-call does nothing. A LeetCode Discuss thread asking whether big companies ask about DI, Factory and Strategy is a common starting point; the short answer from the reports is yes, mostly as follow-ups.

  • Parking lot: inject the spot-allocation strategy, the fee calculator and the clock (for fees by duration).
  • Rate limiter: inject the clock, or token-bucket refill cannot be tested.
  • Splitwise, BookMyShow, library: services receive in-memory repositories.
  • Notification service: inject a list of channel senders, or a map from channel to sender.
  • Digital wallet and checkout: inject payment gateways behind one interface.

See code layout for machine coding for where the composition root goes in a 90-minute round.

Strong 30-second answer: "Dependency Injection means a class receives its collaborators instead of creating them. I use constructor injection for required dependencies so fields are final and the object is always valid, and I wire everything in one composition root, usually main. It makes the class testable with fakes, including a fixed clock, and lets me swap implementations without editing it. It is the usual way to apply the Dependency Inversion Principle. A container like Spring automates the wiring, but in an interview I wire by hand."

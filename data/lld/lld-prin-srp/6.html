Over-splitting: when SRP goes too far

The opposite failure is real, and senior interviewers probe for it. If you create InvoiceTotalAdder, InvoiceTaxMultiplier, InvoiceRoundingApplier and InvoiceLineIterator, every rule change from finance now touches four files. That is shotgun surgery, and it is the mirror image of the god class.

Signs you have split too far:

  • Classes with one method and no state that are only ever called from one place.
  • Data classes with no behaviour, while "service" classes do all the work on them (the anemic domain model).
  • You need a diagram to follow one simple request through eight classes.
  • Every change from one actor still edits many files.

A simple guide: split when you can name two different actors, or when you already feel the pain (a test needs a DB mock, two tickets collide in one file). Do not split on the guess that someone might one day want a different rounding rule. That guess is the YAGNI trap. In an interview, say the trade-off: "I kept tax and rounding together because both belong to finance and change together."

From your world

The kernel has the same tension. Splitting a driver into a core module and bus-specific glue (PCI, USB) pays off, because the buses really are different callers with different change histories. Splitting one tiny register helper into its own file does not. LWN's "midlayer mistake" article (kernel design patterns, part 3) warns about layers that every driver is forced through even when they add nothing: structure without a real reason to change.

The story: knowing the parts vs driving the car

Explain like I'm 5

Two kids take a bicycle test. The first one recites: "A bicycle has two wheels, pedals, a chain and brakes." The second one says the same, then rides around the cone, and when the tester asks "what if the chain falls off?", she shows how to put it back. Both know the words. Only one gets the licence.

OOP interview questions work the same way. "What is polymorphism?" is the first sentence of the test, not the test. Interviewers move quickly from the definition to a small piece of code, then to a trade-off, then to "where did you use this?". This page gives you all four layers for every question.

  1. Define in one plain sentence. "Polymorphism means one call, many behaviours, chosen by the object's real type."
  2. Example from a business domain, not "Animal/Dog": "PaymentMethod.pay() runs UPI, card or wallet logic."
  3. Code: write the 5-10 lines that prove it (interface, two classes, a loop calling the method).
  4. Trade-off: when you would not use it, or what it costs (an indirect call; a class explosion if overused).
  5. Bridge to your experience: "In the kernel this is a struct of function pointers like file_operations." This is the line that makes your answer memorable.

From your world

Your kernel background is an advantage here, so use it out loud. Polymorphism is a struct of function pointers (file_operations, net_device_ops); container_of() is a downcast; kref is shared ownership; an opaque handle is encapsulation; the goto cleanup ladder is manual RAII. One such line per answer makes you stand out from candidates who only recite definitions. Background: From C Structs to Objects.

The story: the mango market

Explain like I'm 5

At a mango market, sellers shout prices: "10 mangoes for 15 rupees each!", "5 for 15.05!". Buyers shout too: "I want 12, I will pay up to 15.10!". The market keeper writes every shout on a board: sellers in one column, cheapest at the top; buyers in another, highest price at the top.

When a buyer's price is at least the cheapest seller's price, the keeper shakes their hands and the mangoes change hands. If two sellers ask the same price, the one who shouted first sells first. If the buyer wants more mangoes than the first seller has, the keeper moves to the next seller. That is all a stock exchange does, a few million times a second.

The board is the order book. A shout is an order (side, price, quantity). A handshake is a trade. "Best price first, then first come first served" is price-time priority. When a buyer takes only part of what a seller offers, that is a partial fill.

Your mother, who wants to buy mangoes but cannot stand in the market, uses a broker: a shop that holds her money, checks she can pay before it shouts for her, and puts the mangoes in her basket after the handshake. Zerodha and Groww are brokers; NSE and BSE are exchanges. Interviews often ask for both in one round.

From your world

An order book is a priority queue of FIFO queues, the same shape as a strict-priority scheduler with per-class FIFOs: the egress scheduler on a switch port picks the highest-priority non-empty queue, then the head of that queue. Here, "priority" is the price. The matching engine for one symbol is single-threaded by design, like a per-CPU softirq that owns its queue: no locks inside the hot loop, ordering is free. And a trade notification fan-out to the broker, the ticker and the alert engine is a notifier chain (atomic_notifier_call_chain): Observer.

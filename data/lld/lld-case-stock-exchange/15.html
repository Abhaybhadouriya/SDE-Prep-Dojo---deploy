Follow-up questions

Which data structure holds the order book, and what are the costs?

Per side, a sorted map from price to a FIFO queue of orders (TreeMap<Long, ArrayDeque<Order>>; bids in reverse order). Best price: O(log P) where P is the number of distinct prices (or O(1) with a cached first entry). Matching at the head of a level: O(1). Adding at a new price: O(log P). Cancel: O(1) to find the order by id in a hash map, then O(k) to remove from its level's deque; a doubly linked list per level makes it O(1).

How to answer

Start from "priority queue of FIFO queues", then explain why a plain heap of orders is worse: no cheap cancel and ties need a sequence number.

Likely follow-ups
  • Prices are in ticks within a band; can you do better? (An array indexed by tick: O(1) everything, as many real engines do.)
At what price does a trade happen when a buy at 1510 meets a sell at 1500?

At the resting order's price. If the sell was resting, 1500: the buyer gets price improvement. If the buy was resting at 1510 and a sell at 1500 arrives, the trade is at 1510. The arriving order is the "taker", the resting one the "maker"; the maker set the price first.

How do you make it concurrent without deadlocks?

One lock per order book (symbol), one lock per account. Fixed order: book then account; never two accounts at once; placement releases the account lock before taking the book lock. Then prove it with a stress test that checks conservation. In production, swap locks for one thread per symbol fed by a queue.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why a fair lock? (FIFO arrival into the book for competing threads.)
  • What if a listener is slow?
How do you stop a user spending the same money on two orders?

Block funds at order time, under the account lock, in the same critical section as the funds check: cash -= limit x qty; blocked += limit x qty. Settlement takes from blocked; cancel and IOC remainders give back to cash. For a market buy, block at the top of the price band (worst case) and refund after fills. Same for sells with shares.

Why is the exchange not allowed to touch user balances?

Single responsibility and real-world boundaries: the exchange matches orders from many brokers and has no idea of their customers' wallets. Keeping money in the broker means the matching engine stays small, fast and reusable, and the broker can be tested with a fake exchange. The link is one interface (TradeListener): Dependency Inversion.

Add stop-loss orders.

A stop order is dormant until the last price crosses its trigger. Keep it out of the main book, in a trigger map sorted by trigger price (sell-stops trigger when price falls to or below; buy-stops when it rises to or above). A TradeListener checks the map after each trade and submits triggered orders. Watch for cascades: a triggered order trades, moves the price, triggers more; process them in a loop in a defined order.

Why the price band, and how is it computed?

It stops fat-finger errors and manipulation: a limit price far from the market is rejected. We compute it from the last traded price: [last x (100 - b)/100, last x (100 + b)/100] in integer paise. It is one OrderRule, so changing the base (previous close) or making it per-stock is local.

How would you make the engine recoverable after a crash?

Make matching deterministic and log every input (orders, cancels) with its sequence number before processing. On restart, replay the log to rebuild the books exactly; take periodic snapshots to shorten replay. Trades are outputs and can be re-derived. This is event sourcing, and the reason we keep a sequence number and avoid wall-clock or random inputs inside matching.

Flipkart's "time shift" moves every price randomly. Where does that go?

It is a market-simulator feature, not exchange matching. Put it in a PriceSimulator service with a counter (max 10 per day) and an injected Random (seeded in tests). It updates each stock's last price and notifies price listeners: the same Observer path as real trades.

How is a pro-rata market different, and how would your design change?

Pro-rata splits an incoming order across all resting orders at the best price in proportion to their size, instead of first-come-first-served. Only the inner loop changes: extract it to a MatchingPolicy strategy, keep the price-level structure, and pass the whole level to the policy. Rounding remainders still need a deterministic tie-break (usually time).

The story: one librarian, many readers

Explain like I'm 5

A tiny library keeps only 100 books on its front shelf. When you borrow a book, the librarian moves it to the front of the shelf, so the books nobody touches drift to the back. When a new book arrives and the shelf is full, the book at the very back goes to the basement.

One librarian, one line: everything is neat. Now imagine eight librarians all moving books on the same shelf at once, each with both hands. Two of them grab the same book. One puts it at the front, the other puts the book next to it somewhere else, and soon the shelf has gaps, a book in two places, and a "full" shelf with 300 books on it.

The fix is not "fewer readers". It is: moving a book is a change, so only one librarian may move books on one shelf at a time. If the library is busy, build eight shelves, each with its own librarian.

That is this whole page in one picture. A thread-safe class is one that stays correct no matter how many threads call it and how their steps interleave. Making something thread-safe means finding the shared state, finding every step that must not be interleaved (the invariants), and protecting each with the right tool: one lock (coarse-grained), many locks (fine-grained), or no lock at all (atomics / CAS).

From your world

You have made these exact choices in C. A big lock around a whole subsystem (the old BKL) vs per-bucket spinlocks in a hash table is coarse vs fine-grained. atomic_inc() is AtomicLong. percpu_counter (per-CPU deltas, summed when read) is exactly Java's LongAdder. And the token bucket is the algorithm behind tc's TBF qdisc and the kernel's __ratelimit() for log messages.

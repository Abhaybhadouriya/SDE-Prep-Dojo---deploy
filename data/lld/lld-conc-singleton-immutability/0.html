The story: the wet paint sign

Explain like I'm 5

A shop gets one big sign that says what time it opens. A painter is making it. As soon as the board is nailed to the wall, people walking past look at it. But the painter has not finished: the board is up, the paint is still wet, and half the letters are missing. One person reads "OPENS AT 1" and goes home. The board was visible before it was finished.

The fix is simple: paint the whole sign in the back room, and only nail it up when it is completely done. After that, nobody ever changes it. If the opening time changes, the painter makes a new sign in the back room and swaps the old one out in one quick move. People see either the old sign or the new sign, never a half-painted one.

That is this whole page. "Only one sign" is a singleton. "Visible before finished" is the double-checked locking bug. "Finish it in the back room, then hang it" is safe publication. "Never change it" is immutability. "Make a new one and swap" is copy-on-write.

From your world

You have fought this exact bug in C. A driver allocates a struct, fills it in, and stores the pointer in a global for an interrupt handler or another CPU to use. Without a barrier (smp_wmb() before the store, or rcu_assign_pointer()), the other CPU can see the new pointer before it sees the fields. The kernel's fix is RCU: build the new object privately, publish the pointer with release ordering, readers use rcu_dereference(), and the old copy is freed after a grace period. Java's volatile reference plus an immutable object is the same design, with the garbage collector playing the part of the grace period.

Core flows: rate, then rank

  1. A test (or the driver) records a rating for alice on 20 January.
  2. The service validates first: stars must be an integer from 1 to 5, the agent id and date must be present. A bad rating changes nothing.
  3. It replaces alice's all-time Stats (5, 1) with (9, 2) in one atomic compute, then does the same in the January bucket. No average is stored anywhere.
  4. Later, averages() takes a snapshot of the all-time entries and wraps each as an AgentScore row.
  5. The rows are sorted by the ranking policy. The comparator compares averages exactly by cross-multiplying, then breaks ties by count and id.
  6. An exporter turns the rows into CSV. The service only passes rows through; it knows nothing about commas or quotes.

Comparing averages exactly

To decide whether a.sum / a.count is bigger than b.sum / b.count, compare a.sum * b.count with b.sum * a.count. Counts are positive, so the inequality keeps its direction, and there is no division and no floating point. Two averages that are mathematically equal (4/1 and 8/2) compare as exactly equal, so the tie-break rule always applies. With double, values like 1/3 and 2/6 are usually equal, but sums of many values can differ in the last bit and silently skip your tie-break. With long, the products are safe while countA x countB x 5 stays below 9.2e18 (Long.MAX_VALUE), because a sum is at most 5 x its count: that is up to about 1.3 billion ratings per agent. Beyond that, use Math.multiplyExact (it throws on overflow instead of wrapping), or compare with BigInteger, or with a 128-bit __int128 in C.

For display, round half-up to two decimals with integer maths: hundredths = (sum * 200 + count) / (2 * count). For 14 / 3 this gives 467, shown as 4.67. Java's BigDecimal.divide(count, 2, HALF_UP) gives the same answer if you prefer a library call.

The story: the gold-star chart

Explain like I'm 5

A classroom has a star chart. Every time a helper helps someone, the person gives them 1 to 5 stars. The teacher wants to know who the best helper is. You cannot just count stars, because a helper who helped ten times has more stars than one who helped twice. So you keep two numbers per helper: how many stars in total and how many times they were rated. The average is total divided by times.

Then the teacher asks harder questions. "Two helpers both have 4.5. Who goes first?" "Who was best in January only?" "Can you print the chart so I can paste it into a spreadsheet?" Each question is a small change if you kept the two numbers, and a painful one if you only kept the average.

The interview is that star chart, built live in about 60 minutes, with tests. The core is small on purpose. What is graded is how your design absorbs each new requirement: whether "per month" is a one-line change or a rewrite, whether you notice that (5 + 4) / 2 in integer maths is 4, not 4.5, and whether ties produce the same order every run.

From your world

This is the kernel's statistics habit. You never store an average in a counter; you keep sum and count (like rx_bytes and rx_packets) and derive averages at read time, so they can be combined, windowed or reset. "Best agent per month" is a per-interval bucket, like keeping per-CPU counters and summing them, or a ring of per-second buckets in a rate estimator. Comparing two averages without division (a.sum * b.count versus b.sum * a.count) is the same trick as comparing ratios in fixed-point code where floating point is not allowed.

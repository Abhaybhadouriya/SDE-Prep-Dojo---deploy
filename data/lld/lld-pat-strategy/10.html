In interviews

Strategy is a Tier 1 pattern: in the interview reports we collected, it is the pattern named most often, and it is the standard answer to the extensibility probe "how would you add a new X without touching existing classes?". Where it fits in the case studies:

Case studyWhat varies (the strategy)
Parking lotFee calculation (hourly, flat, weekend); spot allocation (nearest, by floor, by type)
SplitwiseSplit type: equal, exact, percentage, shares
Ride hailingFare (base, surge, pool) and driver matching (nearest, best rated)
LRU cache and KV storeEviction policy: LRU, LFU, FIFO
Rate limiterAlgorithm: token bucket, fixed window, sliding window
Digital wallet and shopping cartPayment method (UPI, card, wallet); discount and coupon rules
Stock exchangeOrder matching (price-time priority, pro-rata)
ElevatorScheduling: nearest car, SCAN / LOOK, zoning
Snake and ladder, tic-tac-toeDice or move strategy; win-check rule; bot player

Strong 30-second answer: "Fee calculation is the part that varies, so I'll put it behind a FeeStrategy interface with one method, and inject it into the gate. Each tariff is its own class, tested alone. A small registry maps the lot's config to a strategy, so adding a tariff is one new class and one registry line; the gate never changes. That is Open/Closed. Strategies are stateless, so they are thread-safe and shared."

Common mistake: naming Strategy for everything. If there is only one fee rule in the requirements, say "I'll keep it behind an interface because the interviewer hinted at more tariffs" or keep it simple. Over-engineering is a stated failure reason in several reports.

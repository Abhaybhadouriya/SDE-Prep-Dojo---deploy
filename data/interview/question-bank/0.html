The story: a box of flashcards

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you are learning to ride a bike. Reading a book about bikes helps a little. Riding helps a lot. This page is the riding part. It is a big box of flashcards. On the front of each card is a question a grown-up might ask you. On the back is a good answer and a little hint about how to say it. You pick a card, say your answer out loud, flip it over, and check. Cards you get right go to the back of the box. Cards you get wrong come back tomorrow. Soon you know the whole box.

An interview is a conversation, not an exam paper. The interviewer asks a question, listens to how you build your answer, then pushes on the weakest part with a follow-up. So each card here has three parts: the model answer, the approach (the order to say things in), and the follow-ups you should expect.

The questions come from three places: the questions people report from real loops at Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Flipkart, Walmart, Uber, Atlassian and Indian product companies (collected in the research notes for this site), the official docs of each technology, and the learner questions written for this site. The company tags mean "commonly reported at" that company. They do not mean "always asked there". Loops change by team and year.

How the company tags were assigned. A company appears on a card only where public candidate reports (LeetCode Discuss and Glassdoor posts collected in our research notes) or that company's well-known focus support it: Walmart on Kafka and order or booking designs, Flipkart on e-commerce and machine coding, Amazon on Leadership Principles, Locker and rate limiters, Uber on its own domain and on monitoring, Microsoft on chat, unique IDs and caches, Atlassian on rapid-fire basics, crawlers and top-K. Generic fundamentals (most Docker, Kubernetes and pipeline cards) carry no company tag, because every company can ask them. So filtering by a company shows what is specific to it; do the untagged fundamentals for every loop.

From your world

Think of this page as a regression test suite for your own head. Each question is a test case. The model answer is the expected output. The follow-ups are the edge cases. You would never ship a driver after running only the happy path; do not walk into a loop after reading only the happy-path answers. Where this analogy breaks: a test either passes or fails, but an interview answer is graded on reasoning, so a different answer with a clear trade-off can still score full marks.

  1. Start with "must know". About 45 cards carry a green must know chip; the Priority menu shows only those. Do them first.
  2. Pick a filter. Then take one topic at Level 2 and 3. Use the Level, Topic and Company menus above the first question, or type a word such as "lag" or "OOM".
  3. Answer out loud before opening. Give yourself 60 to 90 seconds. Speaking is a different skill from knowing.
  4. Open and compare. Did you name the mechanism, a number and a trade-off? Read the "How to answer" note.
  5. Try one follow-up. Answer it without looking anything up. If you cannot, follow the link to the concept page.
  6. Revisit. Come back to the ones you missed after one day, then three days, then a week.

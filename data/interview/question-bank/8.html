System design fundamentals (38)

The building blocks every design round leans on: scaling, load balancing, caching, databases, consistency, queues, reliability and the interview process itself. Concept pages: fundamentals, building blocks, 45-minute framework, estimation.

Level 2 - Beginner 7 questions

What do you say first when the interviewer finishes the prompt?

Do not draw yet. Ask clarifying questions to fix scope: who the users are, the 2-3 core features, and the scale (daily users, reads vs writes, data size, latency target, consistency needs). Then state your assumptions out loud and write the functional and non-functional requirements in a corner of the board. Then follow a fixed order: estimates, API, data model, high-level diagram, deep dives, bottlenecks, wrap-up.

How to answer

Show that you own the structure. A 3-5 minute requirements phase is expected and scored.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the interviewer says "you decide"?
What does "a million users" mean in requests per second?

Turn it into numbers: 1M daily active users doing 10 actions a day is 10M requests a day. A day is about 86,400 s, round to 100,000, so 10M / 10^5 = about 100 requests per second on average. Peak is often 2-3x average for consumer apps (state it as a rule of thumb) - say 300 RPS. Flash sales can spike far higher. One modern server can often handle that; the hard part at "a million users" is usually data size, peaks and availability, not average RPS.

How to answer

Do the maths aloud in round numbers. Show the shortcut "1M per day is about 12 per second".

Likely follow-ups
  • How much storage per year if each action writes 1 KB?
Vertical vs horizontal scaling?

Vertical: a bigger machine (more CPU, RAM). Simple, no code change, but it has a ceiling, costs rise steeply and it stays a single point of failure. Horizontal: more machines behind a load balancer. Needs stateless app servers (sessions in Redis or a token) and a plan for the data layer (replicas, sharding), but scales further and survives a machine loss. Usual path: scale the app tier horizontally early, keep the database vertical as long as it is comfortable, then add read replicas, caching and finally sharding.

How to answer

Give the trade-off and the realistic order teams follow.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why is the database the hardest tier to scale horizontally?
L4 vs L7 load balancer?

L4 balances TCP/UDP connections by the 5-tuple: fast, protocol-agnostic, cannot see URLs (AWS NLB, IPVS, Maglev). L7 terminates HTTP: routes by path, header or cookie, terminates TLS, retries, rate-limits, adds auth (ALB, NGINX, Envoy, HAProxy http mode). A typical design has L4 at the edge spreading to a fleet of L7 proxies or gateways. Algorithms: round robin, least connections, consistent hashing for stickiness, power of two choices.

How to answer

From your world: L4 is ECMP plus NAT; L7 is a proxy that reads the payload. Then name where each sits in the diagram.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does an LB know a backend is unhealthy? (active probes and passive outlier detection)
SQL vs NoSQL - how do you justify it in one sentence?

Use the trade-off sentence: "I'll use Postgres for orders because we need transactions across order, payment and inventory rows and strong constraints; the cost is harder horizontal write scaling; if writes outgrow one primary I'd shard by customer ID." Or: "I'll use Cassandra/DynamoDB for chat messages because the access pattern is 'messages by conversation, newest first' at huge write volume; the cost is no joins and limited transactions." Choose by access pattern, consistency need and scale - not by fashion.

How to answer

Always attach the reason and the cost. Interviewers grade the reasoning, not the brand.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can a SQL database scale to billions of rows? (yes, with partitioning and sharding, e.g. Vitess or Citus)
What is a cache, where can it go, and how does it go stale?

A cache keeps a copy of hot data in a faster place. Layers: browser/client, CDN at the edge, an in-process cache, a shared cache (Redis/Memcached), and the database's own buffer pool. It goes stale when the source changes and the copy does not: fix with TTLs, deleting the key after a DB write (cache-aside), or invalidation driven by the DB change stream. The hard parts are invalidation, stampedes when hot keys expire, and hot keys themselves.

How to answer

From your world: CPU caches and the TLB - hit ratio, eviction and the cost of a miss. Then the three classic problems.

Likely follow-ups
  • Cache-aside vs write-through?
What is a CDN, and is it like anycast?

A CDN is a network of edge servers close to users that cache static content (images, JS, video segments) and terminate TLS near the user. Partly anycast: many CDNs announce the same IP from many PoPs so BGP routes users to the nearest one; others use DNS-based steering. On a miss the edge fetches from an origin (pull) or content is pre-placed (push, like Netflix Open Connect filling appliances overnight).

How to answer

Confirm the anycast intuition, then explain pull vs push and cache keys and TTLs.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you invalidate a cached asset? (versioned file names beat purge requests)

Level 3 - Intermediate 17 questions

Cache-aside vs write-through vs write-back?

Cache-aside: the app reads the cache; on a miss it reads the DB and fills the cache; on a write it updates the DB then deletes the key. Simple and resilient; first read after a write is a miss. Write-through: write the cache and the DB together; reads are always warm, writes are slower. Write-back: write to the cache and flush to the DB later; fastest writes, but data is lost if the cache dies before flushing. Default to cache-aside; delete rather than update the key to avoid races.

How to answer

One line each plus when to use. Then "delete, don't update" as the expert tip.

Likely follow-ups
  • What race makes "update the cache on write" dangerous?
What is a cache stampede and how do you prevent it?

A hot key expires and thousands of requests miss at once, all hitting the database together (a thundering herd). Fixes: request coalescing or a short lock so one request refills while others wait or get the stale value; jittered TTLs so keys do not expire together; early probabilistic refresh before expiry; serving stale-while-revalidate. After a cache cluster restart, warm it gradually and rate-limit DB reads.

How to answer

Name it, give the failure story, then three fixes.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is cache penetration? (lookups for keys that do not exist; cache the null or use a Bloom filter)
What is sharding and how do you pick a shard key?

Sharding splits one dataset across many databases, each holding a subset of keys. Pick a key that (1) appears in most queries so a request hits one shard, (2) has high cardinality and even spread, and (3) does not concentrate writes in time. Range sharding keeps scans cheap but creates hot spots; hash sharding spreads evenly but loses range scans. Watch for celebrity keys and plan re-sharding with consistent hashing or a directory service.

How to answer

Give the three rules, then an example: "shard orders by customer_id, not by order date".

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens to cross-shard queries and transactions?
Explain consistent hashing.

Place servers and keys on a hash ring; each key belongs to the next server clockwise. When a server is added or removed, only the keys between it and its neighbour move - about K/N keys instead of nearly all of them with hash mod N. Virtual nodes (many ring points per server) smooth out uneven load and let bigger servers take more points. Used by Dynamo-style stores, Cassandra and many cache clients.

How to answer

From your world: resilient hashing in ECMP, so a link change does not reshuffle every flow.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you handle a hot key that one server cannot serve?
Leader-follower vs multi-leader vs leaderless replication?

Leader-follower: one node takes writes, followers copy; simple, but async followers lag (stale reads) and failover needs care. Multi-leader: several nodes take writes (often one per region); lower write latency, but concurrent writes conflict and need resolution. Leaderless (Dynamo-style): any replica takes reads and writes with quorums; with N=3, W=2, R=2 you get W + R > N so reads overlap the latest write; conflicts are resolved with versions.

How to answer

One line each with its main cost. Then the quorum formula.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is read-your-writes and how do you provide it with followers?
Is CAP really "pick two"? What is PACELC?

Not really. Network partitions will happen, so the real choice is: during a partition, refuse some requests to stay consistent (CP) or answer with possibly stale data (AP). PACELC adds the normal case: Else (no partition) you trade Latency against Consistency, because synchronous replication is slower. Most real systems let you tune per operation.

How to answer

Say "P is not optional" first. Then give a user-facing example: a bank balance (CP) vs a like counter (AP).

Likely follow-ups
  • Which do you choose for a shopping cart?
Strong vs eventual consistency - what does the user see?

Strong: once a write is acknowledged, every reader sees it - like refreshing your bank app right after a transfer. Eventual: readers may see old data briefly, but all replicas converge - like a like count that catches up a second later. Middle ground: read-your-writes (you always see your own post) and monotonic reads (you never see time go backwards). Pick strong for money, inventory decrements and bookings; eventual for feeds, counts and recommendations.

How to answer

Use a screen-level example for each. Users do not care about the model, only what they see.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you give read-your-writes with a lagging replica?
Token bucket vs leaky bucket vs sliding window rate limiting?

Token bucket: tokens refill at rate r up to capacity B; each request takes one; allows bursts up to B. Leaky bucket: requests queue and drain at a fixed rate; smooth output, no bursts. Fixed window: count per minute; simple but allows a 2x burst at the window edge. Sliding window log: exact timestamps in a sorted set; precise but memory heavy. Sliding window counter: weight the previous window's count; cheap and close enough. Distributed: keep counters in Redis with an atomic Lua script; return HTTP 429 with Retry-After.

How to answer

From your world: these are exactly QoS policers and shapers. Say that, then pick token bucket for APIs.

Likely follow-ups
  • What do you do if Redis is down? (fail open with local limits, usually)
What is an idempotency key and why does payments care?

The client generates a unique key per logical operation (for example a UUID for "pay order 123") and sends it with every retry. The server stores the key with the result under a unique constraint; a retry with the same key returns the stored result instead of charging again. Networks time out after the server has already acted, so without it a retry can double-charge. Store a hash of the request too, so the same key with a different body is rejected.

How to answer

From your world: sequence numbers that let a receiver drop retransmissions. Then the "timeout after success" story.

Likely follow-ups
  • How long do you keep idempotency keys?
Message queue vs pub/sub vs event stream?

A queue delivers each message to one worker and deletes it on ack - for distributing jobs. Pub/sub delivers each message to every subscriber - for fan-out. An event stream/log (Kafka) retains ordered events; each consumer group reads at its own pace and can replay - for both patterns plus history. Pick by: does each message go to one worker or many, and do you need replay?

How to answer

Two questions decide it; say them.

Likely follow-ups
  • When would you put a queue in front of a database?
Polling vs long polling vs WebSockets vs SSE?

Polling: the client asks every N seconds; simple, wasteful, adds latency. Long polling: the server holds the request until there is data; near real time with plain HTTP. SSE: one long HTTP response streaming server-to-client events; simple, one direction. WebSockets: a full-duplex connection; best for chat, games and collaboration, but stateful connections complicate load balancing and deploys. Rule: SSE for server push only, WebSockets when the client also sends often.

How to answer

Compare on direction, latency and server cost.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you route a message to the server holding a user's WebSocket?
REST vs gRPC vs GraphQL?

REST/JSON over HTTP: universal, cacheable, easy to debug - default for public APIs. gRPC: Protobuf over HTTP/2, typed contracts, streaming, efficient - great between internal services. GraphQL: the client asks for exactly the fields it needs across resources - good for many different front ends, but harder caching and query cost control. Many systems use REST or GraphQL at the edge and gRPC inside.

How to answer

Choose per boundary (public vs internal) rather than one for everything.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you version a REST API?
Monolith vs microservices?

A monolith is one deployable: simple to build, test and debug, with in-process calls and one database. Microservices split by business capability with separate deploys and data, so teams move independently and scale parts separately - at the cost of network calls, partial failures, distributed data and much more operational tooling. Start with a well-modularised monolith; split when team size or very different scaling needs make it painful.

How to answer

Avoid dogma. Tie the decision to team size and scaling differences.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is the first service you would extract?
What is a Bloom filter and where would you use it?

A bit array plus k hash functions. Insert sets k bits; lookup checks them: if any bit is 0 the item is definitely absent; if all are 1 it is maybe present. No false negatives, tunable false positives (about 10 bits per element gives about 1%). Uses: skipping disk lookups in LSM stores, a crawler's "seen URL" check, blocking lookups for non-existent keys before they hit the database, "is this username taken" pre-checks.

How to answer

Say "definitely not / maybe yes" - it is the whole idea.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you delete from a Bloom filter? (you cannot; use a counting Bloom filter)
How do you design for "what if this component fails?"

For every box ask: is there a replica (no single point of failure), how is failure detected (health checks, heartbeats, timeouts), what happens to in-flight requests (retries with backoff and jitter, idempotency), and how do we degrade (serve stale cache, queue writes, turn off a feature). Then name the blast radius and the recovery target (RTO/RPO).

How to answer

Walk the diagram left to right and answer the same four questions for each box. Repetition here is a strength.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is your RTO and RPO for the database?
What latency numbers should you know?

Rounded classics: main memory reference about 100 ns; read 1 MB sequentially from memory about 250 us; random 4 KB SSD read about 150 us; round trip in a datacenter about 0.5 ms; read 1 MB sequentially from SSD about 1 ms; disk seek about 10 ms; a cross-continent round trip about 150 ms. Cloud rules of thumb: under 1 ms inside a zone, 1-2 ms across zones, 50-150 ms across regions. Use them to justify caches and to avoid cross-region calls on the hot path.

How to answer

Do not recite the whole list; use two or three numbers to justify a decision.

Likely follow-ups
  • How many sequential cross-region calls can a 200 ms API budget afford?
What do the availability nines mean in downtime?

Per year: 99% is about 3.65 days, 99.9% about 8.8 hours, 99.99% about 53 minutes, 99.999% about 5 minutes. Serial dependencies multiply: two 99.9% services in series give about 99.8%. Redundant parallel paths raise availability. Use this to argue for removing serial hops or adding redundancy.

How to answer

Do the series multiplication out loud once; it impresses.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does an SLO differ from an SLA?

Level 4 - Advanced 10 questions

Saga vs two-phase commit for a distributed transaction?

2PC: a coordinator asks all participants to prepare, then to commit. It is atomic, but blocking: if the coordinator dies after prepare, participants hold locks. It also couples services tightly - rarely used across microservices. Saga: a chain of local transactions, each with a compensating action (refund, release stock), coordinated by an orchestrator (Temporal, Step Functions) or by events (choreography). It gives eventual consistency and requires idempotent steps and careful compensation design.

How to answer

State why 2PC is avoided across services, then design the saga steps for an order.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if a compensation itself fails?
How does leader election work, and what are fencing tokens?

Consensus systems (Raft in etcd, KRaft) elect a leader by majority vote with randomised election timeouts; a cluster of 2f+1 nodes survives f failures. Applications often take a lease (a lock with a TTL) from etcd or ZooKeeper. Danger: a leader that pauses (GC, network) may still believe it holds the lease after it expired. A fencing token is a number that increases with every new lease; the storage rejects writes carrying an older token, so the stale leader cannot corrupt data.

How to answer

Majority vote, then the pause problem, then fencing. Networking analogy: VRRP made split-brain safe by needing a majority.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not use a Redis lock for this?
Retries: how do you avoid a retry storm?

Retries multiply load exactly when a dependency is struggling. Use exponential backoff with jitter, a retry budget (for example at most 10% extra load), retry only idempotent operations, set deadlines that propagate down the call chain, and put circuit breakers in front of failing dependencies so callers fail fast. Retry at one layer only; retries at every layer multiply (3 layers x 3 retries = 27 calls).

How to answer

Give the multiplication example. It makes the danger obvious.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is a bulkhead?
How do you do back-of-envelope storage and bandwidth?

Storage = writes per day x bytes per write x retention days x replication factor. Example: 100M posts/day x 1 KB = 100 GB/day; x 365 = about 36 TB/year; x 3 replicas = about 110 TB. Bandwidth = QPS x payload: 10k reads/s x 100 KB images = 1 GB/s egress, which says "put images on a CDN". Always state assumptions and round aggressively.

How to answer

Show the formula, plug in round numbers, then say which design decision the result drives.

Likely follow-ups
  • How does media change the estimate? (it dominates; store it in object storage, metadata in the DB)
Single region vs multi-region active-active?

Single region (multi-AZ) is simpler and survives a zone loss; a region outage means downtime. Active-passive keeps a warm standby region with async replication: lower cost, but failover takes minutes and may lose recent writes. Active-active serves from several regions: lowest latency for global users and fastest failover, but writes in two regions can conflict, so you partition users by home region or use conflict-free data types. Choose from RTO/RPO, latency and budget.

How to answer

Use the trade-off sentence and say what data would be home-region pinned.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you route users to the nearest healthy region? (GeoDNS or anycast plus health checks)
Optimistic vs pessimistic locking?

Pessimistic: lock the row before changing it (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE), others wait. Safe under high contention, but locks hold connections and can deadlock. Optimistic: read a version number, write with UPDATE ... WHERE id = ? AND version = ?; if zero rows change, someone else won, so retry or fail. Great under low contention, wasteful under heavy contention (many retries). For seat booking: optimistic or a short-TTL hold record; for a hot flash-sale counter, an atomic decrement.

How to answer

Contention level decides. Show the SQL for each.

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you avoid two users booking the same seat?
How do you generate unique IDs across many servers?

Options: database auto-increment (simple, a bottleneck); UUIDv4 (no coordination, random so bad for B-tree locality); UUIDv7 or ULID (time-ordered, sortable); Snowflake-style 64-bit IDs: timestamp bits + machine ID + per-millisecond sequence, roughly time ordered and generated locally. Risks: clock going backwards (refuse or wait), machine ID assignment (from config or a coordinator). Range allocation (each server leases a block of 1,000 IDs from a DB) is another simple option.

How to answer

Compare on ordering, coordination and size; pick Snowflake for sortable 64-bit IDs.

Likely follow-ups
  • What happens with clock skew?
How do you migrate a database with zero downtime?

Expand and contract: (1) add the new schema or store alongside the old; (2) dual-write (or CDC from old to new); (3) backfill historical data; (4) verify with shadow reads comparing results; (5) switch reads gradually behind a flag; (6) stop writing to the old one; (7) remove it. Every step is reversible until the last. Schema changes follow the same rule: add columns as nullable, deploy code that writes both, then drop old columns later.

How to answer

Number the steps and stress reversibility.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you handle writes during the backfill?
Push vs pull for a news feed (fan-out on write vs read)?

Fan-out on write (push): when a user posts, write the post ID into every follower's feed cache; reads are fast, writes are expensive and terrible for celebrities with millions of followers. Fan-out on read (pull): build the feed at read time from followed users' posts; cheap writes, slow reads. Hybrid: push for normal users, pull for celebrities and merge at read time. Feeds are eventually consistent, which is fine.

How to answer

Name the celebrity problem early; it is the reason the hybrid exists.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you rank the feed?
What do you monitor, and how do you talk about it without hand-waving?

Name signals per component. Services: the four golden signals - latency (p50/p99), traffic, errors, saturation. Queues: lag in seconds. Databases: replication lag, connections, slow queries. Business: orders per minute, payment success rate. Alert on symptoms tied to SLOs (for example "checkout error rate above 1% for 5 minutes"), not on every CPU spike. Use metrics for trends, logs for detail, traces to follow one request across services.

How to answer

Name the actual metric and threshold for two components in your design. That is the opposite of hand-waving.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is p99 and why not the average?

Level 5 - Interview-ready 4 questions

How much detail is expected at SDE-1 vs SDE-2?

SDE-1: a clear, working design with sensible components, an API and data model, awareness of scale, and good answers when prompted - the interviewer may guide you. SDE-2: you drive the whole round, quantify with estimates, pick technologies with reasons and costs, go deep on one or two hard parts (hot keys, consistency, failure), and raise failure modes and monitoring without being asked. In both, clarity beats breadth.

How to answer

Give a concrete example: for a URL shortener, SDE-1 explains the redirect path; SDE-2 also covers key generation collisions, cache hit ratio and abuse.

Likely follow-ups
  • What is the most common reason strong SDE-1 candidates miss SDE-2?
How do you handle a question you do not know?

Say so briefly, then reason from first principles out loud: state what you do know, what the system must guarantee, and propose a design with stated assumptions. Ask a focused question if a fact matters ("is ordering per user enough?"). Interviewers score how you think under uncertainty; bluffing a wrong fact scores worse than "I have not used X, but here is how I would reason about it".

How to answer

Have a sentence ready: "I have not worked with that directly; let me reason it through."

Likely follow-ups
  • Give an example of reasoning from first principles about a technology you have not used.
What are the common reasons people fail system design rounds?

Jumping into components before requirements; no numbers; drawing boxes without saying what data flows and why; going deep on internals the interviewer did not ask about; ignoring failure and consistency; picking technology by brand without trade-offs; running out of time before the deep dive; and not listening to hints. Fix: a fixed framework, a timer in your head, and the trade-off sentence for every choice.

How to answer

Turn each failure into a habit you will follow; it shows self-awareness.

Likely follow-ups
  • How do you recover if you realise mid-way your design is wrong?
How would you translate your networking background into SDE answers?

Map the ideas explicitly: "Kafka key partitioning is ECMP flow hashing", "consumer lag is queue depth", "backpressure is TCP flow control", "Kubernetes reconciliation is a routing daemon programming the FIB", "a readiness probe is pulling a member from an ECMP group". Then translate stories: "I debugged packet drops caused by buffer exhaustion" becomes "I diagnosed a backpressure and queue-depth problem under burst load, found the bottleneck stage, and fixed it with flow control and capacity changes".

How to answer

Lead with confidence: these are the same problems at larger scale. Have three translated stories ready.

Likely follow-ups
  • What do you still need to learn coming from that background?

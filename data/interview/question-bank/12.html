Behavioural and "about you" questions (12)

Amazon asks one or two Leadership Principle questions in every round, and other companies weave similar questions in. Technical people often under-prepare these. The official Leadership Principles page lists all of them.

Level 2 - Beginner 1 question

How do you structure a behavioural answer?

Use STAR: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you owned), Action (what you did - most of the time goes here, with technical detail), Result (a measurable outcome, plus what you learned). Keep it to about two minutes. Say "I", not "we", for your part. Prepare 8-10 flexible stories that each cover several principles.

How to answer

Practise the stories out loud with a timer. Write one number into every Result.

Likely follow-ups
  • What would you do differently next time?

Level 3 - Intermediate 9 questions

Tell me about a time you dove deep into a hard bug. (Dive Deep)

Model shape: "Our line cards dropped packets only under bursty traffic (S). I owned finding the cause before a customer deadline (T). I added counters at each queue stage, reproduced the burst in the lab, and found descriptor-ring exhaustion because the driver refilled buffers in a low-priority context; I moved refill into the NAPI poll and sized the ring from measured burst depth (A). Drops went to zero at 2x the original burst rate and I added an alarm on ring occupancy (R)." Translate terms: "queue depth", "backpressure", "bottleneck stage".

How to answer

Kernel and driver stories are excellent here - just explain them in plain words and end with the measurable result.

Likely follow-ups
  • How did you know you had the root cause and not a symptom?
Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager or team. (Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit)

Show that you disagreed with data, respectfully, and then committed. Shape: the proposal, your concern with evidence (a measurement, a prototype), how you raised it (one-on-one, a short doc), the decision, and what you did after - including fully supporting the decision if it went the other way, and what the outcome taught you.

How to answer

Avoid stories where you were simply right and others were wrong. The "commit" part matters as much as the "disagree".

Likely follow-ups
  • What if the decision turned out badly?
Tell me about a time you took ownership beyond your role. (Ownership)

Pick a story where something was failing between teams and you fixed it end to end: for example a flaky test lab no one owned, which you stabilised, documented and handed over with monitoring. Show the impact on others (time saved, incidents avoided) and how you made it last beyond you.

How to answer

Ownership = long-term thinking plus acting on things outside your job description.

Likely follow-ups
  • How did you balance it with your own deliverables?
Tell me about delivering under a tight deadline. (Deliver Results, Bias for Action)

Show prioritisation: what you cut, what you kept, how you reduced risk (feature flag, phased rollout), how you communicated the trade-off early, and the result on the date. Mention a calculated risk you took and why it was reversible.

How to answer

Interviewers check judgement, not heroics. Reversible decisions fast, irreversible ones carefully.

Likely follow-ups
  • What did you not ship, and how did you decide?
Tell me about a time you simplified something complex. (Invent and Simplify)

Example shape: replacing a hand-maintained set of per-device scripts with one declarative config and a reconciler that applies it (the same idea as Kubernetes). Quantify: fewer steps, fewer errors, faster onboarding.

How to answer

Show the before and after clearly. A simple diagram in words helps.

Likely follow-ups
  • What resistance did you face?
Tell me about a failure and what you learned. (Learn and Be Curious)

Choose a real failure with real impact, own your part without blaming others, explain the technical cause briefly, and focus on the change you made afterwards (a test, an alert, a process) and evidence that it worked.

How to answer

The learning must be specific and lasting. "I learned to be careful" is weak; "I added a pre-merge check that has since caught X" is strong.

Likely follow-ups
  • Has it happened again?
Tell me about a decision driven by the customer. (Customer Obsession)

Show that you started from the customer's real problem (a support ticket, field data) rather than the technical preference, chose a solution that helped them even if it was less elegant, and measured the customer outcome.

How to answer

For infra or systems roles, your "customer" may be another team or an operator. Say that explicitly.

Likely follow-ups
  • How did you find out what the customer really needed?
Tell me about raising the quality bar. (Insist on the Highest Standards)

Example: introducing code review checklists, fuzzing for a parser, or performance regression tests in CI after a production slowdown. Show the standard, the pushback, and the measurable drop in defects.

How to answer

Pair the higher standard with a mechanism that makes it stick.

Likely follow-ups
  • How did you get others to adopt it?
Tell me about earning the trust of a sceptical team. (Earn Trust)

Show listening first, admitting what you did not know, delivering small promises reliably, and sharing credit. A good shape is joining a team in an unfamiliar area (like moving from networking to services) and earning trust by fixing their pain points.

How to answer

Specific actions over adjectives.

Likely follow-ups
  • How did you know trust was earned?

Level 4 - Advanced 2 questions

How do you turn a pipeline or design project into a strong STAR story?

Situation: the business need ("orders took 15 minutes to reach the warehouse"). Task: what you owned ("design and build the event pipeline"). Action: the key decisions with trade-offs (outbox instead of dual writes, partition key choice, idempotent consumers, lag alerts). Result: numbers (latency dropped to seconds, zero lost orders in N months) and what you would change. This one story also prepares you for system design follow-ups.

How to answer

Practise the same project at three lengths: 30 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes.

Likely follow-ups
  • What was the hardest trade-off?
"You don't have web-scale experience." How do you answer?

"My background is systems and networking, so I reason about throughput, latency, failure detection, flow control and state consistency every day - the same problems at a larger scale. For example, Kafka's key partitioning is ECMP flow hashing, and Kubernetes' control loop works like a routing daemon programming the FIB. I have been building with Docker, Kubernetes and Kafka to apply that, for example [your project]." Then give one translated story.

How to answer

Confident, short, then evidence. Never apologise for your background.

Likely follow-ups
  • What was the hardest new concept for you?

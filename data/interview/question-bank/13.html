Scenario drills: weak vs strong answers

Scenario questions describe a production problem and ask what you would do. There is no single right answer; the interviewer is grading your method. The pattern in every strong answer below: detect and scope, mitigate first, diagnose with evidence, fix the root cause, prevent it next time. For 36 more drills, go to Production Scenario Drills.

Scenario: p99 latency doubled right after a deploy

Checkout p99 went from 300 ms to 650 ms ten minutes after a release. Error rate is normal. Traffic is normal.

Weak answer: "I would look at the code changes and try to optimise the slow function."

Strong answer: "The deploy is the prime suspect, so I roll back first - it is cheap and reversible - and watch p99 recover, which also confirms the cause. Then I compare the two versions: traces for the slowest requests to see which span grew, new downstream calls, a changed query plan, a smaller connection pool, or CPU throttling from new resource limits. I reproduce in staging with a load test, fix, and redeploy as a canary gated on p99. Prevention: automated canary analysis on latency, not only on errors."

Likely follow-ups
  • What if rolling back is impossible because of a schema migration?
  • Which percentile would you gate the canary on, and why?

Scenario: consumer lag keeps rising during a sale

The order-events consumer group has 12 partitions and 6 pods. Lag grew from near zero to 2 million records in 20 minutes during a sale.

Weak answer: "Add more consumers until it catches up."

Strong answer: "First I check if lag grows on all partitions or one. All partitions: consumers are too slow for the load, so I scale to 12 pods (the maximum useful, one per partition), then look at per-record processing time - usually the database sink - and batch writes. One partition: a hot key, so scaling will not help; I look at the key distribution. I also check for rebalance loops in the consumer logs. I convert lag to seconds to judge urgency against retention and the freshness SLO. Prevention: KEDA autoscaling on lag capped at the partition count, load tests at sale volume, and more partitions planned before the next sale."

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not add partitions right now?
  • How would you prioritise payment events over analytics?

Scenario: pods are OOMKilled only at night

A reporting service restarts with exit code 137 every night around 2 a.m. Daytime is fine.

Weak answer: "Double the memory limit."

Strong answer: "137 with OOMKilled in `kubectl describe` means the container crossed its memory limit. Night-only points to a scheduled workload: a batch report loading a whole dataset into memory, or a cache warm-up. I check memory graphs per pod against the cron schedule and look for a heap that never drops (a leak) versus a spike (one big job). Short term I raise the limit to stop the restarts. Real fix: stream the report in pages, or move it to a Job with its own resources. I also check that the runtime respects the container limit, for example JVM heap settings."

Likely follow-ups
  • What is the difference between the kernel OOM kill and a kubelet eviction?

Scenario: database CPU hits 100% after the cache cluster restarts

Redis was restarted for maintenance. Seconds later the primary database is at 100% CPU and API errors spike.

Weak answer: "Scale the database up."

Strong answer: "This is a cache stampede: an empty cache sends every read to the database. Mitigate by shedding load (rate-limit at the gateway, turn off expensive features) and letting the cache warm. The real fixes: request coalescing so one request per key refills while others wait, jittered TTLs, warming the hottest keys before switching traffic, and restarting cache nodes one at a time instead of all together. Next time maintenance is a rolling restart with a warm-up step in the runbook."

Likely follow-ups
  • How would you find the hottest keys to pre-warm?

Scenario: customers report being charged twice

Support has 40 tickets of double charges since yesterday. The payment service retries calls to the processor on timeout.

Weak answer: "Turn off retries."

Strong answer: "Timeouts do not mean failure; the processor may have charged and the response was lost, so our retry charged again. First, refund the affected customers and pause automatic retries for timed-out calls. Then confirm by matching the duplicate charges to timeout logs. Fix: send an idempotency key per payment attempt that is reused on every retry, so the processor returns the original result; store it with a unique constraint on our side too. Add a reconciliation job that compares our ledger with processor reports daily and alerts on mismatches."

Likely follow-ups
  • Where exactly is the idempotency key generated, and why there?

Scenario: an availability zone goes down

One of three zones in your region is unreachable. Some requests are failing.

Weak answer: "Wait for the cloud provider to fix it."

Strong answer: "Check that load balancers have stopped sending traffic to the dead zone (health checks), that the remaining zones have capacity (we should run with N+1 headroom so two zones can carry the load), and that stateful systems failed over: the database replica in another zone was promoted, Kafka partitions elected new leaders from in-sync replicas in other zones (rack awareness makes that possible), and Kubernetes rescheduled pods - with pods on the dead nodes evicted after the default five minutes. Then I watch for capacity alarms and pending pods. Afterwards: run zone-failure game days."

Likely follow-ups
  • How do topology spread constraints help here?

Scenario: the Postgres disk is filling and nobody wrote more data

Disk usage on the primary grows 20 GB an hour. Table sizes are flat.

Weak answer: "Delete old logs and add a bigger disk."

Strong answer: "Flat tables but growing disk suggests WAL retention. A common cause is a logical replication slot that nobody is reading, for example a stopped Debezium connector: Postgres keeps all WAL the slot has not confirmed. I check `pg_replication_slots` for an inactive slot with a large retained size. Mitigate by restarting the connector so it consumes, or dropping the slot if it is abandoned (then re-snapshot that pipeline). Prevent with an alert on retained WAL per slot and a limit on maximum slot WAL size."

Likely follow-ups
  • What does the connector lose if you drop its slot?

Scenario: a retry storm takes down a dependency

The inventory service slowed down for 30 seconds, and now it is completely down even though the original slowness is gone.

Weak answer: "Restart the inventory service."

Strong answer: "Callers retried their timed-out requests, multiplying load, so inventory cannot recover - a feedback loop. Break the loop first: shed load at the gateway or open circuit breakers in callers so they fail fast, let inventory recover, then ramp traffic back. Fix: exponential backoff with jitter, retry budgets, retries at only one layer, deadlines that propagate, and circuit breakers. The AWS Builders' Library article on timeouts, retries and backoff describes this exact pattern."

Likely follow-ups
  • Why does jitter matter, not only backoff?

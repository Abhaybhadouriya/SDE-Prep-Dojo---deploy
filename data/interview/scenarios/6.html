Kafka and pipeline drills (F1 - F10)

Kafka questions are very common at Walmart, Uber, Flipkart and Amazon-style loops, and they are almost always scenario questions. Background: Consumers and groups, Replication and ISR, Delivery semantics, Operations and tuning, Retries and DLQ, Pipeline failure drills.

Before the drills, one picture. Consumer lag is the gap between the newest offset in a partition and the offset the group has committed. Look at lag per partition: an even lag and a single hot partition need completely different fixes.

Scenario F1: consumer lag keeps growing during a big sale

It is the first hour of a flash sale. The order-events consumer group is two million messages behind and the number is still rising. Order confirmation emails are delayed by 40 minutes.

Kafka lag, scaling level: SDE-1 and SDE-2 commonly reported at: Amazon, Walmart, Flipkart

Weak answer: "Add more consumers." If the topic has 12 partitions and you already run 12 consumers, extra consumers sit idle. If one partition is hot, more consumers change nothing.

Strong answer:

  1. Look at lag per partition with kafka-consumer-groups.sh --describe.
  2. Case A: lag is even across partitions. The consumers are simply too slow. If there are fewer consumers than partitions, scale consumers up to the partition count. Then make each consumer faster: batch writes to the database, use async I/O, cut per-message network calls, raise max.poll.records only if processing keeps up.
  3. Case B: one partition is far behind. A hot key (one giant seller, one popular product) is sending most messages to one partition. Options: add a sub-key or salt to spread that key if ordering per seller is not strictly needed; process that partition with internal parallelism keyed by order ID; or move the hot tenant to its own topic.
  4. Check the downstream. Often the consumer is slow because the DB or an external API it calls is slow. Lag is a symptom, the database may be the disease.
  5. Protect users. Prioritise: put confirmation emails on their own topic and consumer group so analytics lag does not delay them.
  6. Prevent. Size partitions for peak (not average), load test before the sale, and alert on lag growth rate, not just a fixed number.
$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --describe --group email-sender
GROUP         TOPIC         PARTITION  CURRENT-OFFSET  LOG-END-OFFSET  LAG      CONSUMER-ID
email-sender  order-events  0          9120334         9120454         120      c-1-3f2a
email-sender  order-events  1          4410021         6250021         1840000  c-2-91bc
email-sender  order-events  2          8877410         8877505         95       c-3-77de

From your world

Lag is queue depth. Even lag across partitions is like all egress queues filling because the link is too slow (add capacity). Lag on one partition is like one flow hashed onto one ECMP member and saturating it while the others are idle (fix the hash).

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not just add partitions now? (The key-to-partition mapping changes, which breaks per-key ordering during the transition; new partitions also start empty, so existing lag does not move.)
  • What is the maximum useful number of consumers in a group? (The number of partitions. KIP-932 share groups in Kafka 4.x remove this limit for queue-style work, at the cost of ordering.)
  • How would you autoscale consumers on Kubernetes? (KEDA scaler on lag, capped at the partition count.)

Scenario F2: the consumer group keeps rebalancing every few minutes

Logs show "Attempt to heartbeat failed since group is rebalancing" again and again. Throughput drops to almost zero during each rebalance, and some messages are processed twice.

Kafka rebalancing level: SDE-1 and SDE-2 commonly reported at: Walmart, Uber

Weak answer: "Increase the session timeout to an hour." Then a truly dead consumer would hold its partitions for an hour.

Strong answer: find out why members keep leaving the group. There are three usual reasons.

  1. Processing is too slow between polls. If one batch takes longer than max.poll.interval.ms (default 5 minutes), the consumer is kicked out. Fix: lower max.poll.records (default 500), make processing faster, or move slow work off the poll loop carefully.
  2. Heartbeats are missed. Long GC pauses or CPU throttling (scenario K3) can make a consumer miss heartbeats for longer than session.timeout.ms (45 s default).
  3. Pods restart often. Every Kubernetes rolling deploy or crash makes members leave and join. Fix: static membership (group.instance.id set to the pod name), so a quick restart does not trigger a rebalance; and the cooperative assignor or the new consumer group protocol (KIP-848, GA in Kafka 4.0, group.protocol=consumer), which moves only the partitions that must move instead of stopping everyone.
  4. Make duplicates harmless. Rebalances cause re-delivery under at-least-once, so the handler must be idempotent.
Likely follow-ups
  • What is the difference between eager and cooperative rebalancing?
  • Walmart described building a messaging proxy partly because rebalances caused lag at its scale. Why can a proxy help? (Kafka consumers stay few and stable; services receive pushed messages.)

Scenario F3: after a restart, a consumer group reprocessed a whole week of data

A billing consumer was turned off for 9 days during a migration. When it came back, it started from the beginning of the topic and sent duplicate invoices.

Kafka offsets level: SDE-2 commonly reported at: any company team: payments teams

Weak answer: "Kafka lost our offsets, it is unreliable." Kafka did exactly what it was configured to do.

Strong answer:

  1. Explain the mechanism. Committed offsets live in the __consumer_offsets topic. After a group has been empty for offsets.retention.minutes (7 days by default) its offsets are deleted. When the group returns with no offset, the consumer uses auto.offset.reset, which here was set to earliest.
  2. Mitigate. Stop the consumer, stop the invoice sender, and work out which invoices were duplicates using their business IDs.
  3. Reset to the right place. Use the offset reset tool to a time just after the last known good processing.
  4. Prevent. Make the sink idempotent: an invoice has a unique key (order ID plus billing period), and the database rejects a second insert. Then any replay is safe. Also alert when a critical group has no active members.
$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --group billing \
    --topic invoices --reset-offsets --to-datetime 2026-11-12T08:00:00.000 --dry-run
$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --group billing \
    --topic invoices --reset-offsets --to-datetime 2026-11-12T08:00:00.000 --execute
Likely follow-ups
  • The opposite bug: a new group "sees nothing". Why? (Default auto.offset.reset=latest skips existing data.)
  • When is replaying from the beginning a feature, not a bug? (Rebuilding a search index or a new read model.)

Scenario F4: producers time out, but only from inside Docker or Kubernetes

A producer on a laptop works fine. The same producer in a container connects to the bootstrap server but then fails with timeouts like "Expiring records ... ms has passed since batch creation".

Kafka networking, advertised listeners level: SDE-1 and SDE-2 commonly reported at: any company team: Kafka on Kubernetes teams

Weak answer: "Increase request.timeout.ms." The client is talking to the wrong address; waiting longer will not help.

Strong answer: the bootstrap server is only the first contact. The client asks for metadata and gets back the address of the leader broker for each partition, taken from that broker's advertised.listeners. If a broker advertises localhost:9092 or a host name only resolvable on the host network, the container connects to the bootstrap fine and then cannot reach the leader.

  1. Check what brokers advertise. Look at the metadata the client receives (debug logs, or kcat -L).
  2. Fix the listeners. Configure separate listeners for inside traffic (service DNS names) and outside traffic (a reachable host and port per broker).
  3. On Kubernetes clients outside the cluster need per-broker addresses (a NodePort or LoadBalancer for each broker); a single load-balanced address does not work because the client must reach the specific leader. Strimzi's external listeners do this for you.
  4. Other causes to rule out: the ISR is below min.insync.replicas (errors say NotEnoughReplicas), the producer buffer is full (send() blocks for max.block.ms), TLS or SASL errors.
# server.properties (listener part only): one listener for inside the Docker network, one for the host
listeners=INTERNAL://0.0.0.0:19092,EXTERNAL://0.0.0.0:9092,CONTROLLER://0.0.0.0:9093
advertised.listeners=INTERNAL://kafka:19092,EXTERNAL://localhost:9092
listener.security.protocol.map=INTERNAL:PLAINTEXT,EXTERNAL:PLAINTEXT,CONTROLLER:PLAINTEXT
inter.broker.listener.name=INTERNAL
# KRaft also needs: controller.listener.names=CONTROLLER on every node (even broker-only ones).
# The CONTROLLER://...:9093 entry in listeners is only for a combined broker+controller node;
# never advertise the controller listener to clients.
controller.listener.names=CONTROLLER
Likely follow-ups
  • Why can't a TCP load balancer sit in front of all brokers like a web service? (Partition leadership is per broker; the client must talk to the leader.)
  • What does the producer do with delivery.timeout.ms (default 2 minutes)? (The total time it keeps retrying a record before giving up.)

Scenario F5: a broker died and some acknowledged messages are gone

One broker's disk failed. After recovery, a few thousand order events that producers had received acknowledgements for are missing.

Kafka durability level: SDE-1 and SDE-2 commonly reported at: Amazon, Walmart, PhonePe

Weak answer: "Kafka writes to disk, so it should never lose data. Maybe a bug." Durability in Kafka comes from configuration, not magic.

Strong answer: check the four durability settings, because the out-of-the-box defaults are not durable.

  1. Replication factor. The broker default default.replication.factor is 1. With one copy, a dead disk is lost data.
  2. acks. With acks=1 the leader acknowledges after writing to its own log (page cache, no fsync), before followers copy it. If the leader dies right then, the record is gone. The modern producer default is acks=all, but older or hand-tuned clients may still use 1.
  3. min.insync.replicas. Default 1. Even with acks=all, if the ISR shrank to only the leader, "all" means "one".
  4. Unclean leader election. If unclean.leader.election.enable=true, an out-of-date replica may become leader and the newer records are truncated.
  5. Recommend the standard recipe: replication factor 3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all, idempotent producer, unclean election off, replicas spread across racks or zones. That survives one broker loss with no loss of acknowledged data and still accepts writes.
$ kafka-topics.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --describe --topic orders
Topic: orders  PartitionCount: 12  ReplicationFactor: 1  Configs: min.insync.replicas=1
$ kafka-topics.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --describe --under-replicated-partitions
Likely follow-ups
  • With RF 3 and min ISR 2, what happens when two brokers are down? (Writes with acks=all fail with NotEnoughReplicas: the partition chooses consistency over availability. Reads still work.)
  • Why does Kafka not fsync every message? (It relies on replication across machines for durability and on the OS page cache for speed.)

Scenario F6: one bad message blocks a whole partition (poison pill)

A consumer crashes on one malformed event, restarts, reads the same event, and crashes again. Lag on that partition grows forever; the other partitions are fine.

Kafka retries, DLQ level: SDE-2 commonly reported at: Walmart, Uber

Weak answer: "Catch the exception and skip it." Silently skipping can lose a real order.

Strong answer: separate transient errors (the DB is briefly down: retry) from permanent ones (the message can never be parsed: park it).

  1. Mitigate. Move the one bad offset to a dead-letter topic by hand and commit past it, so the partition flows again.
  2. Build the pattern. Retry a few times in place with back-off for transient errors. If it still fails, publish the record (with error details in headers) to a retry topic with a delay, and after the last retry to a dead-letter queue (DLQ). Uber's engineering blog describes this exact chain of retry topics and a DLQ.
  3. Handle the DLQ. Alert on DLQ depth, give operators a tool to inspect, fix and replay messages back to the main topic.
  4. Stop bad data at the door. Use a Schema Registry with compatibility rules so producers cannot publish data consumers cannot read.
Likely follow-ups
  • What happens to ordering when a message goes to a retry topic? (Later messages for the same key may be processed first. If order per key matters, park the whole key until the bad one is fixed.)
  • How does Kafka Connect handle this? (errors.tolerance=all plus errors.deadletterqueue.topic.name for sinks.)

Scenario F7: customers are charged twice after a consumer rebalance

A payment consumer reads payment-requested events and calls the payment gateway. After a deploy, a few customers were charged twice.

Kafka exactly-once vs idempotency level: SDE-2 commonly reported at: Amazon, Walmart, PhonePe, Razorpay

Weak answer: "Turn on Kafka exactly-once and it is solved." Kafka transactions cover read-from-Kafka, process, write-to-Kafka. They do not cover a call to an external payment gateway.

Strong answer:

  1. Explain why it happened. The consumer called the gateway, then the pod was stopped before it committed the offset. The new owner of the partition read the same event again: normal at-least-once behaviour.
  2. Make the side effect idempotent. Send an idempotency key (the payment ID from the event) to the gateway; most payment providers support it, so a repeated request returns the first result. Also record "payment ID processed" in your own DB with a unique constraint, in the same transaction as the status update.
  3. Reconcile. Run a reconciliation job comparing your ledger with the provider's settlement report, and refund the doubles.
  4. Prevent. Graceful shutdown that commits processed offsets on partition revocation, and a rule in design reviews: "every consumer with side effects must be idempotent".
-- the unique key turns a replay into a harmless no-op
INSERT INTO processed_payments (payment_id, status, processed_at)
VALUES (:paymentId, 'CHARGED', now())
ON CONFLICT (payment_id) DO NOTHING;
Likely follow-ups
  • What does the idempotent producer guarantee, exactly? (No duplicates from the producer's own retries within one session, per partition. Not app-level resends.)
  • See the payment system design for the ledger and reconciliation parts.

Scenario F8: events for one order arrive out of order

The shipping service sometimes receives OrderCancelled before OrderCreated for the same order, and ships a cancelled order. This began after someone increased the topic from 6 to 12 partitions.

Kafka ordering, keys level: SDE-2 commonly reported at: Walmart

Weak answer: "Use one partition so everything is ordered." That kills parallelism and throughput for the whole topic.

Strong answer: Kafka only orders messages within a partition. Related events must share a key.

  1. Check the key. Are all events for one order keyed by orderId? A null or random key spreads them across partitions.
  2. Explain the partition change. The default partitioner picks hash(key) % partitionCount. Changing the count from 6 to 12 moves many keys to new partitions. Old events for an order sit in the old partition, new ones go to the new partition, and two consumers process them in parallel.
  3. Check producer retries. Without idempotence, a retried batch can land after a later one. With enable.idempotence=true (the default today) order is kept with up to 5 in-flight requests.
  4. Make consumers defensive. Carry a version or sequence number per order and ignore events older than the stored state (a simple state machine: you cannot ship a cancelled order).
  5. Prevent. Choose partitions for future peak up front; if you must grow, create a new topic and migrate in a controlled way.
Likely follow-ups
  • How would you guarantee total order across all orders? (Only with one partition, which rarely makes sense; ask whether the business really needs it.)
  • How does a CDC pipeline keep per-row order? (Key by primary key.)

Scenario F9: Kafka brokers are running out of disk

An alert says broker disks are at 88% and rising. UnderReplicatedPartitions has started to go above zero.

Kafka operations level: SDE-2 commonly reported at: any company team: platform teams

Weak answer: "Delete some log files on the broker." Deleting segment files by hand can corrupt the broker's view of its log.

Strong answer:

  1. Find which topics grew. kafka-log-dirs.sh --describe shows size per partition. Often one topic got a traffic spike or a producer bug.
  2. Mitigate using Kafka's own tools. Lower retention.ms or set retention.bytes on the offending topic with kafka-configs.sh; Kafka then deletes whole old segments safely. Check that no consumer still needs that data.
  3. Explain URP. When a broker is overloaded or its disk is slow, followers fall behind and leave the ISR; under-replicated partitions are a durability risk, so treat them as urgent.
  4. Longer term. Add brokers and rebalance partitions (Cruise Control), enable compression on producers, or use tiered storage (KIP-405) so older segments move to object storage.
  5. Prevent. Capacity alerts on disk with time-to-full, and producer quotas per client so one buggy service cannot fill the cluster.
$ kafka-configs.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --alter \
    --entity-type topics --entity-name clickstream --add-config retention.ms=86400000
Completed updating config for topic clickstream.
Likely follow-ups
  • Which broker metrics would you put on the first dashboard? (Under-replicated and offline partitions, active controller count, request handler idle %, bytes in/out, p99 produce latency, disk usage.)
  • How does a compacted topic differ in retention? (Keeps the latest value per key instead of deleting by time.)

Scenario F10: the production database disk fills up because of the CDC pipeline

A Postgres database's disk grows by 50 GB a day although table sizes are flat. A Debezium connector that streams changes to Kafka was paused two weeks ago.

Kafka CDC, Debezium level: SDE-2 commonly reported at: any company team: data platform teams

Weak answer: "Add a bigger disk." The disk will fill again, and you are paying to keep WAL nobody reads.

Strong answer: Debezium reads Postgres through a logical replication slot. A slot promises the database will keep all WAL (write-ahead log) the reader has not confirmed. A paused or abandoned connector never confirms, so WAL piles up until the disk is full, which would take the primary database down.

  1. Confirm. Query pg_replication_slots and look at how much WAL each slot retains.
  2. Mitigate. Resume the connector so it catches up, or if it is truly abandoned, drop the slot (and plan a re-snapshot later).
  3. Prevent. Alert on retained WAL per slot, set max_slot_wal_keep_size as a safety limit (it invalidates the slot instead of killing the DB), and treat connectors as production services with owners.
SELECT slot_name, active,
       pg_size_pretty(pg_wal_lsn_diff(pg_current_wal_lsn(), restart_lsn)) AS retained_wal
FROM pg_replication_slots;
Likely follow-ups
  • Why use CDC and the outbox pattern instead of writing to the DB and Kafka from the app? (The dual-write problem; see drill H5.)
  • See the CDC pipeline design for the full picture.

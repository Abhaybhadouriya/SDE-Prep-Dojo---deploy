The story: the doctor, not the guesser

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine your tummy hurts and you visit two doctors. The first one says, straight away, "Take this medicine." Maybe it helps, maybe not. The second one asks: "Where does it hurt? Since when? Did you eat something new?" She presses gently in a few places, looks at a thermometer, and only then says what is wrong. If it is serious she first gives you something so the pain stops, and then she fixes the real problem. Before you leave she tells you how to not get sick again. Interviewers want you to be the second doctor. Asking, checking, stopping the pain, fixing, and preventing. Every drill on this page practises exactly that.

A scenario question (also called a troubleshooting question or a production incident drill) gives you a symptom: "the consumer lag keeps growing", "pods are stuck in Pending", "users are charged twice". There is no single right answer. The interviewer is checking how you think: do you collect evidence before changing things, do you protect users first, do you know the real tools, and do you learn from the incident?

The good news for you: this is the part of SDE interviews where a systems and networking engineer is often stronger than a pure web developer. You have already chased packet drops, buffer exhaustion, flapping links and memory leaks in C. The tools have new names here (kubectl instead of ethtool, consumer lag instead of queue depth), but the method is the same.

From your world

Think of how you debug a link that drops packets. You do not reboot the router first. You check counters (ip -s link, interface errors), narrow it to one port or one VLAN, look at what changed (a config push? a new optic?), apply a workaround (shift traffic to the redundant path), then find the root cause and add an alarm. That is the exact six-step loop on this page. Consumer lag is queue depth. Kubernetes readiness is a health check before a route is advertised. A retry storm is a broadcast storm made of HTTP requests.

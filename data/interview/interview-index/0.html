The story: an interview loop is a video game

Explain like I'm 5

Think of a video game with a few levels. First there is a small gate where a robot checks you can jump (the online test). Then a friendly guard asks you a few puzzles (the phone screen). Then you reach the big castle, where four or five different teachers each give you one challenge: a puzzle, a drawing of a city, a "build me a toy" task, and a "tell me about a time you were brave" chat. At the end, all the teachers sit at one table and decide together if you win. You do not need to be perfect in every room. You need to show each teacher the one thing they came to check.

That last sentence is the whole secret of interview prep. Each round has a rubric: a short list of signals the interviewer must write down. A coding round looks for problem solving and clean code. A design round looks for clear requirements, sensible numbers and trade-offs. A behavioural round looks for ownership and evidence. If you know the rubric, you know what to say.

This page is the map for the whole site. It explains the loops at Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Flipkart, Walmart and Uber as candidates have reported them, shows which track on this site feeds which round, and gives you ready-to-adapt stories for a person coming from kernel, hardware or networking work. Formats change by team, location and year, so treat every company description as "commonly reported", never "always".

From your world

An interview loop is like a test plan for a line card. Each test case (round) checks one feature. A failure in one case does not always fail the board, but a failure in a critical case (for example, the bar raiser at Amazon) can. Your job is to pass each test case cleanly, with logs (evidence) the reviewer can read. Where this breaks: unlike a test plan, interviewers also judge how you communicate while you work, not only the final output.

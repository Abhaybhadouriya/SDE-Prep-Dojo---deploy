STAR stories for a systems engineer pivoting

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. It is the expected shape for every "tell me about a time" answer at Amazon, and a good default everywhere else. The rule of thumb many coaches give: keep Situation and Task short, spend most of the time on your Action, and end with a Result that has a number.

Worked example: Dive Deep (a kernel story)

"Tell me about the hardest bug you have debugged."

Situation. On our line-card software, customers saw rare packet drops on 100G ports under heavy traffic. It happened about once a week in the field, never in the lab.

Weak version. "We had a packet drop issue. We looked at a lot of logs and eventually found it was a driver bug and fixed it. It was very hard." (No "I", no steps, no number, no lesson.)

Task. I owned the driver and was asked to find the root cause before the next release, in three weeks.

Action. First I added per-queue counters and a ring-buffer trace of descriptor head and tail indices, because the existing logs only showed totals. I wrote a traffic script that reproduced the customer's burst pattern, and got a repro in two days. The trace showed the RX ring filling while the poll loop was stuck in a long critical section on another core. I proposed two fixes: a bigger ring (quick, hides the problem) or splitting the lock (correct, riskier). I chose the lock split, wrote a stress test that ran for 48 hours, and kept the bigger ring as a fallback behind a config flag.

Result. Drops went to zero in a month of field data; the trace tooling became part of our standard debug build and cut similar investigations from weeks to days. Lesson: measure before guessing; the counters found it, not intuition.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why not just ship the bigger ring? (Trade-off: speed vs correctness; you kept it as a fallback.)
  • How did you know the fix worked and was not luck? (48-hour stress, field data window.)
  • What would that look like in a web system? (Consumer lag growing because one consumer is blocked: same shape.)

Numbers in your stories must be your real numbers. The example above is a template: replace every detail with what actually happened.

A story bank: 8 stories that cover most questions

Prepare eight to ten flexible stories, each tagged with the principles it can prove. One story can answer several questions if you shift the emphasis.

Read the official list of the 16 principles on the Amazon Leadership Principles page and map each of your stories to two of them. Microsoft and Google ask similar questions with different labels (growth mindset, collaboration, "Googleyness"), so the same bank works there.

Turn the pipeline project into a story. "I built an order pipeline with an outbox table, Debezium CDC into Kafka, and a stream processor. Under load, consumer lag grew. I found one hot partition because the key was a store ID and one store had most of the traffic. I changed the key and added a lag alert. Lag stayed under a few seconds after the change." That single story answers Dive Deep, Learn and Be Curious, and gives the design round something real to dig into. Use your own measured numbers.

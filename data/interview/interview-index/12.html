Questions about the loop itself

These are real questions candidates face outside the technical rounds, with model answers.

Tell me about yourself (for a systems engineer applying to an SDE role).

Use the 60-second script above (the one canonical version): who you are (one line), what you did (two lines with one concrete achievement), what you have done to move into this area (the pipeline project), and why this role (one line linked to the team).

How to answer

Past, present, future in 60-90 seconds. End with a hook the interviewer can ask about ("happy to go into the pipeline project").

Likely follow-ups
  • Walk me through that project.
  • What was the hardest part of the move?
You have no web or cloud experience. Why should we consider you?

Translate, do not apologise. Name two or three concrete mappings (ECMP hashing to Kafka partition keys, routing daemon to Kubernetes controllers, buffer exhaustion to consumer lag) and one thing you built in the new space. Then say what you would still need to learn and how quickly you learn, with an example.

How to answer

Acknowledge in one sentence, translate in three, prove with one project, close with a learning example. Calm tone; this is a normal question.

Likely follow-ups
  • What surprised you most about distributed systems compared with embedded work?
  • What is one thing you still find hard?
Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager or a senior engineer.

Pick a technical disagreement where you had data. Show you raised it respectfully, brought evidence (a benchmark, a failure case), listened, and then either changed their mind or committed fully to the final decision. Amazon's principle is "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit".

How to answer

STAR. The Action must show both backbone (you spoke up with data) and commitment (you did not sabotage the decision). End with the outcome and the relationship afterwards.

Likely follow-ups
  • What if you turned out to be wrong?
  • How did the senior react?
Tell me about a time you failed.

Choose a real failure with real impact that you owned (a bad release, a missed estimate). Spend most of the answer on what you did to fix it and what you changed so it cannot happen again (a test, a check, a process). Avoid fake failures ("I work too hard").

How to answer

Own it in the first sentence. Fix, prevent, learn. A number for the impact and for the improvement.

Likely follow-ups
  • What would you do differently today?
  • How did you communicate it to stakeholders?
How is an SDE-2 design answer different from an SDE-1 answer?

SDE-1: clear requirements, a correct simple design, good API and data model, responds to hints. SDE-2: drives the round, uses estimates to make choices, picks technologies with reasons and costs, raises failure modes and monitoring without prompts, and explains how the design evolves at 10x.

How to answer

If asked directly, give the two lists above and one example (the URL shortener stepper on this page).

Likely follow-ups
  • Which level do you think you are at, and why?
How do you learn a new technology quickly?

Describe your real method with this pivot as the example: read the official concepts, build the smallest working thing, break it on purpose (kill a broker, OOM a pod), read the logs, then build something bigger and explain it to someone. Mention a concrete result: "within six weeks I had a pipeline running locally and on Kubernetes".

How to answer

Method plus one example plus one honest gap you are still working on.

Likely follow-ups
  • What did you break, and what did you learn from it?
Tell me about a time you dove deep to find the root cause of a problem.

This is where systems engineers shine. Use a story with instrumentation (counters, traces, a repro script), a hypothesis you disproved, the real cause, and a fix that was validated. Keep jargon light: explain "RX ring" as "the receive queue between the network card and the software".

How to answer

STAR, with the Action showing method: measure, hypothesise, test, fix, verify. Say how long each step took.

Likely follow-ups
  • What metric told you it was fixed?
  • How did you prevent the same class of bug?
Walk me through a distributed system you built end to end.

Use the ShopStream pipeline: the business goal, the diagram (app, outbox, CDC, Kafka, processor, sinks), why each piece exists (outbox avoids dual writes, keys keep per-order ordering, DLQ isolates poison messages), one failure you hit and fixed, and the numbers you measured (lag, throughput) on your setup.

How to answer

Draw first (max six boxes), then go left to right. Pause after the overview and ask where they want depth. Have one "what broke" story ready.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why Kafka and not a simple queue?
  • How do you avoid duplicates at the sink?
  • What would change at 100x traffic?
"How would you scale a system you have built?" (the Amazon SDE-2 classic)

Start from measurement: which resource saturates first (CPU, DB connections, disk, network) and which metric shows it. Then apply the standard moves in order of cost: cache reads, add replicas, make slow work asynchronous with a queue, partition the data, and finally split services. For each step, say what it costs (staleness, complexity, operations).

How to answer

Bottleneck first, then the ladder of fixes with trade-offs. Tie to Dive Deep: "I would profile before changing anything."

Likely follow-ups
  • What breaks first at 10x?
  • How would you migrate with zero downtime?
Do you have any questions for us?

Always yes. Ask about the team's real systems and how they work: "What does on-call look like and what was your last major incident?", "How do you deploy: Kubernetes, how often, with canaries?", "What is the hardest scaling problem the team has right now?" These show interest and give you information to decide.

How to answer

Two or three prepared questions per company. Avoid questions answered on the careers page.

Likely follow-ups
  • The interviewer may turn it into a short design chat. Enjoy it.

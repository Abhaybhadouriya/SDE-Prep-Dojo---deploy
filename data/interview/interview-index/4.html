SDE-1 vs SDE-2: the same question, two bars

The most useful thing to understand is what changes between levels. The question can be identical. The expected answer grows. Click Next to watch one design answer grow from "SDE-1 pass" to "SDE-2 pass" for the prompt "Design a URL shortener".

  1. SDE-1 core. Clarify requirements (shorten, redirect, maybe expiry), propose a REST API, and a table code -> long_url. A clean, correct, simple design already passes many SDE-1 bars.
  2. Scale awareness. Estimate: reads far outnumber writes (often assumed 100:1 in this prompt), so add a load balancer and a cache in front of the database. SDE-1 candidates who reach this point unprompted look strong.
  3. SDE-2: you pick technologies with reasons. How are codes generated without collisions (pre-allocated ID ranges, base62)? How is data sharded by code? You explain the choice and its cost.
  4. SDE-2: you drive beyond the prompt. Click analytics go to Kafka asynchronously so redirects stay fast. You say which parts are synchronous and which are not.
  5. SDE-2: failure and trade-offs without being asked. What happens when the cache or the key generator fails, what you would monitor, and what you would change at 10x traffic.
SDE-1 pass looks like
  • Asks clarifying questions
  • A working design with clear API and data model
  • Knows what a cache, a load balancer and an index are
  • Responds well to hints
SDE-2 pass adds
  • Drives the round without being pulled
  • Back-of-envelope numbers that shape decisions
  • Technology choice with reasons and costs
  • Failure modes, monitoring and evolution at 10x
Senior (L5+) adds
  • Org-wide trade-offs: which team owns what, and what other teams must change
  • A migration plan from today's system, with no big-bang cut-over
  • Cost: machines, storage, cross-zone traffic and people to run it
  • Says what not to build, and when "good enough" is the right answer

You are not expected to give the senior column at SDE-1 or SDE-2. One senior-style sentence (for example, "to migrate, I would dual-write for a week and compare") is a nice bonus, but do not spend the round there.

Whiteboard hygiene: draw so it does not look messy

A messy diagram makes a good design look weak, because the interviewer cannot follow it. These six habits fix most of it, on paper, on a whiteboard or in an online drawing tool:

  1. Flow left to right. Client on the left, data stores on the right, the request path in between. Async work (queues, consumers) goes below the main line.
  2. Six boxes at most at first. Draw the high-level picture with six boxes or fewer. Add detail only inside the part you go deep on, ideally in a second, zoomed-in drawing.
  3. Label every arrow. Write the protocol or the data on it: "HTTPS POST /orders", "gRPC", "OrderPlaced event", "SQL write". An unlabelled arrow is a question waiting to happen.
  4. Numbers in a corner box. Keep your estimates (QPS, storage, peak) in one box in a corner, so you can point at them when you make a choice.
  5. Name boxes by job, not by product. "Order service", "Cache", "Event log (Kafka)". Put the product in brackets, so the trade-off is about the job.
  6. Redraw instead of squeezing. When the picture gets crowded, say "let me redraw this part" and draw it again cleanly. Interviewers see that as clarity, not as a mistake.

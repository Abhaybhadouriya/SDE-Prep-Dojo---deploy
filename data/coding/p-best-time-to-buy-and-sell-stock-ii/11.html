Interview follow-ups

Prove the greedy is optimal.

Any trade (i, j) earns the sum of daily changes p[k+1] - p[k] for k in [i, j). Since trades do not overlap, a plan uses each daily change at most once, so its profit is at most the sum of all positive changes. Buying before each rising day and selling right after achieves exactly that sum, so it is optimal.

How to answer

Upper bound plus achievability: two sentences, then code.

Likely follow-ups
  • Does it still hold if same-day sell-and-buy is forbidden? (Yes: merge consecutive rising days into one trade; the total is unchanged.)
Each transaction costs a fee. What changes?

The greedy fails: many small rises each pay the fee. Use the DP: cash = max(cash, hold + p - fee), hold = max(hold, cash - p). O(n), O(1). The DP decides when merging rises into one trade is worth one fee instead of several.

How to answer

Show a counterexample to the greedy, e.g. [1,3,2,8] with fee 2: greedy pays two fees, the best plan buys 1 and sells 8 once.

Likely follow-ups
  • Fee on buy instead of sell? (Same answer; subtract it in the hold update.)
After a sale you must wait one day before buying again.

Three states: hold, sold-today, rest. hold = max(hold, rest - p), sold = hold + p, rest = max(rest, sold_prev). Or keep cash for the last two days and buy from cash[d-2]. O(n), O(1).

How to answer

Draw the state machine first; the code follows from the arrows.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why must the updates use the previous day's values? (Otherwise you sell and buy on the same day.)
Return the list of (buy day, sell day) pairs, not just the profit.

Scan for valleys and peaks: skip while the next price is not higher (buy day = end of the fall), then advance while the next price is higher (sell day = end of the rise), record the pair, repeat. Same O(n), and the pairs' profits add up to the greedy total.

How to answer

Mention that equal neighbouring prices must not start a trade (use strict comparisons).

Likely follow-ups
  • Output when no profit is possible? (An empty list.)
At most k transactions. How does this problem fit in?

Keep buy[t], sell[t] for t = 1..k: buy[t] = max(buy[t], sell[t-1] - p), sell[t] = max(sell[t], buy[t] + p). O(nk). If k >= n/2, the limit can never bind (there are at most n/2 useful trades), so return this problem's greedy in O(n).

How to answer

Present Stock II as the k = infinity case of the same machine.

Likely follow-ups
  • Faster than O(nk)? (Valley-peak pairs plus a heap of merge costs, O(n log n); rarely expected.)

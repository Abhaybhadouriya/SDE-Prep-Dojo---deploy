Scenario drills: the same moment, said badly and said well

Each drill is a real moment from a coding round. Read the situation, decide what you would say, then compare with the weak and the strong answer. The strong answers are not scripts; they show the shape: say what you see, say what you will do, then do it.

Scenario: three minutes of silence

You read "find the length of the longest substring with at most two distinct characters". You have an idea but are not sure it is right, so you start typing a loop without a word. Three minutes pass. The interviewer has written nothing in their notes.

Weak: Keep typing and explain everything at the end: "So, what I did was..." The interviewer cannot tell a good plan from a lucky guess, and if the loop is wrong you have no time left to recover.

Strong: "Let me think out loud for a second. Brute force checks every substring: O(n2) substrings, O(n) to count each, so O(n3). The condition is about a contiguous run, and when a window has three distinct characters, every bigger window that contains it also has three, so I can shrink from the left. That is a sliding window with a count map: O(n) time, O(1) space because the map holds at most three keys. Does that sound reasonable before I code it?"

Likely follow-ups
  • Why is it safe to move left forward and never back? (a bad window stays bad when it grows)
  • What changes for "at most k distinct"? (only the constant: the map holds k + 1 keys)

Scenario: the interviewer gives a hint

You are stuck on "Koko eating bananas" and are trying a greedy over the piles. The interviewer says: "What if you just guessed the speed?"

Weak: "Oh, yes, okay." Then you keep working on the greedy in silence, or you switch to guessing without saying how the hint changes the plan. The interviewer learns that you do not use input.

Strong: "Thanks, that helps. If I fix a speed k, I can check in O(n) whether she finishes in h hours: the sum of ceil(pile / k). A faster speed never makes it worse, so the answer is monotonic: binary search on k between 1 and the largest pile. That is O(n log max). I will write the check first, then the search."

Likely follow-ups
  • Why is the lower bound 1 and the upper bound max(pile)?
  • How do you compute ceil(p / k) without floating point? ((p + k - 1) / k)

Scenario: "can you do better?" after O(n log n)

You solved "top k frequent elements" with a count map plus a sort of the counts: O(n log n). The interviewer asks: "Can you do better?"

Weak: "I think O(n log n) is optimal because we have to sort." This states a false lower bound with confidence, and the interviewer now doubts your other claims too.

Strong: "Two options. A min-heap of size k gives O(n log k), which helps when k is small. And because a count is at most n, I can bucket elements by count, an array of n + 1 lists, and walk it from the top: O(n) time and O(n) space. The bucket version is optimal, since every element must be read once. I would use the heap version if the input is a stream that does not fit in memory."

Likely follow-ups
  • When is the heap version better in practice than the buckets?
  • What if equal counts must come out in a fixed order?

Scenario: you are not sure your approach is optimal

You have an O(n2) DP for a problem and suspect there is something faster, but you cannot see it. Twenty minutes are left.

Weak: Spend ten minutes searching for the perfect solution in silence, then run out of time with nothing written.

Strong: "I have a correct O(n2) DP. I suspect O(n log n) exists, maybe with binary search over the DP values, but I do not see it yet. I would rather have working code first: let me write the O(n2) version, test it, and then use the time left to look for the speed-up. Is that okay with you?"

Likely follow-ups
  • Interviewers usually say yes: a working solution with a clear next step scores better than an unfinished optimal one.
  • If they say "the O(n2) is fine", stop optimising and move on to tests.

Scenario: five minutes left and one bug

Your code fails the second example. Five minutes are left. You have a guess about where the bug is.

Weak: Make random edits and re-run, saying "hmm" and "wait". Each change breaks something else, and the interviewer sees no method.

Strong: "The first example passes and the second fails, and the only difference is a duplicate value. My guess is the comparison in the inner loop: < should be <=, so equal values pop. Let me trace the duplicate once... yes, that is it. Fixed, and the first example still passes. With more time I would also test an all-equal array and an empty one."

Likely follow-ups
  • What would you test next, and why that input?
  • How do you know the fix does not break the first example? (re-run it, out loud)

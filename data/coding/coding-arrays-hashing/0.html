The story: the cloakroom token

Explain like I'm 5

You go to a party and give your coat to the cloakroom. The helper does not pile all coats in one heap. She looks at your token number, walks straight to hook 37 and hangs it there. When you come back with token 37, she walks straight to hook 37 again. She never has to check every coat.

Now imagine she had no hooks and only a heap. To find your coat she would pick up every coat, one by one. With 10 coats that is fine. With 10,000 coats the party is over before you get your coat.

That is the whole idea of this page. A brute force answer to an array question usually says "for every element, look at every other element". That is the heap of coats: n x n = O(n2) work. A hash map (also called a dictionary or hash table) is the wall of hooks: you can store a value and later ask "have I seen this?" in about one step, O(1) on average. One pass over the array plus one O(1) question per element gives O(n).

From your world

You have written this many times in C. The Linux connection-tracking table, the neighbour (ARP) table and the inode cache are all arrays of buckets (struct hlist_head), indexed by a hash of the key (for example jhash of the 5-tuple), with a short chain per bucket. A lookup hashes the key, jumps to one bucket and walks a chain of a few nodes. unordered_map in C++, HashMap in Java, dict in Python and map in Go are the same structure with the chains and resizing hidden from you.

The story: the exam paper pile

Explain like I'm 5

A teacher has a messy pile of 40 exam papers. She wants to know: did two children get the same mark? Looking at every pair of papers would take ages. So she first puts the pile in order, lowest mark on top. Now two equal marks are always next to each other, and one walk down the pile answers the question.

Her friend has a smarter trick for small numbers. Marks are only 0 to 10, so he draws 11 boxes on the board and puts a tally mark in box 7 for each 7. He never compares two papers at all. Counting the tallies gives him the sorted order.

A third teacher only wants the median mark. She picks any paper, puts lower marks on the left and higher on the right, and then only looks at the side where the middle is. She never sorts the whole pile.

Those three teachers are this whole page: sort first to put related items next to each other, count when the values are small, and partition when you need only one position. A fourth idea, counting pairs while merging, is the same "put it in order" move used to answer "how many pairs are out of order".

From your world

The kernel ships two sorts and picks by need. lib/sort.c is a heapsort: no recursion and no extra memory, so the stack stays bounded, but it is not stable. lib/list_sort.c is a merge sort for struct list_head lists: stable and linked-list friendly. Interview sorting is the same trade: memory, stability and worst case. Counting sort is the radix trick behind a hash bucket array, and partitioning is what a NIC does when it steers packets to per-CPU queues by a key.

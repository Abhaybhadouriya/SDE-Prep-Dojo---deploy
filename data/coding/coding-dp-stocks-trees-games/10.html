Interview questions

Stock with cooldown: how do you derive the recurrence instead of memorising it?

List what can be true at the end of a day: I hold a share, I sold today, or I own nothing and am free. Draw arrows: free to hold (buy, minus price), hold to hold (wait), hold to sold (sell, plus price), sold to free (forced), free to free (wait). Each state's value is the max over its incoming arrows, computed from yesterday's values. Answer = max(sold, free) on the last day. O(n) time, O(1) space.

How to answer

Draw the three-box diagram on the whiteboard first. Then the code is three lines and the interviewer can check it against the picture.

Likely follow-ups
  • Cooldown of c days? (Buy reads the "free" value from c + 1 days ago; keep a small array of past sold values.)
  • Also a fee? (Subtract it on the sell arrow; the machine is unchanged.)
At most k transactions. What is the complexity, and what if k is huge?

Keep buy[1..k] and sell[0..k]; each day update buy[j] = max(buy[j], sell[j-1] - p) and sell[j] = max(sell[j], buy[j] + p). O(n k) time, O(k) space. If k >= n/2 the limit never binds (a transaction needs two days), so return the sum of all positive rises in O(n).

How to answer

Show that k = 1 and k = 2 are special cases of the same chain. Mention the guard without being asked; it shows you think about input sizes.

Likely follow-ups
  • Can you beat O(n k)? (Yes, an O(n log n) method using valley/peak pairs and a heap, or "Aliens trick" with a binary search on a per-transaction penalty; rarely expected.)
  • Why is the in-place update within a day safe? (It only adds same-day buy+sell, which earns 0.)
House Robber III. Why does robbing alternate levels fail, and what does your DFS return?

Alternate levels ignores that the best choice can differ per branch: in one subtree you may skip two levels in a row ([2, 1, 3, null, 4]: levels give 6, robbing 3 and 4 gives 7). The DFS returns (take, skip) for each subtree: take = val + left.skip + right.skip; skip = max(left) + max(right). Answer = max of the root's pair. O(n) time, O(h) stack.

How to answer

Give a small counterexample to the greedy, then define the pair. Mention the memo-on-node version as the stepping stone.

Likely follow-ups
  • Deep tree (105 nodes in a line)? (Recursion depth; do an iterative post-order with an explicit stack.)
  • General tree / graph without cycles? (Same, sum over all children: Maximum Independent Set on a tree.)
Binary Tree Cameras: prove the greedy is optimal.

Take a deepest leaf. Someone must watch it: the leaf itself or its parent. A camera on the parent watches a superset of what a camera on the leaf watches (the leaf, the parent, the sibling and the grandparent), so some optimal solution uses the parent. Remove what is now covered and repeat; the post-order DFS with states NOT_COVERED / HAS_CAMERA / COVERED performs exactly this exchange argument bottom up.

How to answer

Exchange argument in two sentences, then the three states, then the root fix.

Likely follow-ups
  • Weighted camera costs per node? (Greedy breaks; use a real tree DP with 3 values per node: has camera, covered by a child, covered by the parent.)
  • Camera covers distance 2? (More states, same idea: track the distance to the nearest camera above and below.)
Predict the Winner: why store the difference instead of both scores?

The game is zero-sum and symmetric: whoever moves faces the same choices. If diff[i][j] is the best (mover - other) on cards i..j, then after I take a card the opponent becomes the mover with value diff on the rest, so mine is card - diff(rest). One recurrence, one max, no turn flag. Fill by interval length, O(n2) time, O(n) space.

How to answer

Start from the recursive minimax, notice the turn flag only flips a sign, collapse to the difference, then memo or table.

Likely follow-ups
  • Even number of cards? (Player 1 always wins: pick all even or all odd positions.)
  • Return the actual scores? (total = a + b and diff = a - b give a = (total + diff) / 2.)
Stone Game III: players take 1, 2 or 3 stones from the front. Who wins?

Same difference trick on suffixes: dp[i] = best (mover - other) when stones i..n-1 remain = max over t in 1..3 of (sum of stones i..i+t-1) - dp[i+t]. dp[n] = 0. Compare dp[0] with 0 for Alice / Bob / Tie. O(n) time, O(1) space with a rolling array of 4.

How to answer

Point out that values can be negative, so "take as many as possible" is wrong; that is why it is a DP.

Likely follow-ups
  • Take 1..M with M changing (Stone Game II)? (State (i, M), with suffix sums; value = best stones for the mover, since totals are fixed.)
Maximum profit in job scheduling: walk through your solution and its complexity.

Sort jobs by end. dp[i] = best using the first i jobs. dp[i] = max(dp[i-1], profit + dp[k]) where k = number of jobs among the first i - 1 whose end <= this start (upper bound by binary search). O(n log n) time, O(n) space. An equivalent version sorts by start and does dp from the right with a lower bound on start >= end.

How to answer

Explain why a time-indexed DP is impossible (times to 109), then why sorting by end makes "compatible jobs" a prefix.

Likely follow-ups
  • At most k jobs? (dp[i][c], same binary search: Maximum Number of Events II, O(n k log n) or O(n k) with precomputed indexes.)
  • Unit profits? (Greedy by earliest end: classic activity selection.)
Stock II has a one-line greedy. Why bother with the state machine?

The greedy (sum of positive rises) is correct for unlimited trades with no fee or cooldown, and is the best answer for that exact question. But the moment the interviewer adds a fee, a cooldown or a cap on trades, the greedy breaks, while the state machine absorbs each change by editing one arrow. Show the greedy, then say "if you add a fee I would switch to the two-state DP".

How to answer

Give both, and name the follow-up that kills the greedy before they ask it.

Likely follow-ups
  • Why does the greedy fail with a fee? (Splitting one long rise into many small trades pays the fee many times.)

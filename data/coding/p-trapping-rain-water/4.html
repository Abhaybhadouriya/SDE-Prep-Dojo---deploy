Approaches: brute → better → optimal

Hint 1

Do not think of pools. Think of one column at a time: how high can water stand on top of column i?

Hint 2

It can stand as high as the lower of the two tallest bars around it. Can you get "tallest to the left" for every i in one pass?

Hint 3

If you already know a bar on the right that is at least as tall as the left max, the right side cannot be the limit for the current left column.

1. Brute force - scan both sides for every column

Idea: for each i, walk left to find the tallest bar (including i), walk right to find the tallest bar, add min(L, R) - height[i].

O(n²)time
O(1)space

What is wasted: the "tallest to the left of i" is the same scan as for i-1 plus one bar. We recompute it n times. -> rung 2.

2. Better - prefix max and suffix max arrays

Idea: leftMax[i] = max(leftMax[i-1], height[i]) left to right, rightMax[i] = max(rightMax[i+1], height[i]) right to left, then sum min(leftMax[i], rightMax[i]) - height[i]. Three passes, each O(n).

O(n)time

What is wasted: two extra arrays. For each column we only ever use the smaller of the two maxima. -> rung 4.

3. Alternative - monotonic stack (fill layer by layer)

Idea: keep a stack of indices with decreasing heights. When a taller bar j arrives, pop the top mid: the new top is its left wall, j is its right wall, and the water between them over mid is (min(h[left], h[j]) - h[mid]) * (j - left - 1). This counts water in horizontal layers instead of columns.

for j, h in enumerate(height):
    while stack and height[stack[-1]] < h:
        mid = stack.pop()
        if not stack: break
        left = stack[-1]
        water += (min(height[left], h) - height[mid]) * (j - left - 1)
    stack.append(j)
O(n)time
O(n)space

When it is useful: it works left to right in one pass, so it is the version to mention for a streaming follow-up; each index is pushed and popped once.

4. Optimal - two pointers

while l < r:
    if height[l] < height[r]:
        left_max = max(left_max, height[l]); water += left_max - height[l]; l += 1
    else:
        right_max = max(right_max, height[r]); water += right_max - height[r]; r -= 1
  1. Each column needs only min(leftMax, rightMax). If we can prove which side is smaller, we do not need the other value exactly.
  2. The lower pointer is the one we can settle. If height[l] < height[r], there is a bar on the right (at r) taller than every bar we have seen on the left; so for column l, rightMax >= height[r] > leftMax is impossible to beat, and the level is leftMax.
  3. Move that pointer inward and repeat. The mirror rule settles column r when height[r] <= height[l].
  4. Stop when the pointers meet. They meet at a tallest bar, which holds no water.
O(n)time
O(1)space

Why it works: the left pointer only ever steps past a bar while a strictly taller bar stands at the right pointer. So the bar that set leftMax had a taller partner somewhere to the right, and leftMax is the true limit for column l. The same holds for the right side with "taller or equal".

Proof (optional)

Claim: when column l is settled, leftMax(l) <= rightMax(l), so min(leftMax, rightMax) = leftMax. Let k <= l be the index whose bar equals leftMax. Column k was settled by the left branch, so at that moment height[k] < height[rk] for the right pointer position rk. The right pointer only moves left and is still > l, so rk > l. Hence a bar right of l is taller than leftMax, and rightMax(l) > leftMax(l). The right branch is the mirror image (with <=). Every column is settled exactly once, so the sum equals the rung-2 formula.

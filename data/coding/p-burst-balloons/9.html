60-second recall

  • Cue: remove items one by one, cost uses current neighbours, n ≤ 500 -> interval DP.
  • Insight: pick the LAST balloon k in open (l, r): its neighbours are l and r, sides independent.
  • State: pad with 1s; best[l][r] over open intervals; best[l][l+1] = 0.
  • Trap: "first burst" split is wrong; fill by increasing gap; ends are exclusive.
  • Cost: O(n3) time, O(n2) space; answer best[0][n+1].
v = [1] + nums + [1]; m = len(v)
dp = [[0] * m for _ in range(m)]
for gap in range(2, m):
    for l in range(m - gap):
        r = l + gap
        dp[l][r] = max(dp[l][k] + dp[k][r] + v[l] * v[k] * v[r] for k in range(l + 1, r))

Why does the recurrence choose the LAST balloon burst in (l, r) rather than the first?

  1. It is faster to code
  2. Its neighbours are then exactly l and r, so the two sides are independent
  3. Greedy says so
After the first burst, its two neighbours become adjacent and the halves interact; the last balloon sees only the walls.

What do the two 1s added to the ends represent?

  1. The 'missing neighbour counts as 1' rule; they are never burst
  2. Two extra balloons you must burst
  3. Sentinels for binary search
They are permanent walls, so best[0][n+1] is the answer.

In what order must the cells best[l][r] be filled?

  1. l increasing, r increasing
  2. Any order
  3. By increasing gap r - l
Each cell reads only strictly shorter intervals.

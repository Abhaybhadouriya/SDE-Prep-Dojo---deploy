The story: the clock and the doubling trick

Explain like I'm 5

A clock only knows 12 numbers. Three hours after 11 o'clock it is 2 o'clock, not 14. Computers count like that too. A number box has a fixed size, and if you add past the top you land back at the bottom. Much interview maths is about remembering the clock: check before you go past the top, or do your sums "on the clock" on purpose (that is % 1,000,000,007).

The other big idea is doubling. To get 2 to the power 16 you do not multiply 2 sixteen times. You go 2, 4, 16, 256, 65536: square, square, square, square. Four steps. Clever maths in interviews is usually a way to do fewer steps by halving or doubling.

From your world

You have met every idea here in the kernel. jiffies wraps around, which is why time_after(a, b) compares a signed difference instead of the raw values. linux/overflow.h has check_add_overflow() and check_mul_overflow() because a size calculation that silently wraps is a security bug. GCC exposes the same thing as __builtin_mul_overflow. Hash tables use power-of-two sizes so hash % size becomes hash & (size - 1). do_div() exists because 64-bit division is not free on 32-bit CPUs. And packet sampling (keep one packet in N, at random) is the reservoir idea at the end of this page.

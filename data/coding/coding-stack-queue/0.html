The story: plates and a lunch line

Explain like I'm 5

In the school kitchen, clean plates go on a pile. You always put a plate on the top, and you always take the top plate. The last plate washed is the first plate used. That pile is a stack. Outside, children wait in the lunch line. The first child who came is the first child served, and new children join at the back. That line is a queue.

A stack is "last in, first out" (LIFO): push puts an item on top, pop removes the top, peek (or top) looks at it. A queue is "first in, first out" (FIFO): enqueue at the back, dequeue at the front. A deque ("deck", double-ended queue) allows push and pop at both ends. All of these are O(1) per operation.

Interview stack questions rarely say "use a stack". They describe something nested or something that cancels: brackets, a file path with .., a string like 3[ab], colliding objects, or "the next bigger value to the right". Your job is to notice that the most recent unresolved item is the one that gets resolved next.

From your world

You already rely on stacks every day: the call stack (each call pushes a return address, each ret pops it), nested interrupt handling, and the parser in every protocol decoder that handles nested TLVs. Queues are your NIC rings and kfifo: a fixed array with a head and tail index that wrap around. When an interview asks for a "circular queue", it is a ring buffer, and the full-versus-empty ambiguity is the same one you solved with a count or one spare slot.

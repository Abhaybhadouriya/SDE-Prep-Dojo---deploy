The story: counting steps with a notebook

Explain like I'm 5

You stand at the bottom of a staircase with 5 steps. Each time you may climb 1 step or 2 steps. How many different ways can you reach the top? You think: "My last move was either a 1-step from step 4 or a 2-step from step 3. So the ways to reach 5 are the ways to reach 4 plus the ways to reach 3." Then you ask the same question about step 4, and step 3, and so on. A clever kid notices she keeps asking about step 2 again and again, so she writes each answer in a notebook the first time and just reads it the next time. That notebook is dynamic programming.

Two properties make a problem a DP problem:

  • Optimal substructure: the answer to the big question can be built from answers to smaller questions of the same kind (ways to reach 5 = ways to reach 4 + ways to reach 3).
  • Overlapping subproblems: the same smaller questions come up many times. Without the notebook you recompute them; with it, each is solved once.

If only the first holds (the subproblems never repeat, as in merge sort), it is plain divide and conquer. If both hold, caching turns exponential time into polynomial time. The name "dynamic programming" is historical (Richard Bellman chose it in the 1950s); it has nothing to do with dynamic code. Read it as "filling a table".

From your world

Memoisation is a cache with a perfect hit rule: the key is the subproblem, the value never goes stale, and you never evict. It is the page cache or the dentry cache without the hard parts. Tabulation is the precomputed table you build once at init, like a CRC-32 lookup table built by a loop at module load, where each entry is computed from simpler ones. And the "keep only the last two values" trick below is a two-slot ring buffer.

Interview Q&A about revision and preparation

Questions you will ask yourself while preparing, plus two that interviewers ask that this system helps you answer.

How many problems do I need to solve before an interview?

Fewer than you think, if you keep them. A common target is 150-250 well-chosen problems that you can redo cold, covering every pattern. The 12-week plan here adds about 236 new problems and revises all of them. Count the problems you can re-solve, not the ones you have seen.

How to answer

Track two numbers: problems solved, and problems at a gap of 14 days or more (they survived several reviews). The second number is your real readiness.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What about Hard problems?" (Do the Hard ones in Tier A for your target groups; they are the follow-ups of Mediums you already know.)
Should I look at the solution if I am stuck on a new problem?

Yes, after a fixed time box: about 25 minutes for a Medium. Struggling longer has low value. Read the idea, close it, code it yourself, write the card, and let the schedule bring it back tomorrow. A problem you solved with help is still worth a card; the 1-day review will show whether it stuck.

How to answer

Read only the hint or the first paragraph of the explanation, then try again for 10 minutes before reading the rest.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Do I mark it Solved?" (Yes, so it enters the review queue; its first review is tomorrow.)
Why 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 days and not some other numbers?

Each gap is roughly double the last, which matches the finding that a reviewed memory decays more slowly each time. The exact numbers matter much less than the shape: short gaps first (most forgetting is early), growing gaps later. Anki and SuperMemo compute a gap per card; we use fixed rungs so the schedule is predictable.

How to answer

Keep the ladder; adjust only the grading. Hard and Easy already let a card move slower or faster than the default.

Likely follow-ups
  • "My interview is in 10 days; does the ladder still help?" (Yes, but only the first rungs fire: focus on the 1 and 3-day reviews and full re-solves of your target company's list.)
I have 400 problems solved over two years and no cards. Where do I start?

Do not write 400 cards. Filter to Tier A problems you have solved, mark them Solved on the site (their first review lands tomorrow), and write the card only when each one comes up for review. On the first review, try it cold; if it fails, it is a new problem for you and goes through the failure steps.

How to answer

Use the 25 rule to stop the backlog flooding you: mark about 20 a day as Solved, not 400 at once.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What about Tier B and C problems I solved?" (Ignore them unless a target company list has them high.)
Is it bad if the interviewer gives me a problem I have on a card?

No. Say you have seen something similar, then solve it properly: clarify, give the brute force, explain the insight, code, test. Your card means the insight comes fast, which leaves time for the follow-up, and the follow-up is where SDE-2 and SDE-3 signal is decided.

How to answer

Use the time you saved to explain why the approach is correct, not only what it is.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Now the input is a stream" or "now k changes per query". (Line 3 of a good card already says which invariant makes the approach work, which is what you adapt.)
Interview question: design a spaced-repetition scheduler. What would you build?

Each card stores interval, due and a review count. A grade maps the interval to a new one (a ladder or SM-2's ease factor). For "what is due today", keep a min-heap or a sorted index on due; for many users, store cards in a table indexed by (user, due) and page through due ones. Reviews are append-only events, so the schedule can be recomputed if the algorithm changes.

How to answer

Start with the single-user data structure (the template above), then scale: per-user partitioning, an index on due date, offline sync with last-writer-wins per card.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Two devices grade the same card offline. Which wins?" (Keep both review events; replay them in timestamp order to compute the final state.)
  • "How do you change the algorithm for existing users?" (Recompute from the review log, which is why you keep it.)
"Tell me about a time you learned something difficult quickly." Can my prep system be the answer?

It can, if you tell it with data, Amazon "Learn and Be Curious" style: the situation (rusty on DSA after years of kernel work), the system you built (cards, schedule, failure triage), the measured result (for example, the share of problems at a 14-day gap or more, or mock pass rate rising from week 6 to week 12), and what you changed after it failed.

How to answer

STAR format, one number per step. Prefer a work example if you have one; use this as a backup.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What did not work?" (Have a real answer, such as grading too kindly at first and finding Cold cards in a mock.)

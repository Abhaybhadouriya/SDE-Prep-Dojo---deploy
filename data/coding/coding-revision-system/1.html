Why you forget problems you already solved

In 1885 the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how fast he forgot lists of nonsense syllables. The shape he found, now called the forgetting curve, is steep at first and then flattens: most of the loss happens in the first day or two. Later research found two things that matter for us:

  1. Each well-timed review makes the next drop slower. A memory you have pulled back several times lasts much longer than one you met once. This is the spacing effect.
  2. Pulling the answer out of your head beats reading it again. Trying to recall, even when you fail, strengthens memory more than re-reading the solution. This is the testing effect (also called retrieval practice).

For interviews this has a blunt meaning. If you solve 300 problems once each and never look back, by the loop you will reliably remember the last few weeks' worth. If you solve 200 and revise them on a schedule, you walk in with most of the 200 ready. Interviewers do not care how many problems you have seen; they care whether you can produce one cold, in 35 minutes, while talking.

"Revision" on this page always means recall first, check second. Reading your old solution is not revision. Closing it and trying is.

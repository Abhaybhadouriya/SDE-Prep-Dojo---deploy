Approaches: brute -> better -> optimal

Hint 1

Once a price of 50 has arrived, can a day with price 30 before it ever stop a future span?

Hint 2

If you throw such days away, how do you still count them in later spans?

1. Brute force - store all prices, walk back

Idea: append each price to a list and walk backwards from today while prices are ≤ today's; the number of steps is the span.

O(n)per call (worst)
O(n)space

What is wasted: on a rising series every call rescans every earlier day: O(n2) in total.

2. Better - jump with stored spans

Idea: keep every price and its span in arrays. To walk back from today, do not step one day at a time: at day j with price ≤ today, jump directly to j - span[j], because all days inside j's span are ≤ price[j] ≤ today.

O(1)amortised per call
O(n)space (all days)

This is already fast; the stack version is the same jumps, but it deletes what can never be visited again.

3. Optimal - monotonic stack of (price, span)

  1. span = 1 (today).
  2. While the stack is non-empty and its top price is ≤ today's price: pop it and add its span.
  3. Push (price, span) and return span.
O(1)amortised per call
O(n)space (worst, falling prices)

Why it works: invariant: the stack holds, from bottom to top, exactly the days that have no later day with a price ≥ theirs, with strictly decreasing prices, and each entry's span counts the days from the previous stack entry (exclusive) up to itself. A popped day d had price ≤ today, and every day it covered was ≤ price[d] ≤ today, so the whole block joins today's span. The first entry left in the stack has price > today, so the span stops exactly there. Popped days can never stop a later span either: today is ≥ them and is closer. Amortised: each day is pushed once and popped at most once, so n calls cost at most 2n stack operations.

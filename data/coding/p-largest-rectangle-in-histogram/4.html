Approaches: brute -> better -> optimal

Hint 1

Every rectangle has a lowest bar. Try fixing that bar instead of fixing the two ends.

Hint 2

For a fixed lowest bar, how far left and right can the rectangle go?

Hint 3

While heights keep rising, no bar's right wall is known yet. What does a lower bar tell you about the bars before it?

1. Brute force - every pair of ends, or expand from every bar

Idea: for each left end i, move the right end j forward while keeping a running minimum; area = min × (j - i + 1). Equivalent: for each bar, walk left and right while neighbours are at least as tall.

O(n²)time
O(1)extra space

What is wasted: each walk rescans bars that an earlier walk already proved taller. On [1,2,3,...,n] it does about n²/2 steps: about 5·10^9 for n = 10^5, too slow. -> rung 2 reuses earlier answers.

2. Better - nearest smaller on each side (two passes)

Idea: compute L[] and R[] first, then take the max of heights[k] × (R[k] - L[k] - 1). Each array comes from one stack pass (pop while the top is >= the current bar; what remains on top is the nearest smaller). A popular alternative needs no stack: to find L[i], start at p = i - 1 and, while heights[p] >= heights[i], jump to p = L[p] - every bar between L[p] and p is already known to be at least heights[p].

O(n)time (three passes)
O(n)space (two arrays)

What is wasted: two extra arrays and three passes. Perfectly fine to code; rung 3 just merges it into one pass.

3. Optimal - one pass with a sentinel bar of height 0

  1. Invariant: the stack holds indices with non-decreasing heights from bottom to top. Each one is a bar whose right wall has not appeared yet.
  2. A lower (or equal) bar i arrives: pop the top t. Bar i is the first bar right of t that is not taller, so it is t's right wall.
  3. The left wall is free: the index now on top of the stack is the nearest bar left of t that is lower (everything between was popped earlier by something lower than it). Empty stack -> left wall is -1.
  4. Measure heights[t] × (i - leftWall - 1), keep the max, repeat while the top is still >= the new bar, then push i.
  5. Sentinel: run i up to n with height 0, so every bar left on the stack is measured at the end.
O(n)time, each index pushed and popped once
O(n)space (the stack, worst case on rising heights)

Why >= is safe: with equal heights, the earlier bar is popped by the later one and measured with a width that stops at the later bar, which is too short. That is fine: the later equal bar stays on the stack with the same left wall and is measured later with the full width. One bar of each equal run is always measured correctly, and that is all a max needs. Using > also works; just be consistent.

Proof (optional)

Claim: when t is popped at step i, (a) every bar strictly between t and i has height >= heights[t] and heights[i] <= heights[t]; (b) with s the new top, every bar strictly between s and t has height >= heights[t] and heights[s] <= heights[t]. (a) holds because any lower bar between them would have popped t earlier. (b) holds because those bars were popped, before t was pushed or while t sat above s, by bars not taller than themselves, and the stack is sorted. So the measured rectangle is valid. For optimality, take an optimal rectangle that cannot be widened, so the bars just outside it are strictly lower (or it touches an end). Let k be the rightmost bar of minimum height inside it. Bars right of k inside the rectangle are strictly taller, so k is popped exactly at the right outside neighbour (or the sentinel). Bars left of k inside the rectangle are at least as tall, so they were all popped (equal ones by k itself, because of >=), and the bar below k on the stack is the left outside neighbour. The measured width is the optimal rectangle's width.

Other known routes, for completeness: divide and conquer on the minimum (O(n log n) average, O(n²) on sorted input unless the minimum comes from a segment tree), and a segment-tree range-minimum version (O(n log n)). Interviewers expect the stack.

The story: school clubs and their captains

Explain like I'm 5

Every child in a school starts in a club of one, and is that club's captain. When two children become friends, their two clubs join into one, and one captain stops being captain and now reports to the other. To check whether two children are in the same club, each one asks "who is my captain?", and if that person also has a captain, asks again, until reaching someone who is their own captain. Same final captain means same club. The clever trick: after you have climbed the chain once, you write the final captain's name on your hand so next time you go straight there.

That is the whole data structure. A disjoint set union (DSU, also called union-find) stores a collection of non-overlapping sets over items 0..n-1 and supports two operations:

  • find(x): return the representative (root, "captain") of x's set. Two items are in the same set exactly when their finds are equal.
  • union(a, b): merge the set containing a with the set containing b.

What it does not support matters as much: you cannot split a set again, cannot list a set's members quickly (you have to scan), and you cannot ask for the path between two items. It answers "together or not?", and nothing else, extremely fast.

From your world

Think of compound_head() in the kernel: every tail page of a compound page points to its head page, and asking "which head owns this page?" is a find. Union-find is the same idea, except chains can be longer and get flattened as you walk them. Another fit: links coming up one by one between switches, and the question "are host A and host B in the same L2 broadcast domain yet?". Each link up is a union; each reachability question is a find. Re-running a BFS after every link change would be the naive way.

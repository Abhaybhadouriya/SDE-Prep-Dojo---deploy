The story: the biggest coin first

Explain like I'm 5

You have to pay 6 rupees at a shop, and you want to hand over as few coins as possible. You have coins of 1, 2 and 5. Easy: take the biggest coin that still fits. 5 fits, so take it. 1 rupee is left, so take a 1. Two coins. Done, and it is the best you can do.

Now a strange country has coins of 1, 3 and 4. You need 6 again. "Biggest first" takes 4, then 1, then 1: three coins. But two 3s also make 6, and that is only two coins. The same simple rule that worked at home gives a worse answer here.

That is the whole topic in one picture. A greedy algorithm builds the answer one step at a time and, at every step, takes the choice that looks best right now. It never goes back to change an earlier choice. When the problem has the right structure, this is the fastest and simplest correct solution, usually one sort plus one pass. When it does not, greedy quietly returns a wrong answer, and nothing in the code warns you.

From your world

You have shipped a greedy algorithm that is proven safe: the SPF run in OSPF and IS-IS is Dijkstra's algorithm. It always settles the closest unsettled node next and never revisits it. That is safe only because link costs are never negative; one negative cost breaks it, which is why Bellman-Ford exists. Every greedy on this page has a similar condition. Your job in an interview is to name it.

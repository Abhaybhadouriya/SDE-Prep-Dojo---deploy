The story: a train of letter carriages

Explain like I'm 5

Think of a toy train. Every carriage carries one letter: R A C E C A R. You can walk along the train from the front, or from the back, or from both ends at once and meet in the middle. If the letters you see from both ends always match until you meet, the train reads the same both ways. That is a palindrome.

Some trains are glued together: you cannot swap two carriages, you have to build a new train. Some trains have a sign on the engine saying how many carriages there are; others have a red flag on the last carriage and you must walk to the end to count. Every string problem is a game played on such a train.

String questions are a favourite warm-up at Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Bloomberg. The ideas are small; the marks are lost on details: an empty string, an extra space, a sign with no digits, a number that does not fit in 32 bits.

From your world

You already know both kinds of train. A C string is a char * with a '\0' flag at the end: strlen() walks the whole thing, and a missing terminator walks off into the next page. A packet buffer (struct sk_buff) or a netlink attribute carries an explicit length instead. Interview languages (Go, Java, Python, C++ std::string) are all length-prefixed, so len(s) is O(1). The parsing half of this page is the same work as validating a header field from the wire: never trust the input, check every byte, and stop before you overflow.

The story: a city of houses and roads

Explain like I'm 5

Imagine a small town drawn on paper. Every house is a dot. Every road between two houses is a line. If you want to visit every house, you can walk one road as far as it goes and come back when you get stuck (that is DFS, "go deep"). Or you can first visit every house next door, then every house two roads away, then three (that is BFS, "go wide", like ripples when a stone drops in a pond). The ripple way has a gift: the first ripple to touch a house found the shortest way there.

A graph is exactly that picture: a set of vertices (also called nodes: the houses) and edges (the roads) that join pairs of them. Edges can be undirected (two-way road) or directed (one-way street: "course A must come before course B"). They may carry a weight (length, cost, time). Vertices joined by an edge are neighbours.

Many problems that never say "graph" are graphs in disguise: a grid of land and water, words that change one letter at a time, course prerequisites, accounts sharing emails. Your first job is always the same: name the vertices and the edges. After that, one of a handful of templates does the rest.

From your world

You have run graph algorithms in production for years. OSPF and IS-IS compute shortest paths with Dijkstra over the link-state database. Spanning Tree Protocol builds a loop-free tree over bridges so broadcast frames do not circle forever: that is "detect and cut cycles". modprobe loads modules in dependency order from modules.dep, and make builds targets after their prerequisites: both are topological sorts. A flood with a TTL is a BFS whose layer number is the hop count.

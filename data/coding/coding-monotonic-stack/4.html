The contribution technique: sum over all subarrays

LC 907, Sum of Subarray Minimums: for every contiguous subarray, take its minimum; return the sum of all these minimums modulo 109 + 7. There are n(n + 1) / 2 subarrays, about 4.5 × 108 for n = 3 × 104, so you cannot list them.

Turn the sum around. Instead of asking "what is the minimum of each subarray?", ask "for how many subarrays is a[i] the minimum?" Then the answer is the sum over i of a[i] * count[i].

The subarrays where a[i] is the minimum are the ones that contain i and do not reach a smaller element. If the previous smaller element is at p and the next smaller is at q, the subarray can start anywhere in p + 1 .. i (that is i - p choices) and end anywhere in i .. q - 1 (q - i choices). So count[i] = (i - p) * (q - i).

The tie trap

What if two equal values could both be the minimum of the same subarray? In [2, 2, 2] the subarray [2, 2] has two minimums. If both sides use "strictly smaller" as the boundary, both 2s claim it and you count it twice. If both sides use "smaller or equal", neither claims it and you miss it.

The fix: make the boundary strict on one side and non-strict on the other. Then each subarray is credited to exactly one of its equal minimums (the leftmost or the rightmost, depending on which side you made non-strict).

Our two-pass template uses "previous strictly smaller" on the left (pop while a[top] > a[i]) and "next smaller or equal" on the right (scanning from the right, pop while a[top] >= a[i]). The one-pass version settles each element at its pop: the popping index is its right boundary and the new top is its left boundary, and the single pop condition decides the tie rule for you.

Sum of subarray ranges (LC 2104)

The range of a subarray is its max minus its min. Because sums split, sum(max - min) = sum(max) - sum(min). Run the contribution pass twice: once with "greater" (each element's count as the maximum) and once with "smaller". O(n) instead of the O(n2) the problem allows. The top-voted LC 2104 solution by lee215 makes exactly this split.

Overflow. In LC 907, a[i] * (i - p) * (q - i) can reach 3 × 104 × (1.5 × 104)2, about 7 × 1012. Use 64-bit and take the modulo after each product. In LC 2104 there is no modulo and the true answer fits in 64 bits, so keep everything in long.

The same "count[i] = left choices times right choices" idea scores well in harder problems: Maximum Subarray Min-Product (LC 1856) uses previous and next smaller plus prefix sums; Sum of Total Strength of Wizards (LC 2281) adds prefix sums of prefix sums on top.

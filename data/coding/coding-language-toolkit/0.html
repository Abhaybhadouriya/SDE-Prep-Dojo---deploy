The story: a new workshop with labelled drawers

Explain like I'm 5

At home you built every tool yourself: you carved a hammer, bent a wrench, wove a basket for screws. You are very good at it. Now you start in a new workshop, and the walls are full of drawers labelled "hammer", "wrench", "basket of screws". You could still carve your own hammer, but the job has to be done before lunch. The winning move is to learn which drawer holds what, and the few odd habits of these tools (this wrench turns the other way).

A coding interview judges your problem solving, not your ability to re-implement a hash map. Every mainstream interview language ships the same core containers: a growable array, a hash map and set, a double-ended queue, a priority queue (heap), sorting with a custom order, and (in C++ and Java) a sorted map. Learn one language's versions cold, know the defaults, and you free your 45 minutes for the actual algorithm.

From your world

The kernel already taught you the ideas: struct list_head and hlist_head buckets are a chained hash table, rb_root is an ordered map, kfifo is a ring-buffer queue, include/linux/min_heap.h is a binary heap, and lib/sort.c takes a comparator callback just like qsort. The library versions differ mainly in ownership (they copy or own the elements instead of embedding a node in your struct) and in defaults you must memorise.

The story: two lines at the school gate

Explain like I'm 5

A teacher wants to know, at any moment, who is the middle child by height. Children keep arriving. She does not line them all up each time. She keeps two groups: the "short" group and the "tall" group, about the same size. In the short group she only watches the tallest child. In the tall group she only watches the shortest child. Those two children stand next to each other at the gate, and the middle child is always one of them, or halfway between them.

A new child joins the right group; if one group gets too big, its edge child walks across. Nobody is ever sorted.

That is the two-heap pattern. A max-heap holds the smaller half and shows its largest item. A min-heap holds the larger half and shows its smallest item. Those two tops are the middle of the data. If you need a refresher on what a heap is, read Heaps and priority queues first; this page assumes push, pop and peek.

The second half of the page is about a cousin: k-way merge. You have k sorted streams. The next smallest item overall must be one of the k front items, so a heap of the fronts gives it to you. Both patterns rest on the same idea: you only ever need to look at a few "edge" items, and a heap keeps those edges cheap.

From your world

The kernel's page reclaim keeps two LRU lists, active and inactive, and moves pages across the boundary to keep their sizes in a target ratio. Two heaps do the same with a stricter rule: sizes equal (or off by one), and every item on one side is no bigger than every item on the other. k-way merge is what you already know from merging sorted runs on disk, or from picking the next expiring timer across several per-CPU queues.

Interview Q&A about mocks and the final week

How many mock interviews should I do before a coding loop?

There is no magic number; what matters is the trend. A practical target: start weekly mocks once you can solve most Tier A Mediums untimed, then two or three per week in the last three weeks, so somewhere between eight and fifteen graded mocks in total. Stop adding mocks once your last three are at or above your level's bar; switch to keeping them there.

How to answer

If a recruiter or mentor asks, give the plan and the stopping rule, not a count: "Weekly, then three a week near the date, until three in a row meet the bar."

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if you have only one week?" (two or three mocks on D-7, D-5, D-3, the rest on cards).
  • "Peer or paid mocks?" (peer mocks for volume; one or two paid ones with an experienced interviewer for calibration).
It is minute 25 and you have not finished problem 1. What do you do?

Say it out loud and decide quickly: "I am over time on this one. The remaining work is the edge case for an empty list; I will handle it in one line and do a short dry run, then move on." Finish the minimum that makes problem 1 correct, test the main path in a minute, and start problem 2. A clean problem 1 plus a well-planned, partly coded problem 2 usually scores better than a perfect problem 1 and nothing else.

How to answer

Show the checkpoint logic: you noticed, you named the trade-off, you chose. Interviewers grade time awareness as part of communication.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Would you rather I gave you a hint?" (yes, take it immediately and say thank you; a fast hint costs less than five slow minutes).
How do you show problem-solving signal if you cannot find the optimal solution?

Make the path visible. State the brute force and its cost, name the bottleneck ("the inner loop re-scans the window"), name the tools that remove that kind of bottleneck (hash map, sorting, two pointers, a heap) and try each aloud. Then code the best version you have and test it. A correct O(n log n) with a clear reason why O(n) may exist is real signal; silence is not.

How to answer

Brute force, bottleneck, candidate tools, chosen approach, code, test. Say each step in one sentence.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Is your solution optimal?" (give the lower bound if you know one, e.g. every element must be read so O(n) is the floor).
Should I practise company-tagged problems or general patterns?

Both, in order. Patterns first: company lists are long tails and only a fraction of any loop repeats a known problem exactly. In the last three weeks, switch mocks to the company's Tier A list in frequency order; the top of those lists is where repeats happen and where the company's style shows.

How to answer

Patterns build the skill; company lists tune it. Say how you use the frequency data, not that you memorised a list.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if you get a problem you have seen?" (say so honestly; interviewers usually switch or ask a follow-up, and honesty is itself a signal).
The interviewer finds a bug after you said you were done. How do you recover?

Thank them, reproduce it on the smallest input that shows it, find the exact line, explain the cause in one sentence, change only that line, then re-run the failing input and the main example. Do not rewrite the function. Then add the missing case to your own test list out loud ("I should have included a single-element array"). The recovery is graded; a calm, local fix limits the damage to the testing signal.

How to answer

Reproduce, locate, explain, fix one line, re-test. The same five-step routine from Testing your code.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Are there other bugs like this?" (check the symmetric boundary: if the end was wrong, look at the start).
What should I do the day before the interview?

Logistics and rest. Confirm the time zone, link, editor, camera, microphone and a backup connection. Skim your weakest 20 revision cards for half an hour, then stop. No new problems and no timed sessions: a bad result the night before costs sleep and confidence, with no time left to fix what it shows.

How to answer

Code freeze: known fixes only, then rest. On the day, one Easy warm-up out loud an hour or two before.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if I feel unprepared?" (read the trend in your mock log; one week cannot change it, but rest can protect it).
How does the bar change from SDE-2 to SDE-3 in a coding round?

The problems overlap more than people expect; the bar moves. SDE-3 candidates reach the optimal solution without hints, handle the follow-up twist (a stream, a memory cap, many queries) and drive the session. In mock terms: every signal at 3 or more, at least one 4, and problem 2 a Hard or a follow-up.

How to answer

Contrast the same problem at both levels: SDE-2 solves it; SDE-3 solves it, explains why it is optimal and adapts it when the constraint changes.

Likely follow-ups
  • "How do you practise follow-ups?" (after each mock, ask "what if the input were a stream / did not fit in memory / had a million queries?" and solve that too).
How much should I talk while coding?

Enough that the interviewer is never more than a minute behind your thinking. Before each block of code say what it does ("now the shrink step"); routine lines can be quiet; before a tricky line, say why. If you need silence, announce it ("thirty seconds to think about the boundary") and come back on time.

How to answer

Narrate intent, not keystrokes. Announce silences and keep them short.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if talking slows you down?" (practise it in every mock until it does not; it is part of the job being tested).

Approaches: brute → better → optimal

Hint 1

Draw the words as dots and connect two dots when they differ in one letter. What are you looking for in this picture?

Hint 2

You do not need the edges in advance. From a word of length L, how many candidate neighbours are there? Which data structure tells you instantly if a candidate is a real word?

Hint 3

You know both the start and the goal. What if two searches meet in the middle?

1. Brute force - build the graph by comparing all pairs, then BFS

Graph: nodes = beginWord + wordList; edge when two words differ in exactly one position (compare all pairs). Traversal: BFS from beginWord. Answer: the BFS level of endWord, counted in words.

O(N² · L)build
O(N²)edges, worst case

What is wasted: most pairs are not neighbours, yet each is compared. A word has at most 25 · L neighbours, so search for them directly. -> rung 2.

2. Better - one-directional BFS with generated neighbours

  • Graph: implicit. Neighbours of w = every word made by changing one letter of w that is in the dictionary set.
  • Traversal: BFS from beginWord, level by level; level 1 = {beginWord}.
  • Visited rule: remove a word from the set the moment it is queued (not when it is dequeued), so no word is queued twice.
  • Answer: the level at which endWord is generated; 0 if the queue empties.
q, unused, level = deque([begin]), set(words), 1
while q:
    for _ in range(len(q)):
        w = q.popleft()
        if w == end: return level
        for nb in neighbours(w):            # 25 * L candidates, kept if in unused
            unused.remove(nb); q.append(nb)
    level += 1
O(N · L² · 26)time (L to build each candidate)
O(N · L)space

What is wasted: the BFS frontier grows roughly like bd (branching b, depth d). Most of the last level is explored for nothing. -> grow from both ends.

3. Optimal - bidirectional BFS, always growing the smaller side

while front and back:
    if len(front) > len(back): front, back = back, front
    nxt = set()
    for w in front:
        for nb in candidates(w):
            if nb in back: return words + 1
            if nb in unused: unused.remove(nb); nxt.add(nb)
    front, words = nxt, words + 1
  1. Two frontiers. front = {beginWord}, back = {endWord} (only if endWord is in the list), words = 1.
  2. Grow the smaller one by one level. Swapping makes the cost depend on the cheaper side; a 1-word frontier grows far less than a 500-word one.
  3. Meeting = answer. If a candidate is already in the other frontier, the path is words + 1 words long.
  4. One shared unused set. A word claimed by either side is never claimed again, so the total work stays O(N · L · 26).
O(N · L² · 26)worst case, same bound
~bd/2words touched, typical
O(N · L)space

Why it works: after the begin side has grown i levels and the end side j levels, every word in the two frontiers is exactly i (or j) steps from its end, and words = i + j + 1. Neither search has met the other before, so no ladder shorter than i + j + 2 words exists; the first touch gives exactly that length.

Proof (optional)

Sketch. Each side is an ordinary BFS, so after the begin side has grown i levels its frontier holds exactly the words at distance i from beginWord, and every word closer than that has been claimed (by one of the two sides); the same holds for the end side with j. Let the shortest ladder have D steps. While i + j + 1 < D, no word of the begin frontier can have a neighbour in the end frontier, because that would be a ladder of i + j + 1 < D steps. When i + j + 1 = D for the first time, the side being grown expands a frontier word that lies on a shortest ladder, and its next word on that ladder is in the other frontier, so the check fires and returns D + 1 words. A word claimed earlier by the other side, but no longer in its frontier, cannot be a closer meeting point, since it would have given a shorter ladder at an earlier level. The size swap only chooses which side grows; it does not change either side's invariant.

Interview Q&A about planning your preparation

Questions people ask while following a plan, plus two that interviewers ask that the plan helps you answer.

I work full time. Is 15 hours a week realistic?

For many people it is the upper end. The plan scales by stretching, not by cutting revision: at 8-10 hours a week, keep the same per-session counts but have 4 study days instead of 6, and the plan takes about 18 weeks. What you must not do is keep 12 weeks and drop the daily review slot.

How to answer

Protect a fixed daily slot (early morning works for many) and treat the weekend timed set as non-negotiable.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Can I skip Sunday?" (Yes if you do the reviews on another day; the queue does not care which day.)
Why only about half of Tier A in 12 weeks?

Because each group's first 30-60% of problems teach the pattern and its common variants; the rest are repetitions with new stories. 236 problems with revision covers every pattern and sub-variant listed in the lessons. After week 12, continue down the Tier A list at 2-3 a day in your weakest groups, or switch to your target companies' lists.

How to answer

Measure readiness by mock scores and the number of cards at a 14-day gap or more, not by list completion.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What about Tier B?" (Use it when a group's Tier A problems run out, or as unseen material for mocks.)
When should I start Hard problems?

From week 2, about one or two a week, always in the pattern you just learned. A Hard in interviews is usually a Medium idea plus one extra twist (a second pointer, a heap on top, a second DP dimension). If you do them right after the Medium version, you see the twist clearly.

How to answer

For Google and Meta targets, add one extra Hard per week from weeks 6-11. For most other loops, the plan's Hards are enough.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if I cannot solve any Hard?" (Read the idea after 40 minutes, code it yourself, and revisit it at the 7-day rung. That is normal.)
How do I adapt the plan for a specific company?

Keep weeks 1-11 as they are; they cover what every company asks. In week 12, and in the last two weeks before any loop, open the company page, sort by frequency, and work through the most frequent problems you have not done. If a group appears often there (for example graphs or design), add 2-3 problems a week from it from week 8 onward.

How to answer

Company lists are a final filter, not a starting point; they make sense only once you know the patterns.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Parent company vs child company lists?" (Use the list of the exact company you are interviewing with.)
I am targeting SDE-3. What changes?

The coding bar is similar; the expectations around it rise. Spend less time on Easies (skip most after week 1), more on follow-ups and on explaining trade-offs. Add the data structure design problems earlier (from week 4) because senior rounds often have an "implement this class" round, and run your system design and LLD preparation in parallel from week 6.

How to answer

In mocks, ask your partner to always add a follow-up, and score yourself on how well you discuss alternatives.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Do SDE-3 rounds have harder DP?" (Sometimes at Google; more often the extra difficulty is in clean code and edge cases under time.)
My mock in week 5 went badly. Should I restart?

No. One bad mock is data. Classify the failure: did you not know the pattern, know it but not see it, or see it but code slowly or silently? Each has a different fix: the lesson, the "how to recognise" table, or more timed sets. Put the fix into week 6 as 30 minutes a day and carry on with trees.

How to answer

Write the failure type next to the mock score so you can see if it repeats in week 9.

Likely follow-ups
  • "What if week 9 fails the same way?" (Then spend week 10 on that pattern and push DP by one week.)
Interviewer: "Walk me through how you prepared for this interview."

This is often a warm-up or a "learn and be curious" style question. Answer with the system, not the count: a pattern-ordered plan, a fixed daily slot, spaced revision, and timed mocks with debriefs that changed what you studied next. One concrete example of a weakness you found and fixed is worth more than any number.

How to answer

Situation (rusty after years of systems work), action (the plan and how you adapted it after a bad mock), result (what improved, with a number from your mock scores).

Likely follow-ups
  • "What would you do differently?" (Have a real answer, such as starting mocks earlier.)
Interview question: given tasks with prerequisites and a daily capacity, build a study schedule.

This is the plan itself as a problem. Model patterns as a DAG (the dependency graph above), order them with a topological sort, and then fill days greedily with up to C tasks whose prerequisites are done. If several tasks are ready, pick by priority with a heap. That is Kahn's algorithm with a priority queue: O((V + E) log V).

How to answer

Clarify whether a prerequisite must be done on an earlier day or can be the same day; that changes whether you release new tasks immediately or at the end of each day.

Likely follow-ups
  • "Minimum number of days?" (With unlimited capacity, the longest path in the DAG; with capacity C it becomes a scheduling problem that is hard in general, so greedy by priority is the usual answer.)
  • "Detect impossible plans?" (A cycle: Kahn's queue pops fewer than V nodes.)

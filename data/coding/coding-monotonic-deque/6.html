The same trick inside a DP

Many DPs look like dp[i] = nums[i] + max(dp[i-k], ..., dp[i-1]): "the best result from the last k positions". Computing that max by a loop costs O(k) per i, so O(n k) total. The max of the last k dp values is a sliding window maximum over the dp array, so a deque makes it O(n).

LeetCode 1696, Jump Game VI, told our way: you stand on cell 0 of a row of n scored cells and must reach cell n - 1. From cell i you may jump forward 1 to k cells (not past the end). Your total is the sum of the scores of every cell you land on, including the first and last. Return the maximum total. Constraints: 1 <= n, k <= 10^5, scores between -104 and 104. Example: [1, -1, -2, 4, -7, 3], k = 2 gives 7 (cells 0, 1, 3, 5).

The loop: pop the front if it is older than i - k; dp[i] = nums[i] + dp[front]; pop back while dp[back] <= dp[i]; push i. Note the order differs from the window-max template: here we read the front before pushing i, because dp[i] needs the best of the positions before i.

LeetCode 1425 is the same recurrence with one twist: a subsequence may start anywhere, so dp[i] = nums[i] + max(0, best of last k). LeetCode 1499 rewrites yi + yj + |xi - xj| as (yj + xj) + (yi - xi) and keeps a deque of the best yi - xi among points within distance k.

From your world

This is the same shape as a token-bucket or rate monitor that answers "peak rate in the last k seconds" for every new sample: samples age out at the front, and a new high sample makes older, lower samples irrelevant. If you have ever written an interface stats collector with a moving peak, you wrote half of this already.

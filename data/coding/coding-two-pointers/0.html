The story: two friends and a bookshelf

Explain like I'm 5

The books on a shelf are sorted from thinnest to thickest. You want two books that together are exactly 15 cm thick, to fill a gap in your bag. Your friend stands at the thin end, you stand at the thick end. You each hold up one book.

Too thick together? Then your thick book is too thick even with the thinnest book still on the shelf (the one your friend holds), so you put it back and step one book inwards. Too thin? Your friend's thin book is too thin even with the thickest book still on the shelf (the one you hold), so your friend steps inwards. You never walk back. In one trip along the shelf you either find the pair or know it does not exist.

That is the two pointers pattern: two indexes (here lo and hi) that move towards each other or in the same direction. The whole skill is knowing which pointer to move and being able to say why that move cannot skip the answer. That "why" is called the invariant: a fact that stays true after every step.

From your world

You already use two indexes on one buffer every day: a ring buffer's head (producer writes) and tail (consumer reads), or a NIC descriptor ring where the driver and the hardware chase each other. Same-direction two pointers is exactly that read/write pair on a linear array. And the kernel's list_sort() in lib/list_sort.c merges two sorted runs with one cursor on each run, the "merge two" pattern on this page.

The story: cables, weak links and a postman

Explain like I'm 5

Five villages want to share one water pump. Laying pipe between two villages costs money, and some pairs are cheaper than others. You do not need a pipe between every pair, only enough so water can reach everyone. The cheapest such set of pipes is a minimum spanning tree. Now suppose one pipe breaks. If some villages lose water, that pipe was a bridge: the only link between two parts of the network. Finally, an inspector wants to walk along every pipe exactly once without walking any pipe twice. Whether he can, and in what order, is an Euler path question.

All three are about undirected graphs (Euler paths also come in a directed form), and all three have short linear or near-linear algorithms that interviewers expect you to know by name:

  • Kruskal and Prim build a minimum spanning tree (MST) in O(E log E) or O(V2).
  • Tarjan's low-link DFS finds every bridge and every articulation point (cut vertex) in one O(V + E) pass.
  • Hierholzer's algorithm builds an Euler path or circuit in O(E), plus sorting if a lexical order is required.

Where these sit in the course: this page assumes BFS/DFS from Graphs, the union-find from Union-Find, and the heap ideas from Dijkstra. It is SDE-3 / Google depth: you will meet MST questions at most companies, bridges and Euler paths mostly at Google, Amazon and Uber.

From your world

The word "bridge" will already mean something to you. An Ethernet LAN with redundant links runs the Spanning Tree Protocol (IEEE 802.1D; the Linux bridge driver implements it in net/bridge/br_stp*.c) so frames cannot loop: it elects a root bridge and blocks ports until the active links form a tree. Careful, that tree is a shortest-path tree towards the root, not a minimum spanning tree, and interviewers who know networking enjoy that distinction. A graph bridge is the opposite worry: a link with no redundancy at all, a single point of failure. Finding them is exactly what a network audit does.
